## Part A — Sentence Skills

### Exercise 1: Concision — Cut the Wordiness

Rewrite each sentence in **as few words as possible** without losing meaning.

1. *Due to the fact that the committee was unable to reach a decision, the meeting was postponed until a later date.* (21 words)
2. *It is important to note that at this point in time there are a large number of people who have the ability to work remotely.* (25 words)
3. *The company carried out an investigation into the matter and made the decision to introduce changes to its policy.* (19 words)
4. *In spite of the fact that the results were completely unanimous, the board still decided to conduct a further review of the proposal.* (23 words)
5. *The reason why the project failed is because there was a lack of communication between the various different departments.* (19 words)

### Exercise 3: Commonly Confused Words

Choose the correct word.

1. The judge must be completely **(disinterested / uninterested)** in the outcome of the case.
2. The report **(implies / infers)** that the minister knew about the problem.
3. The lawyer **(refuted / denied)** the accusation, but offered no evidence.
4. The machine requires **(continual / continuous)** power; even a second's interruption damages it.
5. Buying in bulk is more **(economic / economical)** for large families.
6. Her argument was **(credible / credulous)**, supported by extensive data.
7. The new policy will **(affect / effect)** a complete change in management culture.
8. The film's **(historic / historical)** accuracy has been questioned by experts.

## Part B — Paragraph Skills

### Exercise 6: Reframe the Question

For each question, write a **C2 thesis** that reframes the issue (expose a false dichotomy, shift the level, historicise, or name the tension). You must still **answer** the question.

1. *Should children be allowed to use smartphones?*
2. *Is space exploration a waste of money?*
3. *Should museums return artefacts to their countries of origin?*

---

### Exercise 7: Steel-man the Opposition

Write a paragraph (100–130 words) that presents the **strongest** version of a view you **disagree with**, then answers it.

Choose one:
1. *Zoos should be abolished.*
2. *Governments should not fund the arts.*
3. *Exams should be replaced entirely by continuous assessment.*

Structure: dismiss the weak version → present the strong version → acknowledge its force → answer it.

---

### Exercise 8: Control the Rhythm

Rewrite this paragraph to create **deliberate variation in sentence length**, with at least one short sentence for emphasis and a strong final sentence.

> Many cities have spent enormous amounts of money on new roads in an attempt to reduce traffic congestion, and in most cases the new roads have filled up with cars within a few years, which is a phenomenon that transport planners call induced demand, and it suggests that building more roads is not an effective long-term solution to the problem of traffic, and that cities should instead invest in public transport and cycling infrastructure.

---

### Exercise 9: Synthesise the Sources

Read the three (fictional) source summaries. Write **one** paragraph (100–140 words) that **synthesises** them into an argument. Do **not** summarise them one by one.

> **Source A (Economist):** Minimum wage increases in several regions raised incomes for low-paid workers with only small effects on overall employment.
> **Source B (Business association):** Small businesses in rural areas report that recent minimum wage increases forced them to cut staff hours.
> **Source C (Labour researcher):** The impact of minimum wage increases depends heavily on local living costs and the size of the increase relative to the median wage.

---

### Exercise 10: Edit Purple Prose

This paragraph is **over-written**. Rewrite it in clear, elegant C2 prose (about half the length).

> In the contemporary epoch of unprecedented and inexorable technological metamorphosis, it is incontrovertibly and indubitably the case that the ubiquitous and omnipresent proliferation of digital communicative apparatuses has fundamentally and irrevocably transmogrified the multifaceted tapestry of interpersonal human relationships in myriad, manifold, and multitudinous ways that are, one might perhaps venture to suggest, not entirely unproblematic.

## Part C — Extended Writing

### Exercise 11: Opening and Closing

For the essay question *"Is tourism a force for good?"*, write:
- **(a)** three different **opening sentences** using: a paradox, an anecdote, and a challenge to a common belief;
- **(b)** a **concluding paragraph** (60–80 words) that synthesises, widens the perspective, and ends memorably, **without** starting with *In conclusion*.

---

### Exercise 12: Discursive Essay

Choose **one**. Write **380–420 words**. Give your essay a **title that states or suggests your thesis**.

1. *"The more connected we become, the lonelier we are." Discuss.*
2. *"Progress is measured not by what we gain but by what we are willing to give up." Discuss.*
3. *Is it possible to be both a good citizen and a critic of one's own country?*
4. *"History is written by the victors." To what extent is this still true in the digital age?*

✔️ **Checklist:** thesis reframes but answers the question · organisational model chosen deliberately · steel-manned opposition · varied rhythm · precise connotation · no unsupported statistics · resonant conclusion · no purple prose

### Exercise 13: Opinion Column

Write a **350–400 word** column for a quality newspaper on **one** topic:

1. *The tyranny of the five-star rating*
2. *Why we should stop asking children what they want to be when they grow up*
3. *In defence of boredom*

✔️ **Checklist:** engaging title · personal voice · at least one extended metaphor · anticipate an objection · deliberate short sentences · memorable ending

## ✅ Answer Key

### Exercise 1 (model answers)
1. *Because the committee could not decide, the meeting was postponed.* (10 words)
2. *Many people can now work remotely.* (6 words)
3. *The company investigated the matter and decided to change its policy.* (11 words)
4. *Although the results were unanimous, the board decided to review the proposal further.* (13 words)
5. *The project failed because departments did not communicate.* (8 words)

**What to look for:** *due to the fact that → because · at this point in time → now · carry out an investigation → investigate · completely unanimous → unanimous · the reason why… is because → because · various different → (delete one)*

### Exercise 2 (suggested answers)
| Neutral | Positive | Negative |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| careful with money | thrifty, prudent | stingy, miserly, tight-fisted |
| determined | resolute, tenacious | stubborn, obstinate, pig-headed |
| talkative | chatty, articulate | garrulous, long-winded |
| ambitious | driven, aspiring | ruthless, overweening |
| change | reform, renewal | upheaval, disruption |
| unusual | distinctive, original | eccentric, bizarre, odd |

### Exercise 6 (model answers)
1. *The question is not whether children should use smartphones — most already do — but whether we are willing to demand that the devices be designed for children at all, rather than for the advertisers who profit from their attention.* (**shift the level** — individual choice → industry design)
2. *To call space exploration a waste of money is to assume that its value lies in the destination; historically, it has lain in the journey — in the technologies, alliances and ambitions it forced into being.* (**historicise**)
3. *At the heart of the restitution debate lies a tension between two legitimate claims — that heritage belongs to the culture that created it, and that it belongs to humanity as a whole; yet where an object was taken by force, the first claim should prevail.* (**name the tension** + answer)

### Exercise 7 — Model Answer (Topic 1)
> The strongest case for abolishing zoos is not that animals are unhappy in captivity — well-run zoos can meet many of their needs — but that the justification most zoos offer has quietly changed. Once places of entertainment, they now present themselves as arks, rescuing species from extinction. Yet only a small fraction of zoo animals belong to endangered species in genuine breeding programmes, and few captive-bred animals are ever returned to the wild. This is a serious objection, and it deserves a serious answer. That answer is not to defend the zoo as it is, but to hold it to the standard it now claims: a zoo that cannot demonstrate a conservation purpose should indeed close; one that can has earned its place.

**What to look for:** weak version dismissed → strong version stated fairly → *This is a serious objection* → answer that accepts part of the argument.

### Exercise 8 — Model Answer
> For decades, cities have tried to build their way out of congestion, spending vast sums on new and wider roads. Within a few years, most of those roads were as crowded as the ones they replaced. Transport planners have a name for this: induced demand. Build more space for cars, and more cars will come to fill it. The lesson is uncomfortable but clear. Traffic is not a problem that roads can solve. It is a problem that roads create.

**What to look for:** one long opening sentence → medium → short (*induced demand*) → aphoristic → final **antithesis** (*solve / create*).

### Exercise 9 — Model Answer
> Whether minimum wage increases cost jobs is often framed as a simple empirical dispute, but the evidence suggests that both sides are describing real effects in different places. Regional data showing substantial income gains with little loss of employment sit uneasily alongside reports from rural businesses forced to cut hours — until one considers that a wage floor set nationally lands very differently in a city and a village. As labour researchers have noted, what matters is not the increase itself but its size relative to local wages and living costs. Taken together, these accounts point less towards abandoning minimum wage increases than towards calibrating them regionally, so that a policy designed to protect the lowest-paid does not undermine the very employers on whom many of them depend.

**What to look for:** sources **woven together** (*sit uneasily alongside… until one considers… As… have noted…*); a **new** conclusion that none of the sources states alone.

### Exercise 10 — Model Answer
> Digital communication has transformed how we relate to one another, and not always for the better.

*(or, slightly longer:)* *The spread of digital devices has reshaped almost every human relationship — in ways that are not entirely benign.*

**What to look for:** removed redundant pairs (*ubiquitous and omnipresent, incontrovertibly and indubitably, myriad, manifold, and multitudinous*), removed empty phrases (*in the contemporary epoch, one might perhaps venture to suggest*), kept the one useful idea (**litotes**: *not entirely…*).

### Exercise 11 (suggested answers)
**(a) Openings**
- Paradox: *The places most loved by travellers are often the ones that travellers are most rapidly destroying.*
- Anecdote: *When I first visited the island, the only sound at night was the sea; ten years later, it was the bass line from a beach bar.*
- Challenge: *We like to believe that tourism broadens the mind. It is at least as likely to confirm what the tourist already thought.*

**(b) Conclusion**
> Tourism, then, is neither blessing nor curse; it is an amplifier, magnifying whatever values the host and the visitor bring to the encounter. Where communities control the terms, it can sustain livelihoods and cultures alike. Where they do not, it consumes the very authenticity it came to find. The question is not whether we should travel, but whether we are prepared to travel as guests rather than as customers.

### Exercise 12 — Model Answer (Question 1)
> ### Connected, Not Lonely — Just Differently Alone
>
> **¶1** The claim that connection breeds loneliness has the appeal of a paradox and the convenience of a slogan. It flatters our suspicion of technology and explains a real unease in a single sentence. But slogans are rarely precise, and this one conflates two different things: the number of our connections and the quality of our attention.
>
> **¶2** The case for the proposition is not trivial. Surveys in many countries report rising levels of loneliness, particularly among the young — the very group most immersed in digital life. Social platforms, moreover, are designed to maximise time spent rather than satisfaction gained, and a feed of other people's curated highlights is a reliable way to feel that one's own life is lacking. If loneliness is the gap between the relationships we have and the ones we want, social media seems engineered to widen it.
>
> **¶3** Yet correlation is doing a great deal of work here. Loneliness has many drivers — longer working hours, the decline of local institutions, more people living alone — and most of them predate the smartphone. For a teenager in a remote town, or a migrant thousands of miles from home, digital connection is often the only thing standing between them and genuine isolation. To call their connectedness the cause of their loneliness is to mistake the lifeboat for the shipwreck.
>
> **¶4** The more accurate claim is narrower and more useful: it is not connection that isolates us, but a particular kind of connection — passive, comparative, and endless. Messaging a friend is not the same act as watching strangers perform their lives. The former sustains relationships; the latter substitutes spectatorship for them. The problem, in other words, is not that we are too connected, but that much of what we call connection is merely proximity to other people's content.
>
> **¶5** We are not lonelier because we are connected. We are lonely, when we are, because we have allowed tools built to hold our attention to stand in for the slower, less efficient work of knowing one another. That work has never been easy. It has simply become easier to avoid.
>
> *(≈ 400 words)*

**Techniques:** title states thesis · ¶1 challenges the slogan · ¶2 **steel-mans** · ¶3 metaphor (*lifeboat / shipwreck*) · ¶4 precise reframing + antithesis · ¶5 short sentences, resonant close.

### Exercise 13 — What to Look For
- A title with **attitude** (*The tyranny of…, In defence of…*)
- A **personal hook** in the first two sentences
- One **extended metaphor** carried across at least two paragraphs
- An explicit **concession** (*None of this is to say…*)
- A **call to action** or striking final line
- **No invented statistics** — use general, verifiable claims or personal observation

See the model column *"In Praise of Wasted Time"* in [C2 Examples](../examples/c2_examples.md#opinion-column--the-cult-of-productivity).

### Exercise 14 (model answers)
**(a) Formal notice**
> Following a comprehensive review of service provision, the Council has regretfully concluded that the Central Library cannot continue to operate within the current budget. The library will therefore close on 31 March. Residents may continue to access the Council's digital lending service, and mobile library visits will be arranged for outlying areas. The Council wishes to thank all staff and users for their long-standing support.

**(b) Ironic columnist**
> The Council, having conducted what it assures us was a "comprehensive review", has discovered that the cheapest library is one that does not exist. It is a bold insight. Residents who wish to read may now do so digitally, provided they own a device, a data plan, and the patience to navigate the Council's website — which, in fairness, has always been an education in itself.

**(c) Campaign group**
> Our library is not a building. It is where children learn to read, where job-seekers write their first CV, where older residents find warmth and company. Close it, and we do not save money; we simply move the cost elsewhere — to schools, to health services, to lonely homes. We have one library. Once it is gone, it will not come back. Join us on Saturday and tell the Council: not this, not here, not now.

**What to look for:** (a) passive, nominalised, impersonal · (b) mock-formality, understatement, irony · (c) anaphora, tricolon, imperatives, inclusive *we/our*.
