## 41. Salary or Job Satisfaction

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Work

> **Question:** Some people think that a high salary is the most important factor when choosing a job, while others believe that job satisfaction matters more. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** financial security · job satisfaction · career progression · work-life balance · burnout · sense of purpose

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: money matters most until people have enough; after that, satisfaction matters more.*

**🟡 B1:** Money is important, but after we have enough money, more money does not make us much happier. A person needs money for food, a home and their family. But if a person has a high salary and hates their job, they will feel unhappy every day. For example, my uncle left a well-paid job in a bank to become a teacher, and now he is much happier, even though he earns less.

**🔴 B2:** Although money is essential, its importance seems to decrease once a person's basic needs are met. Research on happiness suggests that higher income improves wellbeing significantly for people on low incomes, but the effect becomes smaller as income rises. After that point, factors such as interesting work, supportive colleagues and a sense of purpose tend to have a greater influence on how satisfied people feel. Therefore, for those who already earn enough to live comfortably, job satisfaction is likely to matter more than a further increase in salary.

**🟣 C1:** The relationship between income and wellbeing is real but subject to diminishing returns. For those struggling to meet basic needs, an increase in salary can be transformative, reducing anxiety and expanding opportunities. Beyond a certain level of financial security, however, additional income appears to contribute progressively less to life satisfaction, while intrinsic rewards — autonomy, mastery and a sense of meaning — become increasingly important. The question of salary versus satisfaction, then, cannot be answered in the abstract; it depends largely on where an individual stands on this curve.

**⚫ C2:** Money is a ladder out of hardship, and while one is still climbing, every rung matters. Once one has reached solid ground, however, adding more rungs does little but improve the view. What shapes daily life at that point is the work itself: whether it absorbs or deadens, whether it is respected, whether one leaves it at the end of the day feeling used or useful. Salary answers the question of how a person lives. Satisfaction answers the harder question of what their days are for.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 272 words

When people choose a job, some think that a high salary is the most important thing. Others believe that enjoying the job is more important. In this essay, I will discuss both views and give my opinion.

On the one hand, many people choose a job because of the salary. Money is necessary to pay for food, rent, school fees and healthcare. With a good salary, people can take care of their families and save money for the future. For example, in my country, many young people want to work for international companies or banks because these jobs pay well. Also, a high salary can make people feel successful and respected.

On the other hand, other people think that job satisfaction is more important. People spend about eight hours a day at work, so if they do not like their job, they will feel bored or stressed every day. People who enjoy their work are usually happier and healthier, and they often work harder. For example, my uncle left a well-paid job in a bank to become a teacher. He earns less money now, but he is much happier.

In my opinion, both are important, but job satisfaction is more important after people have enough money. If a person is poor, a higher salary can change their life. However, when people earn enough for a comfortable life, they should choose a job that they enjoy.

In conclusion, some people choose a job for money, and others choose it for satisfaction. I believe that people need a fair salary, but a job that makes them happy is more important in the long term.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 295 words

Choosing a career is one of the most important decisions people make, and opinions differ on what should matter most. While some believe that a high salary should be the main priority, others argue that job satisfaction is more important. In my view, a reasonable salary is essential, but beyond that point, satisfaction becomes the more important factor.

Those who prioritise salary have strong arguments. A well-paid job provides financial security, allowing people to support their families, buy a home and save for retirement. In countries with limited social welfare, a high income may also be the only protection against unexpected costs, such as medical bills. Furthermore, salary is often seen as a measure of success and status, and a high income can open doors to a more comfortable lifestyle.

On the other hand, supporters of job satisfaction point out that people spend a large part of their lives at work. A job that is boring, stressful or meaningless can lead to unhappiness and even health problems, no matter how well it is paid. In contrast, people who find their work interesting and valuable tend to be more motivated and productive, and they are less likely to suffer from burnout. Many people also value other aspects of a job, such as flexible hours, supportive colleagues and opportunities to learn.

In my opinion, the importance of salary depends on a person's situation. For someone struggling to pay their bills, a higher salary can make an enormous difference. However, once people earn enough to live comfortably, extra money tends to bring less additional happiness, while satisfaction continues to affect their daily lives.

In conclusion, both salary and satisfaction are important, but I believe that, once basic financial needs are met, job satisfaction should be the main priority.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 286 words

The choice between a lucrative career and a fulfilling one is often presented as a straightforward dilemma between material security and personal happiness. In reality, the two are interdependent, and the relative importance of each depends heavily on individual circumstances. I would argue that financial security should be the priority until a reasonable standard of living is secured, after which job satisfaction should take precedence.

The case for prioritising salary is more compelling than it is sometimes given credit for. Income determines access to housing, healthcare and education, and in societies with weak social safety nets, it may be the only buffer against misfortune. For young people from low-income families, a well-paid job can also transform the prospects of an entire household. To dismiss financial considerations as materialistic is, in such contexts, a privilege available mainly to those who have never lacked money.

Nevertheless, research on wellbeing consistently suggests that the benefits of additional income diminish as income rises. Beyond a certain threshold, factors such as autonomy, a sense of competence and the belief that one's work is meaningful exert a stronger influence on life satisfaction. Jobs lacking these qualities can erode wellbeing regardless of pay, contributing to stress and burnout. Moreover, satisfied employees tend to perform better, which may ultimately enhance their long-term earnings.

The optimal choice, therefore, is not fixed but depends on where an individual stands. Someone struggling financially is entirely rational to prioritise income, whereas a person who is already financially secure gains more from pursuing satisfying work.

In conclusion, salary and satisfaction are not truly opposed. Money matters most when it is scarce; meaning matters most when money is sufficient. The wisest career decisions recognise which of these conditions currently applies.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 277 words

The debate between money and meaning is usually conducted by people who already have enough money. That is worth remembering, because it explains why the argument for job satisfaction often sounds so easy — and why, for millions of people, it is a luxury they cannot afford.

For a young graduate supporting parents, siblings and perhaps a loan, a higher salary is not greed but responsibility. It pays for a sister's schooling, a father's medicine, a roof that does not leak. To lecture such a person about following their passion is to mistake a privilege for a principle. Financial security is not the opposite of a meaningful life; it is often its precondition.

Yet the value of money is not constant. The first salary that lifts a family out of hardship changes everything; each subsequent increase changes a little less. Research on wellbeing repeatedly finds that beyond a certain point, extra income adds surprisingly little to happiness, while the character of one's daily work — whether it is interesting, respected and done among people one likes — continues to shape almost every waking hour. A well-paid job that one dreads is a comfortable prison, and many who enter it discover that the salary is spent partly on consoling themselves for the job.

The sensible answer, then, is sequential rather than absolute. Secure enough income to live with dignity and without fear; after that, let satisfaction lead. The mistake is not choosing money, or choosing meaning. It is failing to notice the moment at which one has enough of the first to afford the second.

Salary buys the conditions of a good life. Satisfaction is much closer to the thing itself.

## 42. Changing Jobs More Often

**Type:** Two-part question · **Theme:** Work

> **Question:** In the past, many people stayed with the same employer for most of their working lives. Today, people change jobs much more often. Why is this happening? Is it a positive or negative development?

**Useful vocabulary:** job security · career development · loyalty · short-term contracts · transferable skills · job-hopping

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: changing jobs helps people learn new skills and earn more.*

**🟡 B1:** Changing jobs can help people learn new skills. In a new company, workers must use new software, meet new people and solve new problems. This makes them more experienced. It can also help them to get a higher salary. For example, my cousin changed jobs three times in six years, and now she is a manager with a good salary.

**🔴 B2:** One advantage of changing jobs regularly is that it allows people to develop a wider range of skills and experience. Each new role exposes workers to different technologies, working methods and colleagues, making them more adaptable and valuable in the job market. Moreover, moving to a new employer is often the quickest way to obtain a significant salary increase, as companies competing for talent may offer better pay than current employers are willing to provide. As a result, many ambitious professionals see frequent job changes as a normal part of career progression.

**🟣 C1:** In a labour market where technologies and industries evolve rapidly, mobility can be a strategic asset. Each move exposes workers to new tools, practices and networks, cultivating the transferable skills and adaptability that employers increasingly prize. There is also a financial logic: external offers frequently exceed internal pay rises, so changing employer is often the most effective route to higher earnings. What was once interpreted as disloyalty is therefore increasingly understood as a rational response to an economy in which no single employer can guarantee lifelong security or development.

**⚫ C2:** A career spent in one organisation can produce deep expertise, but it can also produce a curious fragility: skills so specific to one company that they lose their value the day that company changes its mind. Moving between employers is a form of insurance against that risk. Each new role adds tools, contacts and ways of thinking, so that the worker's value lies increasingly in themselves rather than in any single employer's regard for them. In an unstable economy, that is not disloyalty. It is prudence.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 270 words

In the past, many people worked for one company for their whole life. Today, people change jobs much more often. There are several reasons for this, and I think it is mostly a positive development.

There are three main reasons for this change. Firstly, people want a higher salary. Often, the easiest way to get more money is to move to a new company. Secondly, people want to learn new things and have new experiences. When they feel bored in their job, they look for a new challenge. Thirdly, companies do not promise jobs for life any more. Many companies use short contracts, so workers must look for new jobs.

I think this change has many good points. When people change jobs, they learn new skills and meet new people. This makes them more experienced and confident. For example, my cousin changed jobs three times in six years, and now she is a manager with a good salary. Also, people can find a job that they really enjoy.

However, there are also some bad points. Changing jobs often can be stressful, because people must learn new rules and make new friends every time. Some companies do not want to hire people who change jobs very often, because they think these workers will leave soon. In addition, people with short contracts may not have job security, so they worry about money.

In conclusion, people change jobs more often today because they want higher salaries and new experiences, and because companies do not offer jobs for life. I believe this is mostly positive, because people can learn more and find better jobs.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 291 words

Unlike previous generations, who often stayed with the same employer for decades, today's workers tend to change jobs frequently. This essay will examine the reasons for this trend and argue that, on balance, it is a positive development, although it also brings some risks.

Several factors explain this change. The first is economic: employers no longer offer the lifelong job security and generous pensions that once encouraged loyalty. Many companies now rely on short-term contracts and restructure regularly, so workers feel less obligation to stay. Secondly, changing employer is often the quickest way to achieve a substantial pay rise or promotion. Thirdly, younger generations tend to value personal development, meaningful work and flexibility, and they are more willing to leave a job that does not meet these expectations. Finally, technology has made it much easier to search and apply for new positions.

This trend has clear benefits. Workers who move between organisations gain a broad range of skills and experience, making them more adaptable in a rapidly changing economy. They are also more likely to find a job that suits their abilities and interests, which can improve both productivity and job satisfaction. Furthermore, the movement of workers spreads ideas and knowledge between companies.

However, there are also disadvantages. Frequent job changes can be stressful and may prevent people from developing deep expertise in one area. Employers may be reluctant to invest in training staff who are likely to leave, and some workers, particularly those on temporary contracts, suffer from insecurity and lack benefits such as pensions.

In conclusion, people change jobs more frequently because of weaker job security, better opportunities elsewhere and changing values. Overall, I believe this is a positive development, provided that workers on short-term contracts receive adequate protection.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 302 words

The idea of a "job for life" has become something of an anachronism. Where earlier generations often spent their entire careers with a single organisation, today's workers typically move between several employers. I would argue that this shift reflects fundamental changes in the employment relationship and is, on the whole, positive — although its benefits are unevenly distributed.

The causes lie on both sides of that relationship. Employers, facing intense competition and rapid technological change, have increasingly abandoned the implicit promise of long-term security, relying instead on restructuring, outsourcing and fixed-term contracts. Having withdrawn their loyalty, they can hardly expect it in return. Workers, meanwhile, have discovered that moving employer is frequently the most effective way to secure higher pay and faster advancement, since external offers often exceed internal promotions. Changing values also play a role: many younger professionals prioritise learning, flexibility and purpose, and are prepared to move in pursuit of them.

For skilled workers, the consequences are largely favourable. Exposure to different organisations builds adaptable, transferable skills and wider professional networks, both of which are valuable in an economy where entire industries can be transformed within a decade. The circulation of workers also benefits the wider economy by diffusing knowledge and practices between firms.

However, the same trend looks very different at the lower end of the labour market. For workers in precarious employment, frequent job changes are less a choice than a necessity, bringing insecurity, gaps in income and limited access to benefits such as pensions. Employers' reluctance to train staff they expect to lose may also leave such workers with few opportunities to develop.

In conclusion, greater job mobility is broadly positive for those with marketable skills, but potentially harmful for those without them. The policy challenge is to ensure that flexibility does not become a euphemism for insecurity.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 283 words

The gold watch presented after forty years of service now belongs in a museum of working life, alongside the typewriter and the clocking-in card. Its disappearance is often lamented as the end of loyalty. It would be more accurate to describe it as the end of a bargain — and to ask who ended it.

For much of the twentieth century, large employers offered security in exchange for commitment: stay with us, and we will promote you, train you and pay your pension. That arrangement has largely collapsed, and it was usually companies that collapsed it, through restructuring, outsourcing and the spread of short-term contracts. Workers have simply drawn the logical conclusion. If the firm will not stay loyal to them, they need not stay loyal to the firm — particularly when moving to a competitor is so often the fastest route to a better salary.

For those with skills in demand, the new mobility is largely liberating. Each move adds experience, contacts and adaptability, qualities that matter more in an economy where entire professions can be reshaped within a decade. Ideas travel with the people who carry them, and industries become more inventive as a result.

But mobility has two faces. For the software engineer choosing between offers, it means freedom. For the warehouse worker moving between temporary contracts, it means insecurity: irregular income, no pension, and little chance of the training that might lead to something better. The same statistics describe both, which is why averages flatter the trend.

Whether frequent job changes are positive, then, depends on whether they are chosen or imposed. A society that celebrates flexibility should also guarantee that flexibility does not become merely a polite word for precariousness.

## 43. Starting Your Own Business

**Type:** Advantages and disadvantages · **Theme:** Business

> **Question:** More and more young people want to start their own business rather than work for a company. What are the advantages and disadvantages of this?

**Useful vocabulary:** entrepreneur · start-up · financial risk · independence · regular income · innovation

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: starting a business is risky, because many new businesses fail.*

**🟡 B1:** Starting a business is risky. Many new businesses close in the first few years because they do not have enough customers or money. When a business fails, the owner can lose all their savings or have big debts. For example, my friend opened a coffee shop, but after one year he had to close it, and he still has to pay back his loan. So young people should plan carefully before they start.

**🔴 B2:** The most significant disadvantage of starting a business is financial risk. A large proportion of new businesses fail within their first few years, often because of insufficient demand, poor planning or cash-flow problems. Unlike employees, who receive a regular salary, business owners may earn little or nothing for long periods and may lose their savings or be left with substantial debts if the business fails. For young people without financial support from their families, such losses can be particularly difficult to recover from.

**🟣 C1:** The romance of entrepreneurship tends to obscure its statistics. A substantial proportion of new ventures close within their first few years, and failure frequently carries personal as well as commercial consequences, since young founders often finance their businesses through savings, family loans or credit. Unlike salaried employees, entrepreneurs bear the full risk of fluctuating demand and irregular income. This does not mean that young people should avoid starting businesses, but it does suggest that the decision should be informed by realistic expectations and, ideally, by a safety net that makes failure survivable rather than ruinous.

**⚫ C2:** The stories we tell about entrepreneurs are written almost entirely by survivors. We hear of the student who founded a company in a bedroom and sold it for millions, not of the thousands whose ventures quietly closed, taking their savings and their parents' loans with them. Starting a business can be exhilarating and occasionally transformative. It is also, statistically, more likely to end in closure than in fortune — a fact that aspiring founders deserve to hear before, rather than after, they sign the lease.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 263 words

Nowadays, many young people want to start their own business instead of working for a company. For example, they open cafés, online shops or small technology companies. This has some advantages and some disadvantages.

There are several advantages. Firstly, business owners are their own boss. They can make their own decisions and choose their working hours. Secondly, if the business is successful, they can earn much more money than a normal worker. Thirdly, they can do work that they really love. For example, a person who loves cooking can open a restaurant. In addition, new businesses create jobs for other people and help the economy of the country.

However, there are also some disadvantages. The biggest problem is risk. Many new businesses fail in the first few years, and the owner can lose all their money. For example, my friend opened a coffee shop with a bank loan, but after one year he had to close it, and he still has to pay back the money. Secondly, business owners often work very long hours, even at weekends, so they have little free time. Thirdly, they do not have a regular salary, so they may worry about money every month.

In my opinion, starting a business can be a great experience for young people, but they must prepare well. It is a good idea to work for a company first to get experience and save some money.

In conclusion, starting a business can give young people freedom, money and satisfaction. However, it is risky and stressful, so they should plan carefully before they start.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 292 words

In recent years, entrepreneurship has become an increasingly attractive career option for young people, many of whom dream of running their own business rather than working for an employer. This essay will discuss the main advantages and disadvantages of this trend.

The most obvious advantage is independence. Entrepreneurs can make their own decisions, choose the direction of their business and often set their own working hours. For many young people, this freedom is more appealing than following the instructions of a manager. In addition, running a successful business can be financially rewarding, since the profits belong to the owner. Starting a business also allows people to pursue their own ideas and passions, which can lead to greater job satisfaction. From a wider perspective, new businesses create jobs and introduce innovative products and services, which benefits the economy as a whole.

However, there are also significant disadvantages. The main one is financial risk: many new businesses fail within their first few years, and their owners may lose their savings or be left with large debts. Unlike employees, entrepreneurs do not receive a regular salary, paid holidays or a pension, so their income can be unpredictable. Running a business also tends to involve long working hours and considerable stress, especially in the early stages. Young entrepreneurs may also lack the experience, contacts and management skills that are needed to succeed.

To reduce these risks, it may be wise for young people to gain some work experience before starting their own company, and governments could support new entrepreneurs through training programmes and low-interest loans.

In conclusion, starting a business offers independence, potential financial rewards and personal fulfilment, but it also carries serious financial risks and pressures. With careful preparation, however, the advantages can be considerable.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 299 words

Entrepreneurship has acquired an almost heroic image in recent years, fuelled by the success stories of technology founders and the promise of independence that a conventional career seems unable to offer. It is therefore unsurprising that many young people aspire to start their own businesses. The trend brings genuine benefits, both individual and collective, but it also carries risks that are frequently underestimated.

For individuals, the principal attraction is autonomy. Running one's own business offers control over the nature, pace and direction of one's work — a freedom that many young people value highly, particularly in an employment market characterised by insecurity and rigid hierarchies. Successful entrepreneurs may also accumulate considerably greater wealth than salaried employees, and the experience of building an enterprise can develop an unusually broad range of skills, from finance to marketing to leadership. At the level of society, new firms are a vital source of innovation and employment, challenging established companies and introducing new products and services.

The disadvantages, however, are substantial. A large proportion of new businesses close within a few years, and the financial consequences of failure can be severe for young founders who have invested their savings or borrowed heavily. The absence of a regular salary, paid leave and pension contributions exposes entrepreneurs to considerable insecurity, while the demands of running a business frequently blur the boundaries between work and personal life. Inexperience compounds these risks, since many young founders lack the industry knowledge and professional networks that improve a venture's chances of survival.

In short, entrepreneurship offers young people freedom and the possibility of exceptional rewards, while simultaneously exposing them to considerable risk. Policies that make failure less catastrophic — through accessible training, mentoring and affordable credit — would allow more young people to benefit from the advantages without being ruined by the disadvantages.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 312 words

Every generation invents its own model of success. For many of today's young people, it is no longer the lawyer in a corner office or the civil servant with a secure pension, but the founder — the person who answers to no one and builds something from nothing. The dream has real attractions, but it is worth examining both what it offers and what it quietly omits.

What it offers, above all, is ownership: of one's time, one's decisions and one's ideas. For a generation that has watched employers treat loyalty as expendable, working for oneself can feel less like a gamble than like the only honest arrangement available. Success brings rewards that a salary never could, and even failure can teach more about finance, persuasion and resilience than years in a comfortable job. Economies benefit too, since new firms are where much innovation begins and many new jobs are created.

What the dream omits is the arithmetic. A large share of new businesses close within a few years, and the founder, unlike the employee, absorbs the loss personally — in vanished savings, unpaid loans and sometimes damaged family finances. There is no salary in a bad month, no paid holiday in a busy one, and no pension waiting at the end. The freedom to set one's own hours often turns out to mean the freedom to work all of them.

None of this argues against entrepreneurship. It argues for honesty about it. Young people who gain experience before starting, test their ideas cheaply and keep a financial cushion are far more likely to survive, and governments that provide mentoring and affordable credit make the risk less ruinous.

The advantages of starting a business are real, and so are the dangers. The founder's life is not a shortcut to freedom; it is a harder road to it, and worth taking only with open eyes.

## 44. Raising the Retirement Age

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Work and economy

> **Question:** Because people are living longer, some people think that the retirement age should be raised. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** life expectancy · pension system · ageing population · physically demanding jobs · flexible retirement · younger generations

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: pensions become too expensive when people live longer and there are fewer young workers.*

**🟡 B1:** If people live longer but still stop working at sixty, the government must pay pensions for many more years. At the same time, there are fewer young workers who pay taxes. This makes the pension system very expensive. For example, in some countries, people now live for twenty or thirty years after they retire. So working a few more years can help the country to pay for pensions.

**🔴 B2:** The main argument for raising the retirement age is financial. As life expectancy increases and birth rates fall, the number of retirees is growing while the number of working-age people paying taxes is shrinking. This means that pension systems face growing costs with a relatively smaller base of contributors. If the retirement age remains unchanged, governments may be forced to raise taxes significantly or reduce pension payments. A gradual increase in the retirement age would help to keep pension systems sustainable for future generations.

**🟣 C1:** The fiscal logic behind raising the retirement age is difficult to dispute. Pension systems in most countries were designed when retirement typically lasted a decade or so; with rising life expectancy and falling birth rates, it now often extends much longer, supported by a shrinking proportion of working-age taxpayers. Without adjustment, the burden falls on younger generations through higher taxes or on retirees through reduced benefits. Linking the retirement age to life expectancy, as several European countries have done, offers a transparent way of sharing the cost of longer lives between those who enjoy them and those who pay for them.

**⚫ C2:** Pension systems rest on an arithmetic that has quietly changed beneath them. They were designed for a world in which workers were many and retirements were short; they now operate in one where retirements can last a quarter of a century and each new generation of workers is smaller than the last. One can argue about the remedy, but not about the sum. Either people work somewhat longer, or the young pay more, or the old receive less. Refusing to choose is itself a choice — usually the worst one.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 274 words

Today, people live much longer than in the past because of better healthcare and food. Some people think that because of this, people should work until an older age before they retire. I partly agree with this idea.

On the one hand, there are good reasons to raise the retirement age. Firstly, the government must pay pensions to old people, and if people live longer, the government must pay for more years. At the same time, there are fewer young people who work and pay taxes. If nothing changes, the country may not have enough money for pensions. Secondly, many people are still healthy and active at sixty or sixty-five. They have a lot of experience and knowledge, so they can continue to help their companies. Some old people also enjoy working because it keeps them busy.

On the other hand, raising the retirement age is not fair for everyone. Some jobs are very hard, such as building work or farming. People who do these jobs are often very tired and have health problems when they are old, so they cannot work longer. Also, if old people do not retire, there may be fewer jobs for young people.

In my opinion, the government can raise the retirement age a little and slowly, but it should be flexible. People who do office work can work longer if they are healthy, but people who do heavy work should be able to retire earlier.

In conclusion, I partly agree that the retirement age should be raised because people live longer and pensions are expensive. However, the rules should be fair for workers who do hard physical jobs.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 306 words

As a result of improvements in healthcare and living standards, life expectancy has increased significantly in many countries. This has led some governments to argue that the retirement age should be raised. I partly agree with this view, but I believe any increase must be introduced gradually and with flexibility for different types of workers.

The strongest argument for raising the retirement age is the financial pressure on pension systems. Because people are living longer and birth rates are falling, the number of retirees is growing, while the number of working-age taxpayers is shrinking. If the retirement age is not adjusted, governments will face a difficult choice between raising taxes on younger workers and reducing pensions. In addition, many people in their sixties are healthy, experienced and productive, and some would prefer to continue working, both for financial reasons and to remain active and socially connected.

However, a single higher retirement age for everyone would be unfair. Workers in physically demanding jobs, such as construction, farming or factory work, often experience health problems much earlier than office workers. Moreover, life expectancy is not equal across society: poorer people tend to live shorter lives, so raising the retirement age would reduce the number of years they spend in retirement more than it would for wealthier people. There are also concerns that older workers remaining in their jobs could reduce opportunities for young people, although economists debate whether this effect is significant.

For these reasons, I believe the retirement age should be raised gradually, with options for flexible or partial retirement and earlier pensions for those who have done heavy physical work for many years.

Thirdly, some factories cause pollution, for example by putting chemicals into rivers. Finally, local businesses may not be able to compete with big international companies, so some of them close.

In my opinion, international companies can help developing countries, but the government must make good rules. It should protect workers, control pollution and make sure that companies pay fair taxes.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 296 words

Over recent decades, a growing number of multinational corporations have established factories and offices in developing countries, attracted by lower labour costs and expanding markets. This essay will discuss the main advantages and disadvantages of this trend for the host countries.

The most significant advantage is employment. Multinational companies often create thousands of jobs, providing regular incomes in countries where formal employment opportunities are limited. In Cambodia, for example, the garment industry, which largely produces clothing for international brands, has become one of the country's largest employers. Furthermore, foreign companies can bring new technology, management techniques and training, allowing local workers to develop valuable skills. Their presence can also stimulate the growth of local suppliers and service providers, while governments benefit from increased tax revenue and export earnings.

However, there are also considerable disadvantages. Some multinational companies take advantage of weak labour laws to pay low wages and impose long working hours, and poor safety standards have occasionally led to tragic accidents, such as the collapse of the Rana Plaza factory building in Bangladesh in 2013. In addition, a large share of the profits generated is often transferred back to the company's home country rather than reinvested locally. Environmental damage is another concern, as some factories pollute rivers and air in countries where environmental regulations are poorly enforced. Finally, local businesses may struggle to compete with large, well-funded foreign firms.

To maximise the benefits, governments of developing countries should enforce labour and environmental laws, encourage companies to train local staff and buy materials locally, and ensure that fair taxes are paid.

In conclusion, multinational companies can bring jobs, skills and investment to developing countries, but they may also exploit workers and damage the environment. Effective regulation is essential if the advantages are to outweigh the disadvantages.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 276 words

The arrival of multinational corporations in developing countries has been one of the most significant economic phenomena of the globalisation era. For host countries, foreign investment offers substantial opportunities, but it also carries risks, and the balance between them depends largely on the policies of host governments.

The advantages are considerable. Multinational firms can create employment on a scale that domestic industry cannot match, drawing workers from subsistence agriculture into waged work. Cambodia's export-oriented garment industry, for instance, has employed hundreds of thousands of people, predominantly young women, whose earnings support rural households. Foreign investment can also serve as a channel for technology transfer, as local employees acquire technical and managerial expertise and domestic suppliers learn to meet international standards. Over time, such spillovers can help a country to diversify its economy, as South Korea and Taiwan did in earlier decades by building domestic industries alongside foreign investment.

The disadvantages, however, are equally significant. Competition to attract investment can lead governments to tolerate low wages, excessive working hours and inadequate safety standards, sometimes with tragic consequences, as the collapse of Bangladesh's Rana Plaza building in 2013 demonstrated. A substantial proportion of profits may be repatriated rather than reinvested, and aggressive tax planning can deprive host governments of revenue. Environmental regulations, where weakly enforced, may be circumvented, while dependence on a single export industry leaves economies vulnerable to shifts in global demand.

In short, multinational investment is neither inherently beneficial nor inherently exploitative. Its effects depend on whether host governments enforce labour and environmental standards, secure fair taxation and use foreign investment as a platform for developing domestic capabilities rather than as a permanent substitute for them.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 314 words

Few economic arguments generate more heat than the role of multinational companies in poorer countries. To their critics, they are exploiters who chase the cheapest labour and the weakest regulations; to their defenders, they are engines of development. Both descriptions contain truth, which is why the useful question is not whether multinationals help or harm, but under what conditions they do each.

Consider the case for. A factory owned by a foreign company can offer a young worker from the countryside something the village cannot: a wage paid on time, every month. In Cambodia, the garment industry that sews clothes for international brands has employed hundreds of thousands of people, most of them young women, whose earnings keep siblings in school and parents fed. Foreign firms also bring techniques, machinery and access to global markets that local businesses could take years to develop. South Korea and Taiwan used foreign investment as a ladder towards industries of their own.

Now consider the case against. Countries competing for investment are tempted to compete by lowering standards, and the results can be lethal, as the collapse of Bangladesh's Rana Plaza building in 2013 showed. Profits often flow back to headquarters abroad; clever accounting can shrink local tax bills to almost nothing; rivers downstream of factories can pay an environmental price that never appears in the accounts. And an economy built on a single export industry can be shaken badly when fashion, or foreign demand, moves elsewhere.

What separates the two outcomes is not the investors but the host. Governments that enforce safety and labour laws, collect fair taxes and insist on training and local suppliers turn foreign capital into domestic capacity. Governments that do not simply rent out their workers.

Multinationals, then, are neither saviours nor villains. They are powerful tools, and like all powerful tools, they are only as beneficial as the hands that direct them.

## 49. Unpaid Internships

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Work

> **Question:** Some people believe that unpaid internships are a valuable way for young people to gain work experience. Others think they are unfair and should not be allowed. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** internship · work experience · exploitation · equal opportunity · minimum wage · professional network

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: unpaid internships are open mainly to young people from rich families.*

**🟡 B1:** Unpaid internships are not fair for young people from poor families. To work for free for three or six months, you need money for food, rent and transport. Rich students can get money from their parents, but poor students must work in a paid job instead. So rich students get the experience and the good jobs later, and poor students do not. For example, my friend could not accept an internship in the city because she could not pay the rent.

**🔴 B2:** The most serious problem with unpaid internships is that they are accessible mainly to young people from wealthy families. Working without pay for several months, often in expensive cities, is only possible for those whose parents can cover their living costs. Talented students from low-income backgrounds, who cannot afford to work for free, are therefore excluded from valuable experience and professional connections. Over time, this means that competitive industries such as media, law and politics become dominated by people from privileged backgrounds, which reduces diversity and social mobility.

**🟣 C1:** The fundamental objection to unpaid internships is that they convert economic privilege into professional advantage. Only those who can afford to work without income — typically because their families can support them — are able to accept such positions, which frequently serve as the gateway to competitive careers in fields such as media, fashion, law and politics. Talented applicants from less affluent backgrounds are effectively excluded, not by lack of ability but by lack of means. The result is a subtle but powerful mechanism by which advantage reproduces itself, narrowing the social composition of influential professions.

**⚫ C2:** An unpaid internship is advertised as an opportunity open to all. In practice, it carries an invisible entry fee: the cost of living for months without a wage, usually in one of the most expensive cities in the country. Those who can pay it — with a parent's help — collect experience, contacts and the line on a CV that opens the next door. Those who cannot are not rejected; they simply never apply. The system excludes without ever appearing to discriminate, which is precisely what makes it so durable.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 309 words

Internships have become a near-essential stage in the transition from education to employment, particularly in competitive fields. The question of whether they should be paid is therefore not a minor administrative detail but a matter of fairness and social mobility. I would argue that, while internships themselves are valuable, unpaid positions should be severely restricted.

Advocates of unpaid internships emphasise their educational value. Interns gain practical experience, professional contacts and insight into an industry, all of which can significantly improve their employment prospects. Some employers, particularly small organisations and charities, maintain that they could not offer such opportunities at all if they were required to pay, and that interns receive training and mentoring in exchange for their time.

In conclusion, the value of internships is real, but when they are unpaid, access to that value is rationed by wealth. Paying interns is therefore not merely a question of fairness to individuals but of ensuring that entry to important professions depends on ability rather than family income.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 315 words

The defence of unpaid internships deserves to be heard. Small charities and start-ups argue, not unreasonably, that if they had to pay interns they would simply stop offering the opportunity, and some experience is better than none.

But consider who can accept such an offer. A graduate asked to work for six months without pay in an expensive capital must have somewhere to live and someone to feed them. For the child of comfortable parents, this is a minor inconvenience; for the child of a factory worker, it is impossible. The internship does not need to reject the poorer applicant; circumstance has already done so. Since internships increasingly serve as the doorway to careers in journalism, law, finance and government, this silent filter shapes who will run the institutions that govern everyone else.

There is also the matter of honesty. Many "interns" are not learning so much as working — answering phones, writing reports, managing social media — tasks for which organisations would otherwise have to pay. Calling it training does not change what it is.

The answer is not to abolish internships but to pay for work. Brief, course-linked placements focused on learning can remain unpaid; anything that looks like a job should be paid like one. A profession that cannot afford to pay its newest members has a business problem. It should not solve it by selecting them according to their parents' wealth.

## 50. Hard Work or Luck

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Work and society

> **Question:** Some people believe that success in life comes mainly from hard work and determination, rather than from luck or family background. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** determination · opportunity · privilege · social mobility · circumstances · self-made

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: hard work matters, but family background decides where people start.*

**🟡 B1:** Hard work is important, but people do not start from the same place. A child from a rich family can go to a good school, learn English and get help from their parents' friends. A child from a poor family may need to work after school. Both children can work hard, but the rich child has more chances. For example, two of my classmates studied equally hard, but only the one whose parents paid for extra classes got a scholarship.

**🔴 B2:** While hard work is undoubtedly important, family background often determines how far effort can take a person. Children from wealthy families usually attend better schools, receive private tutoring and benefit from their parents' professional networks, all of which multiply the results of their efforts. By contrast, children from poor families may have to work part-time, study in overcrowded classrooms or help to care for younger siblings, leaving them less time and energy to study. Therefore, two people who work equally hard may achieve very different levels of success.

**🟣 C1:** The belief that success is primarily a matter of effort contains an important truth but omits an equally important one. Effort matters, yet its returns are heavily conditioned by circumstance: the same diligence yields very different outcomes depending on the quality of one's schooling, the stability of one's home and the networks available to one's family. Moreover, the capacity for sustained effort is itself shaped by background, since chronic financial stress, poor nutrition and insecure housing all undermine concentration and planning. To attribute success purely to hard work is therefore to mistake a necessary condition for a sufficient one.

**⚫ C2:** Hard work is the engine of success, but circumstance decides the road. Two students may study with equal devotion; one does so in a quiet room with a tutor on call, the other at a kitchen table after a shift at a noodle stall. When the first succeeds, we praise their effort, and rightly so. What we rarely notice is how much of the road was paved before they set off. Effort is essential. It is simply not the whole story, and those who have been lucky are often the last to see it.

The importance of effort is beyond dispute. Mastery in almost any domain requires years of sustained practice, and determination enables individuals to persist through failure and rejection. The stories of people who have overcome poverty or discrimination through extraordinary perseverance are genuine and inspiring, and a culture that values effort encourages individuals to invest in their own development.

Nevertheless, the returns on effort are heavily shaped by circumstances beyond individual control. A child born into an affluent, educated family typically enjoys better schooling, private tuition, stable housing and access to professional networks, each of which amplifies the results of their work.
