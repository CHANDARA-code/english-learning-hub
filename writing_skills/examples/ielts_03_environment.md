## 21. Individuals or Governments and the Environment

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Environment

> **Question:** Some people think that individuals can do little to protect the environment and that only governments and large companies can make a real difference. Others believe that individuals should take responsibility. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** carbon footprint · collective action · lifestyle changes · legislation · consumer pressure · large-scale emissions

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: small individual actions add up and push companies and governments to change.*

**🟡 B1:** I believe that small actions by ordinary people are important. If one person stops using plastic bags, it is not a big change. But if millions of people do the same, the result is very big. People can also choose to buy products from companies that protect the environment. For example, when many customers asked for paper straws, many cafés stopped using plastic ones. So individuals can make a difference.

**🔴 B2:** Although a single person's actions may seem insignificant, individual choices can have a powerful combined effect. When millions of consumers reduce their energy use, avoid single-use plastics or choose more sustainable products, the overall impact on emissions and waste becomes substantial. Moreover, consumer behaviour sends a clear signal to businesses: as demand for environmentally friendly products grows, companies are encouraged to change their practices in order to stay competitive. In this way, individuals can influence the very organisations that are often considered more powerful.

**🟣 C1:** The claim that individual action is futile underestimates the ways in which private choices shape public outcomes. Consumer demand has repeatedly pushed companies towards greener products, from recyclable packaging to electric vehicles, and it is citizens, ultimately, who elect the governments expected to legislate. Individual behaviour also has a cultural dimension: every person who cycles to work or refuses excessive packaging helps to normalise such conduct, making ambitious policies more politically acceptable. Individual and collective action, in other words, are not alternatives but mutually reinforcing.

**⚫ C2:** Every social transformation looks, in retrospect, like the work of governments and great institutions. Look closer and it begins elsewhere: with the first households to install solar panels while they were still expensive, the first shoppers to refuse a plastic bag, the first voters to rank the climate above their tax bill. Individual actions rarely change the world directly. What they change is what seems normal — and once something seems normal, laws and markets follow with surprising speed.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 253 words

Environmental problems such as climate change and pollution are becoming more serious. Some people think that ordinary people cannot do much, and only governments and big companies can protect the environment. Other people think that everyone should help. In this essay, I will discuss both views.

On the one hand, some people believe that governments and companies are more important. Big factories, power stations and airlines produce much more pollution than one family. Governments can make laws, for example to stop factories putting dirty water into rivers. They can also build public transport and invest in solar energy. One person cannot do these things.

On the other hand, other people think that individuals are also important. If one person saves electricity, it is a small change. However, if millions of people save electricity, use less plastic and take the bus, the result is very big. Also, when customers choose environmentally friendly products, companies must change. For example, many cafés now use paper straws because customers asked for them.

In my opinion, both groups have a responsibility, but governments and companies must do more because they have more power and money. However, ordinary people should not wait for them. We can recycle, save water and electricity, and teach our children to love nature. We can also vote for leaders who care about the environment.

In conclusion, governments and big companies can make the biggest difference, but individuals also have an important role. If everyone works together, we can protect the environment for the future.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 283 words

As environmental problems become increasingly urgent, there is disagreement about who should bear the main responsibility for solving them. While some argue that only governments and large corporations can make a meaningful difference, others insist that individuals must also play their part. In my opinion, both are essential, but the greatest responsibility lies with those who have the most power.

Those who emphasise the role of governments and businesses point to the scale of the problem. A large proportion of greenhouse gas emissions comes from power generation, heavy industry and transport systems, which individuals cannot directly control. Governments, by contrast, can introduce laws to limit pollution, invest in renewable energy and build efficient public transport networks, while large companies can transform entire supply chains. From this perspective, asking individuals to use fewer plastic bags seems almost insignificant.

However, supporters of individual action argue that personal choices matter more than they appear. When millions of people change their habits — for example, by reducing meat consumption, saving energy or choosing public transport — the combined effect can be considerable. Moreover, consumer demand influences corporate behaviour: companies are more likely to adopt greener practices if their customers expect it. Individuals also influence governments through their votes and by supporting environmental campaigns.

In my view, these positions are not contradictory. Governments and corporations have the resources and authority to make large-scale changes, so they should lead. Nevertheless, individual action remains important, both because of its cumulative impact and because it creates public pressure for stronger policies.

In conclusion, while governments and large companies have the greatest capacity to protect the environment, individuals still have a valuable role to play. Real progress is most likely when both act together.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 286 words

Debates about environmental responsibility often present a false choice between personal virtue and institutional power. Those who dismiss individual action as futile and those who emphasise lifestyle change each capture part of the truth. I would argue, however, that the two are interdependent, and that the most significant contribution individuals can make is not through consumption alone but through civic participation.

The case for prioritising governments and corporations is compelling. The bulk of global emissions arises from energy systems, industrial processes and infrastructure over which individuals exercise little direct control. A household that diligently recycles cannot offset the emissions of a coal-fired power station, and framing climate change primarily as a matter of personal choice risks shifting responsibility away from those best placed to act. It is worth recalling that the concept of the personal "carbon footprint" was popularised in part by an oil company's advertising campaign.

Yet the conclusion that individuals are irrelevant does not follow. Personal choices, aggregated across millions of people, can shift markets: growing demand for electric vehicles and plant-based foods has already prompted substantial corporate investment. More importantly, individuals are not merely consumers but citizens. Governments rarely adopt ambitious environmental policies without public pressure, and it is through voting, campaigning and professional choices that individuals exert their greatest influence on institutions.

The implication is that the question should be reframed. Rather than asking whether individuals or institutions are responsible, we should recognise that institutional change is largely driven by collective individual action. Private habits matter, but public engagement matters more.

In conclusion, governments and corporations must bear the primary responsibility for environmental protection, but they are unlikely to discharge it without sustained pressure from individuals acting as both consumers and citizens.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 292 words

Few environmental debates are as circular as this one. Individuals point to governments, governments point to industry, industry points to consumer demand, and the planet continues to warm while the finger moves around the room. Breaking the circle requires recognising that the question is badly framed.

The institutional argument has the weight of arithmetic behind it. Power stations, steelworks, cement plants and aviation produce emissions on a scale that no amount of careful recycling can match. There is even something suspicious about the emphasis on personal virtue: the very idea of an individual "carbon footprint" was popularised in part by an oil company's advertising campaign, which suggests that some institutions have been only too happy for the rest of us to feel responsible.

Yet the conclusion that individuals are therefore powerless does not follow. Institutions are not weather systems; they are made of people and answerable to them. Governments legislate when voters demand it; companies invest when customers and employees expect it. The collapse in the price of solar panels, for instance, was driven partly by policies that citizens had campaigned for and partly by millions of households choosing to install them. Individual action matters less as a sum of private sacrifices than as a signal that makes collective change possible.

What individuals can least afford is resignation. The person who concludes that nothing they do matters will neither change their habits nor pressure their representatives — and it is precisely that second form of action on which everything else depends.

The responsibility, then, is shared but not equal. Institutions must make the largest changes, but they will make them only when enough individuals insist. The choice between personal and collective action is a false one: the first is how the second begins.

## 22. Renewable Energy and Higher Bills

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Environment and energy

> **Question:** Some people believe that governments should invest heavily in renewable energy, such as solar and wind power, even if this means higher electricity bills for consumers. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** renewable energy · fossil fuels · electricity bills · energy security · long-term savings · low-income households

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: renewable energy makes a country less dependent on imported fuel.*

**🟡 B1:** Renewable energy can make a country more independent. Many countries buy oil and gas from other countries, and the price can go up very quickly. For example, when there is a war or a problem in another part of the world, fuel prices can rise suddenly. But the sun and the wind are free, and nobody can stop them. So investing in renewable energy can protect a country in the future.

**🔴 B2:** Investing in renewable energy also improves a country's energy security. Nations that depend on imported oil, gas or coal are vulnerable to sudden price rises caused by wars, trade disputes or supply problems, as many European countries discovered when gas prices soared after 2021. By contrast, sunlight and wind are domestic resources that cannot be controlled by other countries. Therefore, although renewable energy requires high initial investment, it can protect consumers from unpredictable fuel prices in the long run.

**🟣 C1:** Beyond its environmental rationale, renewable energy offers a compelling strategic advantage: independence. Countries reliant on imported fossil fuels are exposed to price shocks and political pressure beyond their control, as the dramatic rise in European gas prices following Russia's invasion of Ukraine vividly demonstrated. Solar and wind resources, by contrast, are domestic, inexhaustible and immune to embargoes. Viewed in this light, higher electricity bills in the short term may be better understood as an insurance premium against far greater volatility in the future.

**⚫ C2:** Oil and gas have always come with a hidden clause: whoever sells them can change the price, or stop selling. Governments that built their energy systems on imported fuel have repeatedly discovered this at the worst possible moment. Sunlight carries no such clause. No cartel controls it; no hostile power can blockade it. Paying more for renewable electricity today is less an environmental sacrifice than a purchase of something fossil fuels could never offer at any price — the ability to keep the lights on without asking anyone's permission.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 281 words

Many countries still produce most of their electricity from coal, oil and gas. Some people think that governments should spend a lot of money on renewable energy, such as solar and wind power, even if people have to pay more for electricity. I mostly agree with this idea.

Firstly, renewable energy is much better for the environment. When power stations burn coal and oil, they produce a lot of pollution and greenhouse gases. This causes climate change, which brings floods, droughts and very hot weather. Solar and wind power are clean, so they can help to protect the planet for our children.

Secondly, renewable energy is good for the future of the country. Coal, oil and gas will run out one day, but the sun and the wind will never run out. Also, many countries must buy oil and gas from other countries, and the price can go up suddenly. If a country produces its own clean energy, it can be more independent.

Thirdly, renewable energy can create new jobs. People are needed to build, install and repair solar panels and wind turbines. In my country, for example, more and more homes and factories now have solar panels on their roofs.

However, higher electricity bills can be a big problem for poor families. They already find it difficult to pay for food, rent and school fees. Therefore, the government should help these families, for example by giving them lower prices.

In conclusion, I agree that governments should invest in renewable energy, even if electricity becomes more expensive for a short time. It is good for the environment, for the economy and for the future. However, poor families should get some help.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 282 words

As the effects of climate change become more visible, many governments are under pressure to replace fossil fuels with renewable energy sources. I largely agree that heavy investment in renewables is justified, even if it results in higher electricity bills in the short term, although measures must be taken to protect low-income households.

The most important reason is the environmental cost of fossil fuels. Burning coal, oil and gas is the main source of the greenhouse gases responsible for global warming, which is already causing more frequent heatwaves, droughts and floods. The economic damage caused by these disasters is enormous, so the cost of acting now is likely to be far lower than the cost of doing nothing.

In addition, renewable energy offers long-term economic benefits. Once solar panels and wind turbines have been built, the energy they produce is almost free, since sunlight and wind do not have to be purchased. In fact, in many parts of the world, solar and wind power are now among the cheapest sources of new electricity. Renewables also improve energy security, as countries become less dependent on imported fuels whose prices can rise suddenly because of international conflicts.

Nevertheless, higher bills can place a heavy burden on poorer families, who spend a larger proportion of their income on energy. If governments fail to address this, public support for green policies may weaken. Possible solutions include lower prices for the first units of electricity each household uses, or financial support for energy-saving improvements to homes.

In conclusion, investing heavily in renewable energy is a wise decision, even if it temporarily increases electricity prices. However, governments must ensure that the costs of the transition are shared fairly.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 300 words

The transition from fossil fuels to renewable energy is frequently presented as a trade-off between environmental responsibility and affordability. I agree that governments should invest heavily in renewables, even at the cost of temporarily higher bills, but I would argue that the trade-off is less stark than it appears — and that its distributional consequences deserve far more attention than they usually receive.

The environmental case scarcely needs restating. Fossil fuel combustion is the principal driver of climate change, whose costs — in crop failures, flood damage and heat-related illness — are rising steadily. Delaying the transition does not avoid these costs; it merely postpones and multiplies them.

What is less often acknowledged is that the economic case has shifted decisively. The cost of solar and wind generation has fallen dramatically over the past decade, to the point where they are now among the cheapest sources of new electricity in many markets. The higher bills associated with the transition therefore stem largely from upfront investment in generation, storage and grid infrastructure, rather than from renewables being inherently expensive. Once built, such systems insulate consumers from the volatility of fossil fuel markets, as the energy price shocks experienced across Europe after 2021 made painfully clear.

The genuine difficulty is one of fairness. Energy costs absorb a larger share of poorer households' budgets, and a transition that ignores this risks deepening inequality and provoking political backlash. Targeted measures — social tariffs, subsidised home insulation and support for low-income households to adopt rooftop solar — are therefore not optional additions but essential components of any credible policy.

In short, heavy investment in renewable energy is justified on environmental, economic and strategic grounds. The challenge lies not in whether to pay for the transition, but in ensuring that its costs are borne by those most able to afford them.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 309 words

Every electricity bill contains a price that is visible and a cost that is not. The visible price is what households pay each month; the invisible cost is what fossil fuels charge to the future — in heatwaves, failed harvests and flooded cities — and that bill is sent to people who never used the electricity. Seen in this light, I agree that governments should invest heavily in renewable energy, even at the price of higher bills, though the question of who pays those bills matters as much as whether they are paid.

The strongest objection is not about the climate at all, but about fairness. Energy is a necessity, not a luxury; a rise in prices barely registers in a wealthy household but can force a poor family to choose between cooling and eating. A policy that asks the poorest to finance the transition is not only unjust but politically fragile, since nothing destroys public support for climate action faster than the perception that it is a tax on ordinary people.

That objection, however, is an argument about design rather than direction. Social tariffs, subsidised insulation and targeted grants for rooftop solar can shield the vulnerable while the system changes. And the change itself is increasingly an economic bargain rather than a sacrifice: solar and wind are now among the cheapest sources of new electricity in much of the world. Much of the extra cost lies in the transition — grids, storage, construction — rather than in the destination.

There is also the question of security. Oil and gas can be withheld, weaponised or priced beyond reach by events on the other side of the planet, as Europe discovered after 2021. Sunlight cannot.

Higher bills today, fairly distributed, are therefore not so much a sacrifice for the future as an overdue payment on a debt we have been running up for generations.

## 23. Taxing Air Travel

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Environment

> **Question:** Some people think that governments should discourage air travel by raising taxes on flights, in order to protect the environment. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** carbon emissions · frequent flyers · low-cost airlines · tourism industry · high-speed rail · aviation fuel

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: a tax that grows with the number of flights is fairer than one tax for everyone.*

**🟡 B1:** I think a tax on people who fly very often is fairer than a tax on everyone. Many people fly only once a year to visit their family or have a holiday. But some rich people fly every week for business or for fun. If the tax is higher for people who fly many times, the rich will pay more, and ordinary families can still travel. This is a fair way to protect the environment.

**🔴 B2:** A fairer approach would be a "frequent flyer levy", in which the tax increases with the number of flights a person takes each year. In many countries, a relatively small group of frequent travellers is responsible for a large share of all flights. Under such a system, a family taking one holiday a year would pay little or no extra tax, while those who fly many times would pay considerably more. This would reduce unnecessary flights without making air travel unaffordable for ordinary people.

**🟣 C1:** If flight taxes are to be both effective and equitable, they should target frequency rather than travel itself. Surveys in several wealthy countries suggest that a minority of frequent flyers accounts for a disproportionate share of flights, while many citizens fly rarely or not at all. A progressive levy that rises with each additional flight would therefore concentrate the burden on those whose travel contributes most to emissions, leaving the occasional family holiday largely untouched. Such a design would also blunt the most common objection to aviation taxes: that they turn flying into a privilege of the wealthy.

**⚫ C2:** Taxing all flights equally treats the student flying home once a year exactly like the executive who boards a plane every Monday. It is simple, and it is unjust. Aviation's emissions are not spread evenly across society; they are concentrated in a small group of habitual travellers for whom boarding a plane is as routine as catching a bus. A levy that grows with each additional journey would ask most from those who fly most — the same principle we already accept for almost everything else consumed to excess.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 275 words

Air travel is becoming more popular because flights are now cheaper than in the past. However, planes produce a lot of pollution. Some people think that the government should make flights more expensive with higher taxes to protect the environment. I partly agree with this idea.

On the one hand, higher taxes can reduce the number of flights. Planes burn a lot of fuel and produce greenhouse gases, which cause climate change. If tickets are more expensive, some people will fly less. For example, they may take a train or a bus for short journeys, or they may have a holiday in their own country. The government can also use the tax money to build better trains.

On the other hand, higher taxes can be unfair. Rich people can pay the extra money easily, so they will continue to fly. However, poor families may not be able to visit their relatives who live in other countries. For example, many people from my country work abroad, and they need flights to come home to see their families. Also, many countries need tourists, and if flights are very expensive, fewer tourists will come.

In my opinion, a better idea is to make the tax higher for people who fly very often. People who fly only once or twice a year should pay a small tax, but people who fly every week should pay much more. In addition, governments should build fast trains so that people have another choice.

In conclusion, I agree that we need to reduce flights to protect the environment. However, the tax should be fair, and it should not stop ordinary families from travelling.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 279 words

Low-cost airlines have made air travel accessible to millions of people, but aviation is also a significant and growing source of greenhouse gas emissions. While I agree that governments should take action to reduce the environmental impact of flying, I believe a simple increase in taxes on all flights would be unfair and should be combined with other measures.

There are clear arguments in favour of higher taxes. At present, aviation fuel is taxed lightly in many parts of the world, which means that ticket prices do not reflect the environmental damage flying causes. Raising taxes would encourage people to fly less often, particularly for short journeys where trains or buses are realistic alternatives. The revenue could also be invested in greener transport, such as high-speed railways or research into cleaner aircraft fuels.

However, a flat increase in taxes would affect different groups very unequally. Wealthy travellers would hardly notice the extra cost, whereas lower-income families, including migrant workers who rely on flights to visit their relatives, would be hit hardest. Furthermore, many developing countries depend heavily on tourism, and a sharp rise in the cost of flying could damage their economies.

A fairer solution would be a progressive tax that increases with the number of flights a person takes each year. Under this system, occasional travellers would pay little extra, while frequent flyers, who are responsible for a large share of emissions, would pay considerably more. At the same time, governments should improve alternatives, such as rail links between major cities.

In conclusion, I agree that air travel should be discouraged to protect the environment, but only through well-designed taxes that target frequent flyers rather than ordinary families.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 291 words

Aviation occupies an awkward position in climate policy. It accounts for a modest but growing share of global emissions, yet it is also one of the hardest sectors to decarbonise, since clean alternatives to jet fuel remain expensive and immature. I agree that governments should use taxation to discourage flying, but the effectiveness and fairness of such taxes depend almost entirely on their design.

The rationale for taxation is straightforward. In many jurisdictions, aviation enjoys exemptions from the fuel taxes paid by motorists, so ticket prices fail to reflect the environmental costs imposed by flying. Correcting this distortion would reduce demand at the margin, especially on short-haul routes where rail offers a viable alternative, and would generate revenue that could fund cleaner technology.

A flat tax, however, would be both inequitable and inefficient. For affluent travellers, a modest surcharge is barely noticeable, whereas for a migrant worker saving to visit family abroad it may be decisive. Such a tax would therefore suppress the journeys with the greatest personal value while leaving the most frequent — and often least essential — flights largely untouched. It could also harm developing economies heavily dependent on international tourism.

A more defensible alternative is a progressive levy that rises with each additional flight taken in a year. Since a relatively small proportion of the population accounts for a disproportionate share of flights, this approach would concentrate the burden on those responsible for the most emissions. Combined with investment in high-speed rail and sustainable aviation fuel, it could reduce emissions without turning air travel into a privilege of the rich.

In conclusion, taxing flights is justified, but only if the tax is designed to target frequency rather than access. Otherwise, it risks being both environmentally ineffective and socially regressive.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 303 words

Flying is the most carbon-intensive thing that many people ever do, and it is also, for many, the most meaningful: the journey home for a wedding, the first sight of a foreign city, the reunion at an arrivals gate. Any policy that treats air travel purely as pollution will misunderstand it. Any policy that treats it purely as freedom will misunderstand the atmosphere. Taxes can help — but only if they are aimed with care.

The argument for taxation begins with a simple observation: flying is underpriced. In much of the world, aviation fuel escapes the taxes routinely paid on petrol, and ticket prices ignore the damage each journey does to the climate. A cost that is not paid by the traveller does not disappear; it is transferred to everyone else, including generations not yet born.

But a flat tax would transfer the burden in a different and equally unfair direction. To a frequent business traveller, an extra charge is a rounding error; to a construction worker saving for years to visit their mother abroad, it may cancel the trip. A tax that prices out the rare, precious journey while barely touching the routine weekend flight would reduce happiness far more efficiently than it reduced emissions.

The better instrument is progressive. Tax the first flight of the year lightly, the second more, and the tenth heavily, and the burden falls where the emissions are concentrated — on a restless minority for whom flying is less an event than a habit. Spend the revenue on the alternatives that make flying unnecessary: fast trains between neighbouring cities, and research into fuels that might one day make flying clean.

I agree, then, that governments should discourage air travel — not by closing the sky to ordinary people, but by making the habit, rather than the journey, pay its way.

## 24. Fresh Water Shortages

**Type:** Problem and solution · **Theme:** Environment

> **Question:** In many parts of the world, fresh water is becoming scarce. What are the causes of this problem, and what can be done to solve it?

**Useful vocabulary:** water scarcity · groundwater · irrigation · drought · water pollution · wastewater recycling

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: farms use most of the world's water, so better irrigation is the key solution.*

**🟡 B1:** Farms use a lot of water, so farmers can help to save it. Many farmers still flood their fields with water, and much of it is wasted. With drip irrigation, small pipes give water directly to the roots of the plants. This uses much less water. For example, some farmers in my village started using drip irrigation for vegetables, and now they use less water and still get good harvests.

**🔴 B2:** Since agriculture uses the largest share of fresh water in most countries, improving irrigation is one of the most effective solutions. Traditional methods, such as flooding entire fields, waste large amounts of water through evaporation and run-off. In contrast, modern techniques like drip irrigation deliver water directly to the roots of plants, which can reduce water use considerably while maintaining or even increasing crop yields. Governments could encourage farmers to adopt these methods by offering subsidies or low-interest loans.

**🟣 C1:** Any serious response to water scarcity must begin with agriculture, which accounts for roughly seventy per cent of global freshwater withdrawals. Much of this water is lost through inefficient practices such as flood irrigation, where evaporation and run-off waste a substantial proportion before it ever reaches the crops. Precision techniques, including drip irrigation and soil-moisture monitoring, can dramatically reduce this waste. The obstacle is rarely technological but financial: small farmers often lack the capital for such investments, which is why targeted subsidies and the reform of artificially low water prices are likely to prove decisive.

**⚫ C2:** When we picture water shortages, we imagine taps running dry in cities. The real drain lies elsewhere: in fields where water is poured across the ground and left to evaporate under the sun, because it costs the farmer almost nothing. Agriculture takes the lion's share of the world's fresh water, and much of it is used with a carelessness that only cheap resources permit. Fix the price, help farmers afford the drip lines, and the most effective water conservation programme in history might turn out to be an irrigation pipe.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 270 words

Water is necessary for life, but in many countries there is not enough clean water for everyone. This problem is getting worse every year. In this essay, I will explain the causes of this problem and suggest some solutions.

The first cause is that the population is growing. More people need more water for drinking, cooking and washing. Also, cities are becoming bigger, so they need huge amounts of water every day.

The second cause is agriculture. Farms use more water than anything else, because crops like rice need a lot of water. Many farmers use old methods, such as flooding their fields, so a lot of water is wasted.

The third cause is pollution and climate change. Factories and farms put chemicals into rivers, so the water becomes too dirty to drink. At the same time, climate change causes longer dry seasons and droughts in many places, so there is less rain.

There are several solutions to this problem. Firstly, farmers should use modern methods, such as drip irrigation, which gives water directly to plants and uses much less water. The government can help farmers to pay for this equipment. Secondly, the government should stop factories from polluting rivers and should punish companies that break the law. Thirdly, people can save water at home. For example, they can take shorter showers, fix broken pipes and collect rainwater for their gardens.

In conclusion, water shortages are caused by population growth, wasteful farming, pollution and climate change. If farmers, factories, governments and families all save and protect water, we can make sure that everyone has enough clean water in the future.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 275 words

Although water covers most of the Earth's surface, only a small fraction of it is fresh water that is accessible for human use. In many regions, this precious resource is becoming increasingly scarce. This essay will examine the main causes of water shortages and propose several practical solutions.

One of the main causes is rising demand. The global population continues to grow, and expanding cities and industries require ever larger quantities of water. Agriculture, however, is the biggest consumer by far, and inefficient irrigation methods waste enormous amounts. In some regions, farmers pump groundwater faster than it can be naturally replaced, causing water levels to fall year after year. Pollution is another important factor: untreated sewage, industrial waste and agricultural chemicals contaminate rivers and lakes, making the water unsafe to use. Finally, climate change is making rainfall less predictable, leading to longer and more severe droughts in many areas.

Several measures could help to solve this problem. Firstly, governments should encourage farmers to adopt water-saving technologies, such as drip irrigation, by providing financial support. Secondly, stricter laws and better wastewater treatment are needed to prevent pollution and allow water to be safely reused. Singapore, for example, recycles treated wastewater for industrial use and even to supplement its drinking water supply. Thirdly, cities should repair leaking pipes, which can lose a significant proportion of their water supply. Lastly, public awareness campaigns can encourage households to use water more carefully.

In conclusion, water shortages are caused by growing demand, wasteful agriculture, pollution and climate change. By combining efficient irrigation, stricter environmental regulation, better infrastructure and responsible individual behaviour, societies can secure their water supplies for the future.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 271 words

Water scarcity is often perceived as a problem confined to deserts, yet it now affects regions as diverse as South Asia, southern Africa and parts of Europe. There is no doubt that adaptation is now necessary. Rising sea levels, more intense storms and longer droughts are already affecting millions of people, and some further warming is unavoidable because of the greenhouse gases already in the atmosphere. Governments therefore need to invest in measures such as flood defences, drought-resistant crops, early warning systems for extreme weather and buildings designed to stay cool during heatwaves. Such investments can save lives and protect livelihoods.

However, the claim that it is too late to act is misleading. Climate change is not an all-or-nothing problem: the more the planet warms, the more severe the consequences become. Limiting warming to a lower level would still prevent an enormous amount of damage, even if some harm can no longer be avoided. Furthermore, there are limits to what adaptation can achieve. Some low-lying islands may become uninhabitable whatever defences are built, and in the hottest regions, extreme temperatures could make outdoor work dangerous for much of the year.

In addition, relying only on adaptation would be deeply unfair. Wealthy countries can afford sea walls and air conditioning, but poorer nations, which have contributed least to the problem, often cannot. Continuing to reduce emissions is therefore both a practical and a moral necessity.

In conclusion, governments must adapt to the effects of climate change that can no longer be avoided, but they must also continue to cut emissions in order to prevent far worse consequences in the future.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 312 words

The argument that it is too late to halt climate change, and that resources should therefore be devoted entirely to adaptation, has gained a certain fatalistic appeal. It contains a kernel of truth, but its conclusion is deeply mistaken. Adaptation and mitigation are not alternatives but complements, and abandoning the latter would make the former progressively more expensive and ultimately futile.

The kernel of truth is that a significant degree of warming is now locked in. Greenhouse gases already emitted will continue to affect the climate for decades, so communities must prepare for more frequent heatwaves, floods and droughts regardless of future policy. Investment in resilient infrastructure, early warning systems and climate-tolerant agriculture is therefore urgent, and it has been neglected for too long, particularly in the most vulnerable countries.

The flaw lies in treating climate change as a binary problem that has either been solved or lost. In reality, the severity of its impacts depends on how much further temperatures rise, and the relationship is not linear: each additional increment of warming brings disproportionately greater damage and increases the risk of crossing thresholds — such as the destabilisation of major ice sheets — beyond which changes become effectively irreversible. Adaptation is feasible only within limits, and those limits are set by the success or failure of mitigation.

There is also a question of justice. The capacity to adapt is distributed very unequally: wealthy nations can afford sea walls and cooling systems, whereas poorer nations, which bear the least responsibility for historical emissions, often cannot. A strategy of adaptation alone would effectively abandon those least able to protect themselves.

In conclusion, the choice between adaptation and mitigation is a false one. Governments must prepare for the warming already under way while continuing to cut emissions, because every fraction of a degree avoided reduces both human suffering and the cost of adapting to what remains.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 316 words

There are two ways to give up on climate change. The first is denial: insisting that the problem does not exist. The second, increasingly fashionable, is despair: insisting that it is too late to solve. The two sound like opposites, but they lead to exactly the same place — continued emissions — and for that reason I reject the argument that governments should now focus on adaptation alone.

The despairing view is built on a half-truth. It is correct that some warming is already locked in, and that the heatwaves, floods and failing monsoons of recent years are a preview of what is to come. Adaptation is therefore no longer optional. Coastal cities need defences, farmers need crops that tolerate drought and salt, and the poorest communities need early warning systems that give them time to escape a storm.

But the conclusion drawn from this truth is false. Climate change is not a switch that has already been flipped; it is a dial that is still turning. The difference between one level of warming and a slightly higher one is measured in lives, in harvests, in coastlines, and in the risk of triggering changes — the collapse of ice sheets, the drying of great forests — that no sea wall could ever hold back. Every tenth of a degree matters, which means that every tonne of carbon still matters.

There is also a moral asymmetry that the "adapt only" strategy conceals. Adaptation is a luxury good: the rich can build higher walls and install more air conditioning, while those who did least to cause the crisis are left to face it with the least protection. To stop cutting emissions is, in effect, to decide that the poor will pay.

The honest position is harder than either denial or despair. We must prepare for the damage we can no longer prevent while working relentlessly to prevent the damage we still can.

## 29. Nuclear Power

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Environment and energy

> **Question:** Some people believe that nuclear power is the best way to meet the world's growing energy needs without increasing pollution. Others think it is too dangerous. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** nuclear reactor · radioactive waste · low-carbon electricity · accident risk · reliable supply · construction costs

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: nuclear power gives clean electricity even when there is no sun or wind.*

**🟡 B1:** Nuclear power stations can produce electricity all day and all night. Solar panels do not work at night, and wind turbines stop when there is no wind. But a nuclear power station can work in any weather. It also produces almost no air pollution. For example, France gets most of its electricity from nuclear power, so it uses much less coal than many other countries.

**🔴 B2:** A key advantage of nuclear power is that it provides a reliable and continuous supply of low-carbon electricity. Solar and wind energy depend on the weather, so their output varies throughout the day and across the seasons. Nuclear plants, by contrast, can operate around the clock, providing a stable foundation for the electricity grid. France, which generates the majority of its electricity from nuclear power, has one of the least carbon-intensive electricity systems among major industrial economies, which shows how effective this approach can be.

**🟣 C1:** Nuclear power's principal virtue is that it combines two qualities rarely found together: very low carbon emissions and a constant, controllable output. Renewable sources are indispensable, but their intermittency means that a grid dominated by them requires either vast storage capacity or reliable backup, which in practice is often supplied by gas. Nuclear plants can provide this stability without the emissions. France's experience, in which nuclear reactors generate the majority of the country's electricity, suggests that a large-scale nuclear programme can decarbonise a power system far faster than many alternatives.

**⚫ C2:** The sun sets and the wind drops, but a city's hospitals, trains and refrigerators do not stop needing power. That simple fact is nuclear energy's strongest argument. Renewable sources can supply much of a modern grid, but someone must answer for the still, dark winter evening — and at present, the usual answer is gas. Nuclear power offers another: electricity that is steady, abundant and nearly free of carbon. Its risks are real. So is the risk of the alternative that fills the gap when we refuse it.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 304 words

Few energy sources divide opinion as sharply as nuclear power. To its advocates, it is an indispensable tool for decarbonisation; to its critics, an unacceptable gamble with public safety. Having weighed both positions, I would argue that the risks of nuclear power are often overstated relative to those of fossil fuels, but that its high cost limits its role in most countries' future energy mix.

The case in favour rests on a rare combination of attributes. Nuclear plants generate electricity with very low lifecycle emissions and, unlike solar or wind installations, produce a constant output that does not depend on the weather. This makes them a valuable complement to intermittent renewables in grids that must remain stable around the clock. France's extensive reactor fleet has given it one of the lowest-carbon electricity systems among major industrial economies.

The case against rests primarily on safety and waste. The accidents at Chernobyl and Fukushima demonstrated that nuclear failures, though rare, can render large areas uninhabitable and cause profound social disruption. Long-lived radioactive waste requires secure storage over timescales that exceed any human institution, and only a handful of countries have made real progress towards permanent disposal.

Yet these risks must be assessed comparatively. Air pollution from burning fossil fuels causes a very large number of premature deaths every year, a toll that dwarfs that of nuclear accidents, yet it rarely provokes comparable alarm because it is diffuse rather than dramatic. The more persuasive objection to nuclear power is economic: new plants are notoriously expensive and slow to build, whereas the costs of renewables and battery storage continue to fall.

In conclusion, nuclear power is safer than its reputation suggests, and existing plants should generally be kept in operation. Its future expansion, however, is likely to be justified only where renewables alone cannot provide a reliable supply.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 298 words

The case for nuclear power is strong. It generates vast quantities of electricity with almost no carbon emissions, and — unlike sunlight and wind — it does so on the calmest night of the year. For countries trying to build grids that are both clean and dependable, that combination is precious. France built most of its reactors within a couple of decades and, as a result, now enjoys one of the cleanest electricity systems in the industrial world.

The case against is not merely emotional. Accidents, however rare, can empty whole regions for generations. And there is a more mundane objection that may matter most: new reactors are extraordinarily expensive and slow, while solar panels, wind turbines and batteries grow cheaper almost every year.

My conclusion is therefore divided, as the evidence is. It would be foolish to shut down safe, functioning reactors only to replace them with fossil fuels, as some countries have done. But it would be equally foolish to bet the future mainly on a technology that can take fifteen years to build when the climate does not have fifteen years to wait. Nuclear power should be kept, respected and used where it is needed — neither worshipped nor feared.

## 30. Making Polluters Pay

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Environment

> **Question:** Some people believe that large companies should be forced to pay for the environmental damage they cause. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** "polluter pays" principle · fines · clean-up costs · carbon pricing · regulation · passed on to consumers

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: if companies do not pay for pollution, ordinary people pay instead.*

**🟡 B1:** When a company pollutes a river or the air, somebody must pay to clean it. If the company does not pay, the government uses taxpayers' money, or local people suffer. For example, if a factory puts chemicals in a river, farmers cannot use the water and fishermen lose their jobs. This is not fair. The company made the pollution, so the company should pay.

**🔴 B2:** When companies are not required to pay for the damage they cause, the costs do not disappear; they are simply transferred to the public. Communities living near polluting factories may suffer from health problems, and governments often have to use taxpayers' money to clean up contaminated land and water. Economists describe these costs as "externalities". Forcing companies to pay for them would not only be fairer but would also encourage businesses to invest in cleaner technology, since polluting would become more expensive than preventing pollution.

**🟣 C1:** The principle that polluters should pay is, at heart, a correction of a market distortion. When a company releases toxins into a river or carbon into the atmosphere without bearing the cost, its products appear cheaper than they truly are, while the real price is paid by others — in illness, lost livelihoods and public clean-up bills. Requiring firms to internalise these costs restores honest pricing. It also changes behaviour: once pollution appears on the balance sheet, investment in cleaner processes becomes a matter of commercial self-interest rather than voluntary goodwill.

**⚫ C2:** Pollution is a form of taking that economists politely call an externality. A factory that pours waste into a river takes something — clean water, a fisherman's income, a child's health — without paying for it, and records the saving as profit. Forcing polluters to pay is therefore not a punishment but a restoration of ordinary commercial honesty: the principle that a business should bear the full cost of what it produces. A company that can survive only by sending part of its bill to strangers is not efficient. It is subsidised.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 306 words

The idea that those who cause environmental damage should bear its cost — the so-called "polluter pays" principle — commands wide support in theory yet is inconsistently applied in practice. I strongly agree that large companies should be required to pay for the damage they cause, both as a matter of justice and as the most efficient means of reducing pollution.

The justice argument rests on the concept of externalities. When a firm discharges effluent into a river or emits greenhouse gases, it imposes costs on third parties — in health, livelihoods and public expenditure — that are not reflected in the price of its products. The company's profits are, in effect, partially subsidised by those who suffer the consequences. Requiring firms to internalise these costs corrects this imbalance and ensures that prices reflect the true social cost of production.

The efficiency argument is equally compelling. Once pollution carries a price, companies acquire a direct financial incentive to reduce it, and they are generally better placed than regulators to identify the cheapest ways of doing so. Mechanisms such as carbon taxes and emissions trading exploit this insight, allowing reductions to occur wherever they are least costly.

Two objections deserve consideration. The first is that costs will be passed on to consumers. This is partly true, but it is precisely the point: higher prices for pollution-intensive goods encourage a shift towards cleaner alternatives, and the revenue raised can be used to protect low-income households. The second is that unilateral action may drive industry to countries with weaker regulations. This risk is genuine, but it can be reduced through border adjustments that apply equivalent charges to imports, as the European Union has begun to do.

In conclusion, making polluters pay is not an attack on business but the correction of a market failure. Applied consistently, it aligns commercial incentives with environmental protection.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 300 words

There is a simple rule that governs most of economic life: if you damage someone's property, you pay for it. Break a shop window and you will be billed for the glass. Yet for much of industrial history, a company could poison a river, blacken a city's air or warm the entire planet and send the bill to no one in particular. The principle that polluters should pay is therefore not a radical innovation. It is the belated extension of an ordinary rule to the most extraordinary kind of damage.

The moral case is almost self-evident. Pollution transfers costs from those who profit to those who suffer: the family downstream from the tannery, the child breathing beside the highway, the farmer whose harvest fails in a drought made likelier by distant emissions. A business that depends on that transfer is being quietly underwritten by its victims.

The practical case is just as strong, and more interesting. Regulators cannot know the cheapest way for every factory to clean up. Put a price on pollution, however, and the factories will find out for themselves, with an ingenuity that no inspector could command. Carbon taxes and emissions markets work precisely because they harness self-interest rather than fighting it.

The objections are familiar. Costs, it is said, will be passed to consumers — which is true, and is how markets are supposed to signal that something is scarce or harmful. Industry, it is said, will flee to countries with laxer rules — a real risk, but one that border charges on imports are beginning to address. Neither objection defends the status quo; both merely argue for designing the policy well.

I agree, then, without reservation. Making polluters pay is not anti-business. It is simply the insistence that business, like everyone else, should pay its own bills.
