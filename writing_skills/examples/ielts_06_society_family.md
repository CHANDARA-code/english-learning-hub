## 51. An Ageing Population

**Type:** Problem and solution · **Theme:** Society

> **Question:** In many countries, the proportion of older people in the population is increasing. What problems does this cause, and what solutions can you suggest?

**Useful vocabulary:** ageing population · birth rate · pension costs · healthcare demand · labour shortage · flexible retirement

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: letting healthy older people keep working flexibly helps everyone.*

**🟡 B1:** One solution is to let older people continue working if they want to. Many people aged sixty or seventy are still healthy and have a lot of experience. They can work part-time, teach young workers or help in their community. For example, my grandfather retired from his job as an engineer, but he still teaches young engineers two days a week. This helps the company, and he feels useful.

**🔴 B2:** One effective response to an ageing population is to make it easier for older people to continue working. Many people in their sixties and seventies are healthy and experienced, and they could contribute to the economy through part-time or flexible employment. Employers could offer reduced hours, mentoring roles or phased retirement, allowing older workers to pass on their knowledge to younger colleagues. This would help to reduce labour shortages, ease pressure on pension systems and give older people a sense of purpose and social connection.

**🟣 C1:** A promising response to demographic ageing is to rethink the rigid boundary between work and retirement. Many people now reach their sixties in good health and with decades of expertise, yet workplace norms often push them into full retirement abruptly. Flexible arrangements — part-time roles, mentoring positions and phased retirement — would allow them to continue contributing, easing labour shortages and pension costs. The benefits are not solely economic: continued engagement is associated with better physical and mental health in later life, which in turn reduces demand on health services.

**⚫ C2:** Retirement at a fixed age was invented for a world in which work was physically exhausting and life after sixty-five was short. Both conditions have changed; the rule has not. Many people now leave work at the peak of their judgement and experience, often with decades of healthy life ahead, while employers complain of shortages of exactly the skills those people possess. Allowing a gradual withdrawal — fewer hours, advisory roles, the mentoring of successors — would turn a demographic burden, at least in part, back into a resource.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 266 words

In many countries, such as Japan, South Korea and many European countries, there are more and more old people and fewer young people. This happens because people live longer and families have fewer children. This change causes several problems, but there are also some solutions.

The first problem is money. When many people retire, the government must pay more pensions. At the same time, there are fewer young workers who pay taxes. This can make it difficult for the government to pay for everything.

The second problem is healthcare. Old people often have health problems, so they need more doctors, nurses and medicine. Hospitals may become very busy, and healthcare costs will increase.

The third problem is that there are not enough workers. When old people retire and there are not many young people, companies cannot find enough workers, especially in factories, hospitals and care homes.

There are some solutions to these problems. Firstly, the government can encourage families to have more children, for example by giving money to parents and providing cheap childcare. Secondly, older people who are still healthy can work part-time for a few more years. They have a lot of experience, and many of them enjoy working. Thirdly, countries can allow more foreign workers to come and work, especially in jobs where there are not enough people. Finally, people should live healthy lives, so they stay healthy when they are old.

In conclusion, an ageing population causes problems with money, healthcare and workers. If governments support families, allow older people to work longer and welcome foreign workers, these problems can be reduced.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 272 words

Due to rising life expectancy and falling birth rates, many countries are experiencing a rapid increase in the proportion of older people. This essay will examine the main problems associated with this trend and propose some solutions.

The most obvious problem is economic. As more people retire and fewer young people enter the workforce, governments must pay pensions to a growing number of retirees while collecting taxes from a shrinking working population. This places enormous pressure on public finances. A second problem is the rising demand for healthcare and long-term care, since older people are more likely to suffer from chronic illnesses such as heart disease and dementia. Hospitals and care homes may face shortages of staff and funding. Finally, an ageing population can lead to labour shortages in key sectors, which may slow economic growth.

Several measures could help to address these challenges. Firstly, governments could gradually raise the retirement age and encourage flexible working arrangements, allowing healthy older people to continue working part-time and share their experience. Secondly, policies that support families, such as affordable childcare and paid parental leave, may encourage young couples to have more children, although the effect of such policies is often modest. Thirdly, carefully managed immigration could help to fill labour shortages, particularly in healthcare and care work. In addition, investment in preventive healthcare would help people to remain healthy for longer, reducing the burden on health services.

In conclusion, an ageing population puts pressure on pensions, healthcare and the labour market. However, a combination of flexible retirement, family-friendly policies, managed immigration and preventive healthcare can help societies to adapt successfully to this demographic change.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 308 words

Population ageing, driven by longer life expectancy and persistently low birth rates, is one of the most significant demographic transformations of the modern era. Societies such as Japan and South Korea are at the forefront of this shift, but many others will follow. The challenges it creates are substantial, though they are more manageable than alarmist accounts suggest.

The most widely discussed problem is fiscal. Pension and healthcare systems were designed for populations with many workers per retiree; as this ratio declines, the cost of supporting older citizens falls on a proportionally smaller workforce. Demand for healthcare and long-term care also rises, since the prevalence of chronic conditions such as dementia increases sharply with age. Meanwhile, shrinking cohorts of young workers can produce labour shortages, particularly in care work, which is itself in growing demand.

Responses must therefore address both the supply of workers and the demand for care. Raising and, crucially, making more flexible the retirement age would allow healthy older people to remain economically active for longer, easing fiscal pressure while offering them continued purpose and social connection. Managed immigration can help to fill shortages in essential sectors, as many ageing societies have already recognised. Investment in preventive healthcare, meanwhile, would compress the period of life spent in poor health, reducing the demand for intensive care. Policies supporting families, such as affordable childcare and generous parental leave, may modestly raise birth rates, although evidence suggests their effect is limited.

Technology may also play a role, from assistive devices that allow older people to live independently for longer to automation in sectors facing labour shortages.

In short, population ageing is a genuine challenge, but it is not a catastrophe. Societies that adapt their institutions to longer lives, rather than treating older citizens solely as a burden, are well placed to turn a demographic problem into an opportunity.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 303 words

That people are living longer is one of humanity's great achievements; that it is so often discussed as a crisis says a good deal about the rigidity of our institutions. An ageing population does create real problems. But most of them arise not from longevity itself, but from systems designed for a world that no longer exists.

The problems are not imaginary. Pension systems built on the assumption of many workers supporting few retirees are now asked to support many retirees with fewer workers. Health services face growing numbers of patients with chronic conditions, dementia prominent among them. And in countries such as Japan and South Korea, where birth rates have fallen dramatically, entire regions are losing their young people, leaving villages of the elderly with few hands to care for them.

Yet the standard framing — old people as a burden, young people as providers — obscures as much as it reveals. Today's seventy-year-olds are, on average, healthier than their grandparents were at sixty, and many would welcome the chance to keep working in some form. The rigid retirement age pushes them off a cliff when a gentle slope would serve everyone better. Part-time roles, mentoring and phased retirement could turn apparent dependants into contributors.

Other remedies are more familiar. Managed immigration can fill gaps in care and other essential work; preventive healthcare can shorten the years people spend in poor health; support for young families, though its effect on birth rates is modest, makes having children less of a sacrifice. Technology, from assistive devices to automation, can help older people live independently for longer.

The ageing of society, then, is less a disaster than an unfinished redesign. We added decades to the average human life within little more than a century. It is not surprising that our institutions have yet to catch up.

## 52. Caring for Elderly Parents

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Family

> **Question:** Some people think that elderly people should be looked after by their families, while others believe that care homes or government services are better. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** family duty · care home · professional care · dementia · emotional support · respite care

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: family care gives older people love and familiarity that professionals cannot.*

**🟡 B1:** Many old people are happier when they live with their family. At home, they can see their children and grandchildren every day and feel loved. They can also eat familiar food and live in a place they know. In Cambodia, it is traditional for children to look after their parents, and many old people would feel sad or lonely in a care home. For example, my grandmother lives with us, and she enjoys helping to cook and telling stories.

**🔴 B2:** Family care offers emotional benefits that are difficult to replace. Older people who live with or near their relatives usually enjoy regular contact with their children and grandchildren, which can reduce loneliness and give them a continuing sense of purpose. They are also able to remain in a familiar environment, surrounded by people who know their habits, preferences and history. In many Asian cultures, caring for one's parents is also seen as a moral duty and a way of showing gratitude, so being moved to a care home may feel like rejection.

**🟣 C1:** The strongest argument for family care lies in the quality of relationships it can sustain. Professional carers may be highly skilled, but they are rarely able to offer the continuity of affection and shared history that relatives provide. For many older people, the daily presence of children and grandchildren is not a supplement to care but a central source of meaning and dignity. In cultures where caring for one's parents is regarded as a fundamental obligation, moreover, placement in an institution may be experienced not merely as a change of residence but as a painful break in a lifelong bond.

**⚫ C2:** No care home, however well run, can offer what a grandmother receives when her grandson sits with her after school: the sense of being known. Professional carers can bathe, feed and medicate with great skill, but they meet their residents at the end of long lives about which they know almost nothing. Family care, at its best, is not simply a cheaper service. It is a continuation of the relationships that give an ageing person's life its shape — which is why its loss can feel, to the old, like a second bereavement.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 286 words

In the past, most old people lived with their children. Today, some old people live in care homes or receive help from the government. Some people think that families should look after their old parents, but others think that care homes are better. In this essay, I will discuss both views.

On the one hand, many people believe that families should take care of their elderly parents. Old people often feel happier when they live with their children and grandchildren, because they feel loved and they are not lonely. Also, in many cultures, including in Cambodia, children feel it is their duty to look after their parents, because their parents looked after them when they were young. For example, my grandmother lives with my family, and she enjoys helping to cook and telling stories to my younger brother.

On the other hand, other people think that care homes or government services are sometimes better. Many young people work long hours, so they do not have time to look after their parents all day. Also, some old people have serious illnesses, like dementia, and they need nurses and doctors who have special training. In a good care home, they can get professional care 24 hours a day and meet people of their own age.

In my opinion, family care is the best choice when it is possible, because love and family are very important for old people. However, when old people need special medical care, a good care home can be a good choice, and families should visit them often.

In conclusion, both family care and care homes have advantages. I believe families should look after their parents if they can, but professional care is sometimes necessary.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 314 words

As populations age and family structures change, the question of who should care for elderly people has become increasingly important. While many believe that this responsibility belongs to families, others argue that professional care homes or government services are more suitable. In my view, family care is usually preferable, but professional support is essential in certain circumstances.

Supporters of family care emphasise the emotional benefits for older people. Living with or near their children and grandchildren allows them to remain part of family life, which can reduce loneliness and give them a sense of purpose. They are also able to stay in familiar surroundings, which is particularly important for their wellbeing. In many cultures, caring for one's parents is considered a moral obligation and an expression of gratitude, so placing them in a care home may be seen as abandonment. In addition, family care is generally less expensive than professional care.

On the other hand, those who favour care homes or state services point out that family care is not always practical or safe. In modern societies, adult children often work full-time, live far from their parents or have young children of their own, so they may be unable to provide constant support. Moreover, older people with serious conditions, such as dementia or severe mobility problems, may require specialised medical care that family members are not trained to provide. Caring for a seriously ill relative can also cause exhaustion, stress and financial difficulties for carers.

In my opinion, families should take primary responsibility where possible, but governments should support them, for example through home visits by nurses, day-care centres and financial help for family carers. When needs become too complex, a good-quality care home may be the best option.

In conclusion, family care provides love and familiarity, while professional care offers expertise. The best solution combines both, with families supported rather than left alone.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 321 words

The care of elderly people raises questions that are simultaneously practical, economic and deeply moral. Traditionally, in most societies, the family bore this responsibility; today, smaller households, geographic mobility and longer lifespans are straining that model. I would argue that family care remains the ideal where it is feasible, but that the state has an essential role in making it sustainable and in providing alternatives where it is not.

The case for family care rests primarily on the quality of relationships it can sustain. Older people cared for by relatives typically remain embedded in familiar routines, surroundings and social ties, and the daily presence of children and grandchildren can provide meaning and dignity that institutions struggle to replicate. In many cultures, including much of Asia, caring for one's parents is regarded as a fundamental expression of gratitude, so institutional care may be experienced as a painful rupture of that bond.

Yet this ideal often collides with reality. Adult children increasingly live far from their parents, work long hours and support children of their own, and the burden of care falls disproportionately on women, many of whom reduce their working hours or leave employment altogether. For older people with advanced dementia or complex medical needs, moreover, well-intentioned family members may lack the skills and equipment required for safe care, and the resulting strain can damage both carer and recipient.

The most sensible approach, therefore, is not a choice between family and institution but a partnership. Governments can support family carers through respite services, home nursing, day centres and financial allowances, enabling them to continue caring without sacrificing their own health or livelihoods. Where needs exceed what families can safely provide, high-quality residential care should be available and affordable.

In conclusion, families remain the best source of the emotional support older people need, but they cannot carry the burden alone. A humane system supports families where possible and replaces them only where necessary.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 309 words

How a society treats its oldest members is one of the more revealing tests of its character, and the question of who should care for them exposes a tension between two of our deepest values: loyalty to family and the professional competence we expect from institutions.

The case for family care is, at its heart, a case about recognition. An elderly woman cared for by her daughter is cared for by someone who remembers who she was before illness narrowed her world — the jokes she told, the dishes she cooked, the life she built. That continuity is a form of dignity that no rota of kind strangers can fully replicate. In many cultures, moreover, caring for one's parents is not a burden accepted reluctantly but a debt repaid willingly, and placing a parent in an institution may feel, to both generations, like a quiet betrayal.

But sentiment is not a care plan. Families today are smaller and more scattered than ever, adult children often work long hours in distant cities, and the labour of caring falls overwhelmingly on women, who sacrifice careers, income and health to provide it. When an elderly parent develops advanced dementia, love alone cannot lift them safely, manage their medication or keep watch through the night. Insisting that families must always cope is not a defence of tradition; it can be a way of making exhausted daughters carry what society refuses to share.

The wisest answer is not to choose between family and institution, but to build a partnership between them. The state can make family care sustainable — through home nurses, day centres, respite breaks and allowances for carers — and provide good residential care where needs become too great.

Old age should not force anyone to choose between being loved and being safe. A decent society arranges things so that they do not have to.

## 53. When Both Parents Work

**Type:** Two-part question (effects and solutions) · **Theme:** Family

> **Question:** In many families today, both parents work full-time. What effects does this have on children, and what can be done to reduce any negative effects?

**Useful vocabulary:** dual-income family · childcare · quality time · independence · role model · flexible working

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: children of working parents often become more independent and see good role models.*

**🟡 B1:** Children of working parents often learn to be independent. They learn to prepare their school bag, do their homework and help with housework without waiting for their parents. They also see that their mother and father work hard, so they learn that work is important. For example, my friend's parents both work in a bank, and she learned to cook simple meals when she was only ten. Now she is very responsible.

**🔴 B2:** Although dual-income families face challenges, children can also benefit from having two working parents. They often become more independent, learning to organise their time, complete homework without constant supervision and contribute to household tasks. Moreover, parents who work provide positive role models, showing children the value of effort, responsibility and financial independence. Some research even suggests that the daughters of working mothers are more likely to have successful careers themselves. In addition, two incomes can give families greater financial security and more educational opportunities.

**🟣 C1:** The debate about working parents often assumes that children can only lose from their parents' employment. The evidence is more nuanced. Children in dual-earner households frequently develop greater self-reliance, and parents who work model qualities such as diligence and financial responsibility. Studies across several countries have also found that the daughters of employed mothers are more likely to be in paid work and to hold supervisory roles as adults, suggesting that parental employment can broaden children's sense of what is possible. What matters most, it appears, is not the number of hours parents work but the quality of care and attention children receive.

**⚫ C2:** Children learn less from what their parents say than from what they watch them do. A child who sees both parents leave for work each morning, return tired but purposeful, and share the chores in the evening absorbs a lesson no lecture could deliver: that effort is normal, that independence is valuable, and that the world of work belongs to women as much as to men. The absence of a parent during working hours is a real cost. The presence of that example may be an equally real gift.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 284 words

Nowadays, in many families, both the father and the mother work full-time. This has some good and bad effects on children. In this essay, I will explain these effects and suggest some ways to reduce the bad effects.

There are some negative effects. Firstly, parents have less time to spend with their children. When parents come home late and tired, they may not have time to play, talk or help with homework. Some children may feel lonely or sad. Secondly, some children spend a lot of time alone at home, so they watch TV or play on their phones for many hours. Thirdly, young children often stay with babysitters or grandparents, so their parents may miss important moments in their lives.

However, there are also some positive effects. When both parents work, the family has more money, so children can have a better education, better healthcare and more activities. Also, children often become more independent. They learn to do their homework, prepare their things and help with housework. In addition, children see their parents work hard, so they learn that work is important.

There are several ways to reduce the negative effects. Firstly, parents should spend quality time with their children, for example by eating dinner together and doing activities at weekends. Secondly, companies should give parents flexible working hours, so they can take their children to school or come home earlier. Thirdly, the government should provide good and cheap childcare and after-school activities.

In conclusion, when both parents work, children may have less time with their parents, but they can also become more independent and have more opportunities. If parents, companies and the government work together, children can grow up happy and healthy.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 295 words

In many societies today, it is increasingly common for both parents to work full-time, whether because of financial necessity or personal choice. This essay will examine the effects of this trend on children and suggest measures to reduce any negative consequences.

One of the main concerns is that children may receive less time and attention from their parents. Long working hours and commuting can leave parents exhausted in the evenings, with limited energy for conversations, homework support or play. As a result, some children may feel neglected, and young children in particular may spend long hours in childcare or with other relatives. Without adequate supervision, older children may also spend excessive time on screens or be more vulnerable to negative influences.

However, the effects are not entirely negative. Two incomes usually provide greater financial security, allowing families to invest in education, healthcare and extracurricular activities. Children of working parents often become more independent and responsible, as they learn to manage their time and contribute to household tasks. Furthermore, working parents serve as role models, demonstrating the value of hard work and, in the case of mothers, challenging traditional gender stereotypes.

Several measures could help to minimise the negative effects. Parents can prioritise quality time, such as shared meals, reading together or weekend activities, which can matter more than the total number of hours spent together. Employers can support families by offering flexible working arrangements, remote work where possible and adequate parental leave. Governments should ensure the availability of affordable, high-quality childcare and after-school programmes.

In conclusion, having two working parents can reduce the time children spend with their families, but it can also bring financial benefits and encourage independence. With flexible workplaces, good childcare and a focus on quality time, families can minimise the drawbacks.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 307 words

The rise of the dual-earner household is one of the most significant social changes of recent decades. It has been driven partly by economic necessity, as single incomes have become insufficient in many cities, and partly by the expansion of opportunities for women. Its effects on children are more complex than either its critics or its defenders tend to acknowledge.

The potential disadvantages are real. Long working hours and commutes can leave parents with limited time and energy, reducing opportunities for conversation, play and support with schoolwork. Where affordable, high-quality childcare is unavailable, young children may spend extended periods in inadequate arrangements, while older children may be left unsupervised for long periods. The resulting strain can also affect parents' own wellbeing, with consequences for the emotional climate of the home.

Yet the evidence suggests that parental employment in itself does not harm children; much depends on the quality of care they receive and the circumstances of the family. Two incomes can provide financial stability, which is itself strongly associated with better outcomes for children. Children in such households frequently develop greater self-reliance, and working parents offer models of diligence and, particularly in the case of mothers, of broader gender roles. Research in several countries has found that daughters of employed mothers are more likely to be in paid work and to hold supervisory positions as adults.

Reducing the negative effects therefore requires attention to conditions rather than a return to single-earner households. Flexible working arrangements and adequate parental leave allow parents to be present at critical moments; affordable, high-quality early childhood education benefits children's development; and a fairer division of domestic labour prevents mothers from shouldering a double burden.

In conclusion, dual-earner families pose challenges, but these are largely challenges of policy and workplace culture. Where families are adequately supported, children can thrive with two working parents.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 320 words

Much of the anxiety about working parents rests on a nostalgic picture of a past that most families never actually lived: the mother at home, the father at work, the children supervised, fed and content. In reality, for most of history, both parents worked — in fields, workshops and markets — while children were raised partly by grandparents, siblings and neighbours. The dual-income household is less a break with tradition than a new version of it, and its effects on children depend far more on how it is organised than on the simple fact of parents' employment.

The costs are genuine. Modern jobs often demand long hours and long commutes, and a parent who returns home exhausted at eight in the evening has little left to give. Young children may spend much of their day in childcare of uneven quality; older ones may drift into long, unsupervised hours in front of screens. Children notice absence, even when they cannot name it.

But the benefits are genuine too. Two incomes can mean the difference between insecurity and stability, and financial stress in a household is itself harmful to children. Children of working parents often learn to organise themselves and to share responsibility for the home. And they absorb lessons from example: a daughter who watches her mother build a career grows up assuming that the world of work belongs to her as well — an assumption that research suggests shapes her own adult life.

What reduces the costs, then, is not a return to the past but a change in conditions: workplaces that allow flexibility and treat parental leave as normal, childcare that is affordable and good, and fathers who share the domestic load rather than merely helping with it.

Children do not need a parent at home every hour. They need to be cared for well, and to know that they matter. Two working parents can provide both — if society makes it possible.

## 54. Children and Household Chores

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Family

> **Question:** Some people think that children should be given household chores, such as cleaning and cooking, from a young age. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** household chores · responsibility · life skills · independence · age-appropriate tasks · contribution

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: chores teach children responsibility and useful skills for life.*

**🟡 B1:** Doing housework teaches children to be responsible. When a child must wash the dishes or clean their room every day, they learn that they are part of the family and must help. They also learn useful skills for the future, such as cooking and cleaning. For example, I started to cook rice when I was eight, and when I went to university, I could cook for myself.

**🔴 B2:** Giving children household chores teaches them responsibility from an early age. When children are expected to complete regular tasks, such as setting the table, washing dishes or looking after a pet, they learn that their actions affect others and that the family depends on everyone's contribution. These tasks also develop practical life skills that will be essential when they eventually live independently. Moreover, completing chores successfully can give children a sense of competence and self-confidence.

**🟣 C1:** Household chores are among the earliest and most concrete lessons in responsibility available to a child. Unlike schoolwork, which is performed largely for the child's own benefit, chores contribute to the welfare of others, teaching children that they are participants in a shared enterprise rather than mere recipients of care. Such tasks also cultivate practical competences — cooking, cleaning, managing time — that many young adults now lack when they leave home. Crucially, the sense of being genuinely needed can foster self-esteem more effectively than praise alone.

**⚫ C2:** A child who is never asked to do anything for the household learns, without anyone intending it, that the household exists to serve them. It is a quiet lesson, but a lasting one. Chores teach the opposite: that a home is a shared project, that clean plates and folded clothes do not appear by magic, and that one's own effort matters to other people. Few lessons in childhood are so ordinary, and few are so useful for the rest of a person's life.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 280 words

In many families today, parents do all the housework, and children only study and play. Some people think that children should help with housework, like cleaning and cooking, from a young age. I agree with this idea.

Firstly, household chores teach children to be responsible. When children must do a job every day, such as washing the dishes or cleaning their room, they learn that they are part of the family and must help. They also learn that other people depend on them.

Secondly, chores teach children important life skills. One day, children will leave home and live by themselves. If they can cook, clean and wash their clothes, they will find it easier to live independently. For example, I started to cook rice when I was eight, and when I went to university, I could cook my own meals. However, some of my friends could not cook at all.

Thirdly, housework helps parents. Many parents work long hours, and they are tired when they come home. When children help with housework, parents have more time to rest and to spend time with the family.

On the other hand, some people think that children should spend their time on studying and playing. They also worry that some jobs, like cooking with hot oil, can be dangerous. I think this is true, so parents should give children easy and safe jobs for their age. For example, young children can tidy their toys, and older children can help to cook.

In conclusion, I agree that children should do household chores from a young age. Chores teach responsibility and useful skills, but the jobs should be safe and suitable for the child's age.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 303 words

In some families, children are expected to help with housework from an early age, while in others, parents prefer their children to focus solely on their studies. I strongly agree that children should be given household chores, as long as these tasks are appropriate for their age.

The first reason is that chores develop a sense of responsibility. The primary driver is the affordability crisis in urban housing.
