## 61. Prison or Rehabilitation

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Crime

> **Question:** Some people believe that the best way to reduce crime is to give longer prison sentences. Others think there are better ways to reduce crime, such as education and rehabilitation. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** deterrent · reoffending · rehabilitation · vocational training · overcrowded prisons · community sentences

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: education and training in prison stop people from committing crimes again.*

**🟡 B1:** Prison alone often does not change criminals. When prisoners leave prison without skills or a job, many of them commit crimes again. But if prisoners learn to read, get job training or receive help with drug problems, they can start a new life. For example, Norway gives prisoners education and job training, and fewer people there return to prison than in many other countries.

**🔴 B2:** Rehabilitation programmes can be more effective than long sentences in preventing future crime. Many offenders lack education, job skills or treatment for addiction, and prison alone does little to address these problems. When prisoners receive education, vocational training and psychological support, they are more likely to find work and avoid reoffending after release. Norway, whose prison system focuses strongly on rehabilitation, is often cited as having one of the lowest reoffending rates in Europe. This suggests that preparing prisoners for life after release protects society more effectively than simply keeping them locked up.

**🟣 C1:** The most compelling argument for rehabilitation is that almost every prisoner will eventually be released. What matters for public safety, therefore, is not only how long offenders are confined but who they are when they return. Prisons that offer education, vocational training and treatment for addiction and mental illness address the conditions that often lead to offending in the first place. Norway, whose system is explicitly organised around preparation for release, is frequently cited as having one of the lowest reoffending rates in Europe — evidence that humane treatment and public protection need not be in conflict.

**⚫ C2:** Visitors to some Norwegian prisons are startled to find libraries, workshops and kitchens where inmates cook their own meals. It can look like indulgence. It is better understood as rehearsal. A prisoner who spends years practising the ordinary disciplines of adult life — working, budgeting, cooperating, taking responsibility — is far better prepared for freedom than one who spends those years learning only how to survive behind bars. Norway's comparatively low reoffending rate suggests that rehearsal works better than resentment.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 285 words

Crime is a serious problem in many countries. Some people think that the best way to reduce crime is to put criminals in prison for a longer time. Others believe that education and helping criminals to change is better. In this essay, I will discuss both views.

On the one hand, some people believe that longer prison sentences can reduce crime. When criminals are in prison, they cannot hurt other people, so society is safer. Also, if people know that they will go to prison for many years, they may be afraid to commit crimes. In addition, victims of crime often feel that long sentences are fair, because criminals should be punished for what they did.

On the other hand, other people think that there are better ways to reduce crime. Many criminals are poor or uneducated, or they have problems with drugs. If they only stay in prison, they do not learn anything useful, and they may meet other criminals. When they leave prison, many of them commit crimes again. However, if prisoners get education, job training and help with drug problems, they can find a job and start a new life. For example, Norway gives prisoners education and training, and far fewer of them return to prison than in many other countries.

In my opinion, prison is necessary for dangerous criminals, but education and training are more useful for most prisoners. Governments should also try to stop crime before it happens, for example by helping poor young people to study and find jobs.

In conclusion, longer prison sentences can protect society for a short time, but education and rehabilitation can reduce crime in the long term. I believe the second way is better.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 294 words

How societies should respond to crime is a question that divides public opinion. While some people argue that longer prison sentences are the most effective way to reduce crime, others believe that education and rehabilitation offer better long-term results. In my view, although prison is necessary for serious offenders, rehabilitation is generally more effective in reducing crime.

Those who support longer sentences make several arguments. Firstly, keeping criminals in prison prevents them from committing further offences while they are there, which protects the public. Secondly, harsh sentences may act as a deterrent, discouraging potential offenders from breaking the law. Thirdly, many people believe that long sentences deliver justice for victims, reflecting the seriousness of the harm that has been done.

On the other hand, critics point out that longer sentences often fail to reduce crime in the long term. Many prisoners come from disadvantaged backgrounds, with low levels of education, unemployment or addiction problems. If these issues are not addressed, prisoners are likely to reoffend after release, and time spent in prison may even strengthen their connections with other criminals. By contrast, education, vocational training and treatment for addiction can give prisoners the skills and stability they need to lead law-abiding lives. Norway, whose prisons focus strongly on rehabilitation, is often cited as having one of the lowest reoffending rates in Europe.

In my opinion, prison remains essential for dangerous and violent criminals, but for most offenders, particularly those who commit less serious crimes, rehabilitation programmes and community sentences are more effective. Furthermore, investing in education and opportunities for young people can prevent crime before it happens.

In conclusion, while longer sentences may satisfy the desire for punishment, I believe that rehabilitation and prevention are more likely to reduce crime in the long run.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 313 words

The belief that longer prison sentences are the most effective response to crime has considerable intuitive and political appeal. Yet the evidence suggests that its effectiveness is far more limited than its popularity implies. I would argue that while imprisonment remains necessary for the most serious offenders, rehabilitation and prevention offer a more effective route to reducing crime overall.

Advocates of longer sentences advance three principal arguments. Incapacitation ensures that offenders cannot harm the public while imprisoned; deterrence supposedly discourages others from offending; and proportionate punishment satisfies a widely held sense of justice, particularly among victims. The first and third of these have genuine force, especially for violent and dangerous offenders.

The deterrence argument, however, is considerably weaker. Research in criminology generally indicates that the certainty of being caught deters crime far more effectively than the severity of the punishment, since many offenders act impulsively or do not expect to be apprehended. Moreover, long sentences can prove counterproductive: prisons may function as schools for crime, severing offenders from families and legitimate employment while strengthening criminal networks. Since the vast majority of prisoners are eventually released, a system that returns them less employable and more alienated may increase rather than reduce future crime.

Rehabilitation addresses these problems directly. Education, vocational training and treatment for addiction and mental illness tackle the conditions that frequently underlie offending, and systems organised around these principles, such as Norway's, are often cited as achieving notably lower reoffending rates. Prevention, through investment in education, youth services and employment in deprived areas, can reduce crime before it occurs.

In conclusion, longer sentences have a role in protecting the public from dangerous offenders, but as a general strategy for reducing crime, they are less effective than rehabilitation and prevention. A rational justice system would reserve long imprisonment for those who genuinely require it and invest in changing the lives of the rest.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 310 words

When crime rises, the instinctive political response is to promise longer sentences. It is an understandable instinct — punishment satisfies a deep sense of justice — but it is a poor strategy, because it confuses the feeling of doing something with the fact of achieving it.

The case for long sentences deserves its due. Some offenders are genuinely dangerous, and for them, prison protects the public in the most direct way possible. Victims, too, have a legitimate claim that punishment should reflect the gravity of what they suffered. A justice system that ignored these considerations would lose public trust.

But as a general method of reducing crime, severity disappoints. Most offenders do not calculate sentences before acting; they act on impulse, desperation or the confident belief that they will not be caught. What deters is the likelihood of being caught, not the length of the sentence. Worse, prison can manufacture the very behaviour it aims to punish. A young person imprisoned for a minor offence may emerge with a criminal record that closes the doors of legitimate work, a network of more experienced criminals, and a bitterness that did not exist before.

And almost every prisoner comes home. That is the fact around which any sensible system must be built. Prisons that teach literacy, trades and self-control, and that treat addiction and mental illness, send back people who are less likely to offend again. Norway, whose prisons are designed around the day of release, is often cited as having one of the lowest reoffending rates in Europe.

The wiser course, then, is to reserve long sentences for the dangerous and to invest in rehabilitation for the rest — while spending more on prevention, in the schools and neighbourhoods where crime first takes root. The goal of a justice system is not to express our anger, however justified. It is to produce fewer victims.

## 62. Youth Crime

**Type:** Problem and solution · **Theme:** Crime

> **Question:** In some countries, crime committed by teenagers is increasing. What are the causes of this, and what can be done to solve the problem?

**Useful vocabulary:** juvenile crime · peer pressure · gangs · dropping out of school · youth centres · mentoring

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: youth centres and mentors give teenagers something better to do.*

**🟡 B1:** Youth centres and sports clubs can help to stop teenage crime. Many teenagers commit crimes because they are bored and have nothing to do after school. If there are free places where they can play football, learn music or use computers, they will spend their time in a safe and useful way. For example, in my town, a new youth centre opened, and now many teenagers go there after school instead of staying on the streets.

**🔴 B2:** One effective solution is to provide young people with positive activities and role models. Many teenagers become involved in crime because they are bored, lack guidance or are influenced by older friends and gangs. Youth centres, sports clubs and arts programmes can offer them safe places to spend their free time, while mentoring schemes connect them with adults who can provide advice and encouragement. Such programmes help young people to develop skills, confidence and a sense of belonging that makes them less likely to turn to crime.

**🟣 C1:** Among the most effective preventive measures are programmes that offer adolescents structure, purpose and trusted adult relationships. Much youth offending is less a product of calculated criminality than of boredom, peer influence and the absence of guidance, particularly among young people whose families are under strain. Youth clubs, sports teams and mentoring schemes address these conditions directly, providing supervised activity during the after-school hours when many offences occur and connecting teenagers with adults who can model alternative paths. Such programmes are considerably cheaper than detention, and they build capacities — confidence, discipline, belonging — that punishment alone cannot supply.

**⚫ C2:** A teenager's evening has to be spent somewhere. For some, it is spent at a training session, a music lesson or a homework club where an adult expects them to turn up. For others, it is spent on a street corner where the only people paying attention are those with something to sell. Much youth crime is decided not by character but by geography — by which of these places happens to be open. Funding youth programmes is sometimes treated as a luxury. It is closer to crime prevention in its purest form.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 276 words

In some countries, more and more teenagers are committing crimes, such as stealing, fighting and using drugs. This is a serious problem for society. In this essay, I will explain the main causes and suggest some solutions.

The first cause is family problems. Some teenagers do not get enough love and attention from their parents because their parents work long hours, or because the family is poor or broken. Without guidance, these teenagers may make bad choices.

The second cause is peer pressure. Teenagers want to be accepted by their friends. If their friends steal or join gangs, they may do the same things to feel part of the group.

The third cause is having nothing to do. Some teenagers leave school early and do not have a job. They are bored, so they spend time on the streets and may get involved in crime. In addition, some violent films and games may influence young people.

There are several solutions to this problem. Firstly, parents should spend more time with their children, talk to them and know who their friends are. Secondly, the government should build more youth centres, sports clubs and libraries, so that teenagers have safe places to go after school. Thirdly, schools should help students who have problems, for example with counsellors and extra classes, so they do not leave school early. Finally, teenagers who commit small crimes should get education and community work, not only punishment, so they can change their behaviour.

In conclusion, teenagers commit crimes because of family problems, peer pressure and boredom. If families, schools and the government work together, we can help young people to make better choices.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 301 words

Youth crime is a growing concern in a number of countries, as it harms communities and can have lasting consequences for the young people involved. This essay will explore the main causes of this problem and suggest several ways in which it could be tackled.

One of the most significant causes is family circumstances. Teenagers who grow up with little supervision, whether because their parents work long hours or because of family breakdown, may lack guidance and emotional support. Poverty can also play a role, as young people from disadvantaged backgrounds may be tempted by the quick rewards of theft or drug dealing. A second major cause is peer pressure. Adolescents have a strong desire to fit in, and those whose friends are involved in crime or gangs are more likely to follow their example. Finally, a lack of opportunities, such as leaving school early or being unable to find work, can leave young people bored and without a sense of direction.

To address these causes, action is needed from families, communities and governments. Parents can be supported through parenting programmes and flexible working arrangements that allow them to spend more time with their children. Local authorities should invest in youth centres, sports facilities and mentoring schemes, which provide positive activities and role models. Schools have an important role in identifying students at risk of dropping out and offering them additional support. Finally, the justice system should focus on rehabilitation for young offenders, using measures such as community service and education programmes rather than prison, which can expose teenagers to more experienced criminals.

In conclusion, youth crime is caused by a combination of family problems, peer pressure and a lack of opportunities. By supporting families, providing positive activities and focusing on rehabilitation, societies can help young people move away from crime.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 299 words

An increase in offending among teenagers tends to provoke calls for tougher punishment. Yet youth crime is rarely the product of calculated criminality; it more often reflects a combination of social disadvantage, developmental vulnerability and the absence of alternatives. Effective responses must therefore address these underlying causes rather than merely punishing their consequences.

The first cluster of causes concerns family and economic circumstances. Adolescents who experience poverty, neglect, domestic conflict or inadequate supervision are significantly more likely to become involved in offending, not because disadvantage determines behaviour, but because it removes many of the protective factors — stable routines, attentive adults, material security — that guide young people through adolescence. The second cluster is social and developmental. Adolescents are particularly sensitive to peer approval and less adept at weighing long-term consequences, which makes them especially susceptible to the influence of friends and, in some areas, to recruitment by gangs offering status and belonging. Exclusion from school and the absence of employment prospects compound these risks.

Solutions should be preventive wherever possible. Early support for struggling families, through parenting programmes and social services, can reduce the risk factors before offending begins. Schools can identify pupils at risk of exclusion and provide targeted support rather than removing them from education. Youth services — clubs, sports programmes and mentoring schemes — offer structured activity and trusted relationships, particularly during the after-school hours when many offences occur. For those who do offend, a youth justice system focused on rehabilitation, restorative justice and education is more likely to prevent reoffending than custody, which can entrench criminal identities and networks.

In short, youth crime is best understood as a symptom of unmet needs. A society that addresses those needs early will spend less on prisons later, and will lose fewer of its young people to lives defined by crime.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 307 words

No teenager is born a criminal, yet some become offenders with depressing predictability. Understanding why requires resisting two opposite temptations: to excuse young offenders entirely, as mere victims of circumstance, and to condemn them entirely, as though a sixteen-year-old's choices were made in the same way as a forty-year-old's.

The causes tend to cluster. Many young offenders come from homes marked by poverty, conflict or neglect — not because hardship makes crime inevitable, but because it strips away the ordinary protections of childhood: an adult who notices, a routine that holds, a sense that the future is worth planning for. Add the particular psychology of adolescence, when the approval of peers matters intensely and consequences feel distant, and one has a volatile mixture. Gangs understand this perfectly. They offer belonging to the lonely, status to the overlooked and excitement to the bored, and they recruit accordingly.

The solutions, therefore, must compete on the same terms. Youth clubs, sports teams and mentoring schemes are sometimes dismissed as soft options, but they offer precisely what gangs offer — identity, purpose, a place to be after school — without the violence. Schools that support struggling pupils rather than excluding them keep teenagers inside the one institution that can still redirect them. Families under strain can be helped before their difficulties become their children's.

When young people do offend, the response matters enormously. Imprisonment tends to confirm a teenager's sense of being an outsider and introduces them to more experienced criminals; restorative justice, community work and education are more likely to produce an adult who has left crime behind.

Youth crime, in the end, is a measure of what a society has failed to offer its young. Punishment has its place, but a society that invests only in punishment is choosing to pay later, and more, for what it declined to pay for earlier.

## 63. Compulsory Voting

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Government

> **Question:** In some countries, citizens are required by law to vote in elections. Some people think that voting should be compulsory everywhere. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** voter turnout · democratic legitimacy · civic duty · freedom of choice · blank ballot · civic education

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: when everyone votes, governments must listen to everyone.*

**🟡 B1:** When voting is compulsory, almost everyone votes, not only some groups. In many countries, older and richer people vote more than young and poor people. So politicians often make promises to these groups only. If everyone must vote, politicians must listen to all people. For example, in Australia, voting is compulsory, and about ninety per cent of people vote in elections.

**🔴 B2:** One strong argument for compulsory voting is that it makes election results more representative. In countries where voting is voluntary, turnout is often much lower among young people and those on low incomes, so politicians tend to focus on the concerns of older and wealthier voters. When voting is compulsory, as in Australia, turnout is usually very high, so governments are elected by a much broader section of society. This encourages politicians to consider the needs of all citizens rather than only those who are most likely to vote.

**🟣 C1:** Perhaps the most persuasive case for compulsory voting concerns equality of political voice. Where voting is voluntary, turnout tends to be skewed towards older, wealthier and better-educated citizens, and politicians rationally tailor their policies to those who are most likely to vote. Compulsion corrects this distortion, producing electorates that more accurately mirror the population as a whole. Australia, where voting has been compulsory for about a century and turnout routinely exceeds ninety per cent, demonstrates that the system can operate stably within a mature democracy.

**⚫ C2:** A ballot left uncast is not a neutral act; it quietly transfers influence to those who did vote. When the young, the poor and the overworked stay at home, their absence is not a protest that anyone hears but a vacancy that others fill. Compulsory voting refuses to let that vacancy form. It does not dictate anyone's choice. It simply insists that everyone be counted — which is, after all, the whole point of the exercise.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 278 words

In some countries, such as Australia, every adult must vote in elections, and people who do not vote must pay a small fine. Some people think that all countries should have this rule. I partly agree with this idea.

On the one hand, compulsory voting has some advantages. Firstly, when everyone votes, the government is chosen by all the people, not only by some groups. In many countries, older and richer people vote more often than young and poor people, so politicians listen more to them. If everyone must vote, politicians must think about everyone's problems. Secondly, compulsory voting teaches people that voting is an important duty, like paying taxes. It can also make people more interested in politics and the news.

On the other hand, compulsory voting also has some disadvantages. Some people think that voting is a right, not a duty, so people should be free to choose. Also, some people do not know anything about the candidates, and if they must vote, they may choose randomly. This is not good for democracy. In addition, some people do not like any of the candidates, so they do not want to vote for anyone.

In my opinion, compulsory voting can be a good idea, but people should have the choice to vote for "none of the candidates" or give an empty ballot. In this way, people must take part, but they are still free. The government should also teach people about politics at school so they can vote wisely.

In conclusion, I partly agree that voting should be compulsory. It can make governments represent everyone, but people should also have the freedom not to choose a candidate.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 295 words

In a small number of countries, such as Australia and Belgium, citizens are legally required to vote in elections. Some people believe that this system should be adopted everywhere. While I recognise the benefits of compulsory voting, I only partly agree with this view, as its success depends on how it is implemented.

The main argument in favour of compulsory voting is that it makes election results more representative. In many democracies with voluntary voting, turnout is considerably lower among young people, poorer citizens and those with less education. As a result, politicians tend to prioritise the interests of groups who vote regularly, such as older and wealthier citizens. Compulsory voting ensures that nearly the entire adult population participates, so governments have a stronger mandate and must consider the needs of everyone. In addition, it may encourage citizens to pay more attention to political issues.

However, there are also strong arguments against it. Many people believe that democracy includes the freedom not to participate, and that forcing people to vote violates this freedom. Furthermore, citizens who have no interest in politics may vote without considering the issues, choosing candidates randomly or simply based on their appearance, which could reduce the quality of democratic decisions. Enforcing the law can also be difficult and costly in countries with weak administrative systems.

In my view, compulsory voting works best when it is combined with protections for freedom of choice, such as the option to vote for "none of the above" or to submit a blank ballot. Civic education in schools is also essential, so that citizens are able to make informed decisions.

In conclusion, compulsory voting can strengthen democracy by making it more representative, but it should not be adopted everywhere without careful design and adequate civic education.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 317 words

Compulsory voting occupies an uneasy position in democratic theory. It appears to sacrifice individual freedom for collective benefit, yet its supporters argue that it strengthens democracy itself. Having weighed both positions, I am persuaded that compulsory voting has considerable merits, although I would stop short of arguing that it should be adopted universally.

The strongest case for compulsion concerns political equality. Where voting is voluntary, turnout is systematically lower among the young, the poor and the less educated, and rational politicians consequently direct their attention towards the older and more affluent citizens who reliably vote. The resulting policies can entrench existing inequalities. Compulsory voting corrects this skew, producing an electorate that mirrors the population as a whole. Australia, where voting has been compulsory for about a century and turnout routinely exceeds ninety per cent, demonstrates that the system can function stably within a long-established democracy. It may also reduce political polarisation, since parties no longer need to mobilise only their most committed supporters and must instead appeal to the broad middle of the electorate.

The objections, however, are substantial. Liberal democracies typically treat participation as a right rather than a duty, and compelling citizens to vote sits uneasily with that principle. There is also a concern that compulsion adds uninformed or indifferent voters to the electorate, potentially lowering the quality of collective decisions. Moreover, in countries with weak state capacity, enforcement could be selective or abused.

These objections can be partly addressed. What is compulsory in most such systems is attendance, not choice: citizens may submit blank or spoiled ballots, preserving their freedom not to endorse any candidate. Civic education can reduce the problem of uninformed voting.

In conclusion, compulsory voting offers a powerful remedy for unequal participation, and it deserves serious consideration. Whether it suits a particular country, however, depends on its institutions and political culture; it is a promising option rather than a universal prescription.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 330 words

Democracy rests on a promise of political equality: one person, one vote. In practice, the promise contains a loophole. A vote that is not cast has no weight, and those who cast the fewest votes — the young, the poor, the exhausted — end up with the least influence over the governments that shape their lives. Compulsory voting is an attempt to close that loophole. I think it deserves more support than it usually receives, though not as a universal rule.

The case for it is essentially about representation. Politicians are not fools; they direct their promises towards the people who turn up. Where turnout is voluntary and unequal, policy bends towards the concerns of older and wealthier citizens, whose votes can be counted on. Compulsion changes the audience. In Australia, where voting has been required for about a century, turnout regularly exceeds ninety per cent, and parties must compete for the whole electorate rather than simply energising their most loyal supporters.

The objections are serious. Freedom, many argue, must include the freedom to abstain; a right one is compelled to exercise begins to resemble an obligation. There is also the worry that forcing the uninterested to vote simply adds noise to the result. Yet both objections are weaker than they first appear. What is usually compulsory is attendance, not choice: a citizen may still leave the ballot blank. And the claim that non-voters are uninformed can cut both ways, since many abstain not out of ignorance but out of the belief that their vote will not matter — a belief that compulsion may help to overturn.

Still, institutions do not travel easily. In countries where the state is weak or distrusted, a law compelling citizens to vote could be enforced unfairly or used for intimidation.

Compulsory voting, then, is not a cure for every democracy. But in stable ones, it offers something valuable: a government chosen by the whole people, not merely by those with the time and confidence to show up.

## 64. Foreign Aid

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Government and global issues

> **Question:** Some people think that wealthy countries should give more financial aid to poorer countries. Others believe that aid does not solve the real problems and may even make them worse. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** foreign aid · dependency · corruption · humanitarian aid · development projects · fair trade

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: well-targeted aid, especially for health, can save millions of lives.*

**🟡 B1:** Foreign aid can save many lives. For example, money from rich countries has helped to pay for vaccines, clean water and medicine in poor countries. Because of this, millions of children did not die from diseases. Also, some countries that received aid in the past are now rich. South Korea received a lot of help after the war, and now it gives aid to other countries.

**🔴 B2:** Well-targeted aid can achieve remarkable results, particularly in health. International funding has supported vaccination campaigns, malaria prevention and clean water projects that have saved millions of lives. The eradication of smallpox, completed in 1980 through a global programme supported by wealthier nations, is one of the most striking examples. Moreover, aid can help countries to build the foundations for economic growth. South Korea, which received substantial assistance after the Korean War, is now a wealthy country that provides aid to others.

**🟣 C1:** Criticism of aid often overlooks its most conspicuous successes. Some of the most dramatic improvements in human welfare over the past half-century — the eradication of smallpox, steep declines in child mortality, the expansion of vaccination — owe a great deal to international assistance. Aid has been most effective where it has funded specific, measurable interventions, such as immunisation or malaria nets, rather than open-ended budget support. South Korea's transformation from aid recipient to aid donor, moreover, suggests that assistance can contribute to development when it is combined with capable domestic institutions.

**⚫ C2:** Aid succeeds when it resembles a good investment and fails when it resembles a guilty gesture. Money that buys vaccines, bed nets or school textbooks can be counted, delivered and evaluated; money handed to an unaccountable ministry cannot. The difference lies not in the donor's generosity but in its discipline. The lesson of decades of assistance is not that helping is futile. It is that helping well is harder than helping at all — and far more worthwhile.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 301 words

Every year, rich countries give money to poorer countries to help them develop. Some people think rich countries should give more money, but others believe that this aid does not solve the real problems. In this essay, I will discuss both views and give my opinion.

On the one hand, many people believe that rich countries should give more aid. Poor countries often do not have enough money for hospitals, schools, clean water and roads. Aid can help to build these things and save many lives. For example, money from other countries has helped to pay for vaccines, so millions of children did not die from diseases. Also, when there is a disaster, such as an earthquake or a flood, aid can help people very quickly. In addition, some countries that received aid in the past, such as South Korea, are now rich.

On the other hand, other people think that aid does not always help. Sometimes, the money goes to corrupt leaders instead of poor people. Also, if a country receives aid for many years, it may depend on this help and not try to develop its own economy. Some people also believe that aid sometimes helps the rich countries more, because the poor country must buy products or services from them.

In my opinion, rich countries should continue to give aid, but they should give it in a better way. They should check carefully how the money is used, and they should give it to useful projects, such as education, health and farming. Rich countries can also help by buying more products from poor countries.

In conclusion, foreign aid has some problems, but it can save lives and help countries to develop. I believe rich countries should give more aid, but they must make sure it is used well.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 310 words

The role of foreign aid in reducing global poverty is a subject of considerable debate. While some argue that wealthy nations have a responsibility to increase their financial assistance to poorer countries, others believe that aid is ineffective or even harmful. ## 69. Facial Recognition Cameras

**Type:** Advantages and disadvantages (outweigh) · **Theme:** Law, technology and privacy

> **Question:** Some cities use facial recognition cameras in public places to help the police find criminals. Do the advantages of this technology outweigh the disadvantages?

**Useful vocabulary:** facial recognition · surveillance · privacy · false match · civil liberties · independent oversight

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: the technology makes mistakes, and innocent people can pay the price.*

**🟡 B1:** Facial recognition cameras sometimes make mistakes. The computer can think that an innocent person is a criminal because they look similar. Then the police may stop or arrest the wrong person. This can be very frightening and unfair. For example, there have been news stories in other countries about people who were arrested because a camera made a mistake.

**🔴 B2:** A serious disadvantage of facial recognition technology is that it can make mistakes. Studies have found that some systems are less accurate at identifying people with darker skin, women and older people, which means that innocent members of these groups are more likely to be wrongly identified. In several cases in the United States, people have been arrested because a system incorrectly matched their face to a suspect. Such errors can have serious consequences for innocent people, including lost jobs and damaged reputations.

**🟣 C1:** The technology's fallibility poses a more immediate danger than is often acknowledged. Independent testing has found that the accuracy of some facial recognition systems varies across demographic groups, with higher error rates for certain ethnicities, women and older people. When such systems are used to identify suspects, these disparities translate directly into unequal risks of being wrongly stopped, searched or arrested — as documented cases of wrongful arrest in the United States have shown. A tool that distributes its errors unevenly cannot be regarded as neutral, however objective its algorithms may appear.

**⚫ C2:** A machine that is ninety-nine per cent accurate sounds reassuring until one does the arithmetic. Scan a million faces in a busy city, and even a small error rate produces a crowd of innocent people wrongly flagged as suspects — each of whom may be stopped, questioned or worse. And if the errors fall more heavily on some groups than on others, as studies of certain systems have found, then the technology does not merely make mistakes; it automates an injustice. Precision in the laboratory is not the same as fairness on the street.

## 70. Punishing Famous Lawbreakers

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Law

> **Question:** Some people think that famous people who break the law should receive stricter punishments than ordinary people, because they are role models. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** equality before the law · role model · deterrent · reputation · media attention · the rule of law

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: the law must treat everyone equally, whoever they are.*

**🟡 B1:** I believe the law must be the same for everyone. A judge should look at the crime, not at the person's job or how famous they are. If famous people get bigger punishments, it is not fair, just like it is not fair if they get smaller punishments. For example, if a singer and a taxi driver both drive after drinking, they should get the same punishment.

**🔴 B2:** The principle of equality before the law is one of the foundations of a fair justice system. Punishments should be based on the seriousness of the crime and the circumstances of the offence, not on the identity or status of the offender. If famous people received harsher sentences simply because they are well known, this would undermine the idea that everyone is treated equally. It would also set a dangerous precedent, since it could encourage courts to treat other groups differently according to their social position.

**🟣 C1:** Equality before the law is not merely a procedural detail but the principle that distinguishes justice from arbitrary power. Sentences should reflect the gravity of the offence and the culpability of the offender; the defendant's celebrity is irrelevant to both. To impose harsher punishment on the famous would be to punish status rather than conduct, and once courts accept that social position may legitimately affect sentencing, there is no principled reason why the same logic could not be used to favour the powerful or disadvantage the unpopular.

**⚫ C2:** There is a quiet irony in the demand that celebrities be punished more harshly. In much of the world, the real complaint about the famous is that they are punished too little — that money, lawyers and connections allow them to slip past rules that bind everyone else. The remedy for that injustice is not a new injustice pointed in the other direction. It is the old, unglamorous principle that the law should not notice who you are. Applied honestly, that principle is demanding enough.
