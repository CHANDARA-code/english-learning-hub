## 71. Traffic Congestion

**Type:** Problem and solution · **Theme:** Cities and transport

> **Question:** Traffic congestion is becoming a serious problem in many cities. What are the causes of this problem, and what measures could be taken to reduce it?

**Useful vocabulary:** traffic jams · private vehicles · public transport · congestion charge · urban planning · rush hour

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: charging drivers to use busy roads reduces traffic.*

**🟡 B1:** One way to reduce traffic jams is to make drivers pay to enter the city centre at busy times. Then some people will take the bus, ride a bicycle or travel at another time. For example, Singapore and London charge drivers money to enter busy areas, and traffic there has become better. The government can also use this money to improve buses and trains.

**🔴 B2:** One proven method of reducing congestion is road pricing, in which drivers pay a fee to enter busy areas at peak times. Singapore introduced electronic road pricing decades ago, and cities such as London and Stockholm have also adopted congestion charges. By making driving into the city centre more expensive, these schemes encourage people to use public transport, share cars or travel at less busy times. Moreover, the revenue can be invested in better buses and trains, which makes alternatives to driving even more attractive.

**🟣 C1:** Road space in a city centre is a scarce resource, yet it is usually given away free, with the predictable result that it is overused. Congestion pricing corrects this by charging drivers for using roads at the busiest times. The approach has a strong record: Singapore has operated electronic road pricing since 1998, and London and Stockholm have both reported substantial reductions in traffic following the introduction of charges. Its effectiveness, however, depends on the availability of genuine alternatives, which is why the revenue should be reinvested in public transport rather than treated as general income.

**⚫ C2:** A single lane of road can move a few thousand people an hour in cars, or many times that number in buses and trains. Seen this way, a traffic jam is not really a case of too many people trying to move; it is too many people trying to move in the least efficient way possible. Road pricing, used in Singapore for decades, simply asks drivers to pay for the space their choice consumes. Many discover that the journey was not worth the price — and the road, freed of them, begins to flow.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 267 words

In many big cities, such as Phnom Penh, Bangkok and Jakarta, traffic jams are a big problem. People often spend hours in traffic every day. In this essay, I will explain the causes of this problem and suggest some solutions.

The first cause is that there are too many private vehicles. As people become richer, more of them buy cars and motorbikes. Many cars carry only one person, so they use a lot of space on the roads.

The second cause is poor public transport. In some cities, buses are slow, uncomfortable or not available in many areas, and there are no trains. So people prefer to use their own vehicles.

The third cause is bad planning. Many cities grow very fast, but the roads do not grow at the same speed. Also, many offices, schools and shopping centres are in the city centre, so everyone travels to the same place at the same time.

There are several solutions to this problem. Firstly, the government should improve public transport. It can build more bus lines, trains and a metro system, and make tickets cheap. Secondly, drivers can pay money to enter the city centre at busy times, as in Singapore and London. Thirdly, companies can let workers start at different times or work from home some days, so fewer people travel at the same time. Finally, cities should build safe paths for walking and cycling.

In conclusion, traffic jams are caused by too many private vehicles, poor public transport and bad planning. If the government improves public transport and makes smart rules, traffic can become much better.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 286 words

Traffic congestion has become a daily reality in many of the world's cities, wasting time, harming the economy and polluting the air. This essay will examine the main causes of this problem and propose several measures to reduce it.

The most important cause is the rapid increase in private vehicle ownership. As incomes rise, more people can afford cars and motorbikes, and many cities have not been designed to accommodate so many vehicles. The problem is made worse by inadequate public transport: where buses are slow, unreliable or overcrowded and rail networks are limited, people have little choice but to drive. Poor urban planning also plays a role. When offices, schools and shopping centres are concentrated in the city centre while housing spreads to the suburbs, large numbers of people must travel in the same direction at the same time.

Several measures could help to tackle this problem. The most effective long-term solution is to invest in fast, affordable and reliable public transport, such as metro systems and bus rapid transit lanes, which can carry far more people than private cars using the same road space. In addition, road pricing schemes, like those in Singapore, London and Stockholm, can discourage unnecessary car journeys by charging drivers to enter congested areas at peak times. Better urban planning, which places homes, jobs and services closer together, would reduce the need for long journeys. Finally, encouraging flexible working hours and remote work can spread demand more evenly throughout the day.

In conclusion, traffic congestion is caused mainly by the growth of private vehicles, weak public transport and poor planning. A combination of better public transport, road pricing and smarter urban design offers the best chance of keeping cities moving.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 306 words

Congestion has become the defining frustration of urban life in many rapidly growing cities, imposing substantial costs in lost time, fuel, productivity and public health. Its causes are well understood, and so, increasingly, are the solutions — although these often require political courage as much as investment.

The fundamental cause is an imbalance between the demand for road space and its supply. Rising incomes have fuelled rapid growth in car and motorcycle ownership, while cities have expanded outwards faster than transport infrastructure has developed. Where public transport is slow, unreliable or absent, private vehicles become the default choice. Urban form compounds the problem: sprawling residential suburbs combined with concentrated central business districts generate predictable tidal flows of traffic at peak hours. Crucially, road space is usually provided free at the point of use, so drivers have no incentive to consider the congestion their journeys impose on others.

Efforts to solve congestion simply by building more roads have a poor record, since new capacity tends to generate additional traffic — a phenomenon known as induced demand. More effective strategies manage demand while expanding alternatives. High-capacity public transport, such as metro systems and bus rapid transit, moves far more people per lane than private vehicles. Road pricing, pioneered by Singapore and adopted in London and Stockholm, reduces traffic by charging for peak-time use of the busiest roads, and its revenue can fund better public transport. In the longer term, planning that integrates housing, employment and services — creating denser, mixed-use neighbourhoods — reduces the need to travel at all.

In short, congestion results from treating a scarce resource as though it were unlimited. Cities that price road space sensibly, invest in public transport and plan compact neighbourhoods can keep moving; those that rely on building ever more roads are likely to find themselves stuck in the same traffic, only on wider roads.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 301 words

Every city that has tried to build its way out of traffic congestion has discovered the same frustrating truth: new roads fill up. Widen a highway, and within a few years it is as crowded as before, because the extra space invites journeys that were previously not worth making. Congestion, in other words, is not simply a shortage of roads. It is what happens when something valuable is given away free.

The causes are easy to list. Rising prosperity has put cars and motorbikes within reach of millions who once walked, cycled or took the bus. Cities have sprawled outwards faster than railways and bus networks could follow, so that for many people driving is not a preference but the only realistic option. And urban planning has often concentrated jobs in the centre and homes on the edge, so that each morning a tide of vehicles flows inwards and each evening it flows out again.

The solutions work by changing the economics of the journey. The first is to offer something better: metros and dedicated bus lanes that move people quickly, cheaply and reliably, so that leaving the car at home feels like an advantage rather than a sacrifice. The second is to stop giving road space away. Singapore has charged drivers for entering its busiest roads since 1998; London and Stockholm followed, and traffic fell. The money raised can pay for the better buses and trains that make the charge fair. The third is slower but deeper: planning neighbourhoods where homes, schools, shops and workplaces are close enough that many journeys need never be made at all.

None of these measures is technically difficult. What they require is the political courage to tell drivers that the road is not free — and the imagination to offer them something better in return.

## 72. High-Rise Housing

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Cities and housing

> **Question:** Some people believe that building tall apartment blocks is the best way to solve housing shortages in cities. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** high-rise apartments · population density · urban sprawl · green space · sense of community · maintenance

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: building upwards saves land and stops cities from spreading outwards.*

**🟡 B1:** Tall apartment buildings can give homes to many people on a small piece of land. If cities only build small houses, they must use a lot of land, and the city becomes bigger and bigger. Then people must travel very far to work, and farms and forests are destroyed. For example, in Singapore, most people live in tall apartment buildings, so the city can have many homes and still keep parks.

**🔴 B2:** One important advantage of high-rise housing is that it uses land efficiently. By building upwards rather than outwards, cities can house large numbers of people without destroying farmland and forests on their edges. This also reduces urban sprawl, which forces residents to travel long distances and makes public transport less efficient. Singapore, where the majority of residents live in high-rise public housing, has managed to accommodate a large population on a small island while still preserving parks and green spaces.

**🟣 C1:** The strongest argument for high-rise housing is spatial. Cities that grow outwards consume farmland and natural habitats, lengthen commutes and make public transport uneconomic, since low-density suburbs cannot generate the passenger numbers that efficient bus and rail services require. Building upwards concentrates population, allowing more people to live within walking distance of transport, jobs and services. Singapore, where most residents live in high-rise public housing, demonstrates that density need not come at the expense of green space when it is carefully planned.

**⚫ C2:** Land is the one thing a city cannot manufacture. Every family housed on the city's edge in a detached house consumes fields, lengthens a commute and adds a car to the morning traffic. Building upwards is, at its simplest, a refusal to keep swallowing the countryside. Singapore houses most of its population in tall blocks on an island smaller than many metropolitan areas and still keeps room for parks. Density, done well, is not a sacrifice of quality of life. It is often the condition for it.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 277 words

Many cities have a housing problem, because more and more people move there, but there are not enough homes. Some people think that building tall apartment blocks is the best solution. I partly agree with this idea.

On the one hand, tall buildings have many advantages. Firstly, they can give homes to many people on a small piece of land. Cities do not have much free land, so building up is better than building out. Secondly, if people live close together, the city does not need to grow bigger into farms and forests. Thirdly, it is easier to build public transport, shops and schools near tall buildings, because many people live in the same area. For example, in Singapore, most people live in tall apartment buildings, and the city is clean and well organised.

On the other hand, tall buildings also have some problems. Some people feel lonely in tall buildings, because they do not know their neighbours. Also, families with children may not have enough space to play. In addition, if the buildings are not built well, they can be dangerous, for example in a fire or an earthquake. Tall buildings also need lifts and good management, which cost a lot of money.

In my opinion, tall apartment blocks are a good solution, but they are not the only solution. Cities should also build smaller apartment buildings, keep parks and playgrounds, and make sure buildings are safe and well managed.

In conclusion, I partly agree that tall apartment blocks can help to solve housing shortages. However, they must be well designed, with parks and community spaces, so that people can have a good quality of life.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 307 words

As urban populations continue to grow, many cities face severe shortages of affordable housing. Some people argue that the construction of high-rise apartment blocks is the best solution. While I agree that building upwards is an essential part of the answer, I do not believe it is the only or always the best one.

There are strong arguments in favour of high-rise housing. The most obvious is that it allows large numbers of people to be housed on a limited amount of land, which is particularly important in crowded cities where land is scarce and expensive. By increasing density, cities can avoid spreading outwards into surrounding farmland and forests, which reduces the need for long commutes. Higher density also makes public transport, shops and services more viable, since many residents live within a short distance of them. Singapore, where most people live in high-rise public housing, shows how this approach can provide homes for a large population on limited land.

However, high-rise housing also has drawbacks. Poorly designed tower blocks can feel isolating, with residents rarely meeting their neighbours, and they may lack space for children to play. They require high standards of construction, fire safety and maintenance, which can be expensive, especially for low-income residents. Moreover, building very tall towers is not the only way to achieve high density; medium-rise buildings of six to ten storeys can also house many people while creating a more human scale and a stronger sense of community.

In my view, cities should increase housing density, but through a mix of building types, combined with parks, community facilities and good public transport. The quality of design and management matters as much as height.

In conclusion, tall apartment blocks can make an important contribution to solving housing shortages, but they should be part of a wider strategy rather than the only solution.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 309 words

The argument for building upwards to relieve urban housing shortages is, in many respects, compelling. Yet the claim that high-rise towers are the best solution conflates density, which cities undoubtedly need, with height, which is only one way of achieving it. I would therefore agree with the proposal only in part.

The case for density is strong. Cities that expand horizontally consume farmland, fragment natural habitats and generate car-dependent suburbs, lengthening commutes and undermining the economics of public transport. Concentrating population, by contrast, enables efficient transport, supports local shops and services, and reduces per-capita energy use. Singapore, where the majority of residents live in high-rise public housing, demonstrates that a dense city can provide decent homes for a large population while preserving extensive green space.

Height, however, is not synonymous with density, nor is it always the most effective means of achieving it. Very tall towers are expensive to build and maintain, require sophisticated fire safety systems and lifts, and can leave residents dependent on management that may fail them — sometimes with tragic consequences. Poorly designed estates of isolated towers, surrounded by empty open space, have in several countries become associated with social isolation and neglect. Many European cities achieve comparable densities through medium-rise buildings of six to eight storeys arranged along streets, which foster neighbourly contact and a human scale.

What ultimately determines the success of any housing strategy is not the height of buildings but the quality of their design, the provision of public space and services, and the long-term commitment to maintenance. Equally important is affordability: towers of luxury apartments may increase housing supply without helping those most in need.

In conclusion, high-density housing is essential for growing cities, but high-rise towers are only one tool among several. The best solution combines varied building forms with good design, public amenities and a firm focus on affordability.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 320 words

Cities have two directions in which to grow: outwards and upwards. Outward growth consumes farmland, lengthens commutes and fills the roads with cars; upward growth concentrates people where transport, jobs and services can reach them. On that basis, the case for building high is strong. But the claim that tall apartment blocks are the best solution to housing shortages mistakes a means for an end.

The end is density — enough homes, close enough together, to make urban life efficient and affordable. Singapore offers the most celebrated demonstration: the great majority of its citizens live in high-rise public housing, and the city remains green, orderly and largely free of the slums that have accompanied rapid urbanisation elsewhere. Density of this kind allows metro lines to run full, neighbourhood shops to survive, and the surrounding countryside to remain countryside.

Yet height is only one route to density, and not always the wisest. Towers are expensive to build and even more expensive to maintain; they depend on lifts that must work, fire systems that must function and managers who must care. When any of these fails, residents on the twentieth floor discover how fragile vertical life can be. Some of the twentieth century's grandest housing estates — isolated towers surrounded by windswept, unused space — became symbols of neglect within a generation. Meanwhile, many European cities achieve comparable densities in buildings of six or eight storeys, arranged along streets where neighbours meet and children can be seen from the kitchen window.

The deeper point is that housing shortages are solved less by architecture than by commitment: to building enough homes, keeping them affordable and maintaining them well. A tower of luxury flats adds to the skyline without housing the people who need it most.

I agree, then, that cities must build densely — but not that they must always build tall. The question is not how high we can build, but how well we can live.

## 73. Unaffordable Housing

**Type:** Problem and solution · **Theme:** Cities and housing

> **Question:** In many cities, house prices and rents have risen so much that many young people cannot afford a home. What are the causes of this problem, and what can be done to solve it?

**Useful vocabulary:** housing affordability · property speculation · housing supply · social housing · planning rules · empty homes

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: governments can provide good homes at fair rents, as Vienna does.*

**🟡 B1:** The government can build good, cheap apartments for people with normal incomes. If there are many cheap homes, young people do not need to pay very high rent to private owners. For example, in Vienna, the capital of Austria, many people live in good apartments owned by the city, and the rent is not too high. This can help young people to save money and start a family.

**🔴 B2:** One effective long-term solution is large-scale investment in social and affordable housing. When governments or non-profit organisations build and manage a significant proportion of a city's homes, they can offer rents based on residents' incomes rather than on market prices. Vienna is often cited as a successful example: a large share of its residents live in high-quality municipal or subsidised housing, which helps to keep the overall cost of living in the city relatively affordable. Such housing also provides stability, since tenants are less vulnerable to sudden rent increases.

**🟣 C1:** Among the most durable remedies for unaffordable housing is a substantial, permanent stock of social housing. Where a large share of homes is owned by public or non-profit bodies, rents can be set according to cost and income rather than speculative market value, and the presence of this sector exerts downward pressure on private rents as well. Vienna, where a large proportion of residents live in municipal or subsidised housing, is widely regarded as one of Europe's more affordable major cities despite its high quality of life. The lesson is that affordability depends less on occasional subsidies than on long-term public commitment.

**⚫ C2:** Most cities treat housing as a market that occasionally needs help. Vienna treats it more like infrastructure — like water or roads — that the city has a duty to provide. For about a century it has built and maintained homes rented at moderate prices to a wide cross-section of its residents, not only the poor. The result is a capital where a teacher and a lawyer may live in the same building, and where young people can leave home without surrendering most of their salary to a landlord. Affordability, it turns out, is a policy choice.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 297 words

In many big cities, house prices and rents are very high. Many young people cannot buy a home, and some of them spend most of their salary on rent. In this essay, I will explain the causes of this problem and suggest some solutions.

The first cause is that many people move to big cities to find jobs and study. When many people want homes, but there are not enough homes, the prices go up.

The second cause is that some rich people and companies buy houses and apartments as an investment, not to live in them. They wait for the prices to go up and then sell them. Some apartments are empty for a long time, so there are fewer homes for normal people.

The third cause is that salaries do not increase as fast as house prices. So young people need many years to save enough money to buy a home.

There are several solutions to this problem. Firstly, the government should build more cheap apartments for young people and families with normal incomes. For example, in Vienna, many people live in good apartments owned by the city, and the rent is not too high. Secondly, the government can put a higher tax on empty homes and on people who own many houses, so they will sell or rent them. Thirdly, the government can help young people to buy their first home, for example with low-interest loans. Finally, cities should build good public transport, so people can live in cheaper areas outside the city centre.

In conclusion, housing is very expensive because many people move to cities, some people buy homes as investments and salaries are low. If the government builds cheap homes and makes fair rules, young people can afford a place to live.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 288 words

In many major cities around the world, housing has become so expensive that young people, even those with steady jobs, struggle to afford a home. This essay will examine the main causes of this problem and suggest measures to address it.

The most fundamental cause is a shortage of housing supply relative to demand. Growing populations and the concentration of jobs in large cities mean that more people are competing for a limited number of homes. In many places, restrictive planning rules and slow approval processes make it difficult to build new housing quickly. A second cause is the treatment of property as an investment. Wealthy individuals, companies and foreign investors often buy homes as assets rather than places to live, pushing up prices, and some properties are left empty. Finally, low interest rates in recent years allowed buyers to borrow more, which further increased prices, while wages for young workers rose much more slowly.

Several measures could make housing more affordable. Firstly, governments should increase the supply of homes by simplifying planning rules and encouraging higher-density construction near public transport. Secondly, investment in social and affordable housing, as in Vienna, where a large share of residents live in municipal or subsidised apartments, can provide stable, reasonably priced homes. Thirdly, taxes on empty properties and on owners of multiple homes would discourage speculation and bring more homes onto the market. Finally, improved public transport would allow people to live further from city centres, where housing is cheaper, without facing extremely long commutes.

In conclusion, unaffordable housing is caused by insufficient supply, speculative investment and slow wage growth. By building more homes, expanding social housing and discouraging speculation, governments can help young people to secure a place to live.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 305 words

Housing affordability has become one of the most pressing social issues in many of the world's major cities, where a generation of young people faces rents that consume much of their income and a home ownership ladder whose lowest rungs seem to have been removed. The causes are multiple and mutually reinforcing, and effective solutions must address several of them simultaneously.

The first and most fundamental cause is insufficient supply. Employment and opportunity have become increasingly concentrated in large cities, driving demand, while restrictive zoning, lengthy approval processes and local opposition to new development have constrained construction. The second cause is the financialisation of housing. Property has increasingly been treated as an investment asset, attracting domestic and international capital seeking secure returns; homes bought as investments, sometimes left empty, compete with those bought as places to live. Prolonged periods of low interest rates, moreover, inflated asset prices while wages — particularly for younger workers — stagnated.

Solutions should therefore combine increased supply with measures to restore housing's primary function as shelter. Reforming planning systems to permit denser development, particularly near public transport, would allow supply to respond more readily to demand. A substantial, permanent stock of social and non-profit housing, of the kind that has helped Vienna remain comparatively affordable, provides secure homes independent of market fluctuations and moderates private rents. Taxes on vacant properties and on speculative multiple ownership can discourage the use of housing as a passive investment, while carefully designed protections for tenants can prevent sudden rent increases from displacing established residents.

In short, unaffordable housing is not an inevitable feature of successful cities but the result of policy choices about how much to build and whom housing should serve. Cities that treat homes primarily as places to live, rather than as financial assets, are far more likely to remain affordable for the young.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 304 words

A home has become, in many of the world's great cities, two incompatible things at once: a place to live and a place to store wealth. As long as it is both, the interests of those who need homes and those who own them will pull in opposite directions — and in recent decades the owners have been winning.

The causes are not mysterious. Jobs, universities and opportunities have concentrated in large cities, and people have followed them. Supply has failed to keep pace, often because planning rules, slow approvals and the objections of existing residents make building difficult. Meanwhile, property has become a favoured investment for domestic savers and international capital alike. Years of cheap borrowing inflated prices further, while the wages of young workers barely moved. The result is a generation that may earn good salaries and still find that a modest flat is beyond reach.

The remedies must address both identities of the home. As a place to live, housing needs to be built in far greater quantities, which means reforming the rules that make construction slow and allowing denser development near public transport. As a store of wealth, it needs to be made less attractive: taxing empty properties and multiple ownership would push homes back into use and cool the speculation that inflates prices.

And some homes should be removed from the market altogether. In Vienna, a large share of residents rent homes owned by the city or by non-profit associations at moderate prices, and the city remains one of the more affordable major capitals in Europe despite its high quality of life.

Unaffordable housing, then, is not a natural disaster. It is the outcome of decisions about how much to build and whom housing is for. Cities that decide homes are primarily for living in can make them affordable again.

## 74. Free Public Transport

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Transport

> **Question:** Some people think that public transport should be free for everyone. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** fare-free transport · passenger numbers · service quality · frequency · public funding · low-income commuters

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: fast and frequent service matters more than a free ticket.*

**🟡 B1:** Free buses are good, but people also want buses that are fast, clean and on time. If buses are free but they come only once an hour and are very crowded, many people will still use their cars or motorbikes. So I think the government should first make public transport better, and then make it cheaper. For example, many people in my city do not take the bus, not because it is expensive, but because it is slow.

**🔴 B2:** Although free public transport sounds attractive, research suggests that the quality of service may matter more than the price. People are more likely to leave their cars at home when buses and trains are frequent, reliable, fast and comfortable. If a system is free but slow and overcrowded, many drivers will continue to use their cars, and the main new passengers may be people who previously walked or cycled. Therefore, governments should prioritise investment in better services before removing fares completely.

**🟣 C1:** The evidence from cities that have abolished fares suggests a counterintuitive conclusion: price is often less decisive than quality. Experiments such as Tallinn's fare-free system, introduced for residents in 2013, increased ridership but appear to have drawn many new passengers from walking and cycling rather than from cars. Motorists, it seems, are more responsive to speed, frequency and reliability than to the cost of a ticket. Removing fares while neglecting service quality may therefore consume substantial public funds without delivering the reduction in car use that is usually its principal aim.

**⚫ C2:** Ask drivers why they do not take the bus, and they rarely mention the fare. They talk about waiting in the rain, standing in crowded aisles, and arriving late because the timetable is fiction. A ticket price is a small, predictable cost; unreliability is a large, unpredictable one. Any city that wants to lure people out of their cars must therefore compete first on the things drivers actually value — speed, frequency and certainty. Only then does a lower fare become the finishing touch rather than a wasted gesture.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 268 words

In some cities, the government has made buses and trains free for everyone. Some people think that all cities should do this. I partly agree with this idea.

On the one hand, free public transport has many advantages. Firstly, it helps poor people. Many workers and students do not have much money, and transport costs are a big part of their spending. If buses are free, they can save money for food, rent or school. Secondly, if public transport is free, more people will use it instead of their cars and motorbikes. In my opinion, public transport should be cheap, and it should be free for some groups, such as students, old people and poor people. However, the government should first make buses and trains faster, cleaner and more frequent. When the service is good, many people will use it even if they have to pay a small amount.

In conclusion, I partly agree that public transport should be free. It can help poor people and reduce traffic, but it is more important to make public transport better first.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 298 words

The idea of making public transport free for everyone has attracted growing attention, and a few places, such as Luxembourg, have already done so. While free public transport offers some important benefits, I only partly agree that it should be introduced everywhere, as better services may be more important than lower prices.

Supporters of free public transport highlight its social and environmental benefits. For low-income households, transport costs can take up a significant proportion of their budget, so removing fares would give them greater access to jobs, education and services. ### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels

The claim that a building's appearance matters less than its function contains an important truth, but I would argue that it underestimates the public, enduring and psychological significance of architecture.

The primacy of function is difficult to dispute.
