## 11. News from Social Media

**Type:** Two-part question · **Theme:** Technology and media

> **Question:** Nowadays, many people get most of their news from social media rather than from newspapers or television. Why is this happening? Is it a positive or negative development?

**Useful vocabulary:** news feed · algorithm · misinformation · breaking news · traditional media · filter bubble

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: news on social media is often false or one-sided.*

**🟡 B1:** One problem with news on social media is that it is not always true. Anyone can write a post, and many people share it without checking. Also, social media apps often show us the same kind of news again and again, so we only see one side of a story. For example, my uncle believed a false story about a new disease because many of his friends shared it. This can be dangerous.

**🔴 B2:** A major disadvantage of getting news from social media is that users are often exposed to a narrow and unreliable range of information. Platforms use algorithms that show people content similar to what they have already liked, which means that users may rarely encounter opinions different from their own. Furthermore, false stories can spread quickly because posts are shared before anyone checks whether they are accurate. As a result, people may feel well informed while actually having a distorted picture of events.

**🟣 C1:** The most troubling consequence of the shift towards social media news is not simply the spread of falsehoods but the fragmentation of shared reality. Because platforms prioritise engagement, their algorithms tend to promote content that confirms users' existing beliefs or provokes strong emotion, gradually enclosing people within so-called filter bubbles. Citizens who consume entirely different versions of events find it increasingly difficult to agree even on basic facts, let alone on solutions to common problems. The danger lies less in any single false story than in the slow erosion of the common ground on which democratic debate depends.

**⚫ C2:** A lie, the old saying goes, can travel halfway around the world while the truth is still putting on its shoes. Social media has given the lie a jet engine. Platforms reward whatever provokes a reaction, and falsehood, unconstrained by facts, can always be more shocking than the truth. By the time a correction appears, the original story has been shared, believed and forgotten — leaving behind not a memory of the claim, but the vague conviction it created.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 272 words

Today, many people read the news on Facebook, TikTok or other social media apps instead of reading newspapers or watching the news on TV. There are several reasons for this change, and I think it has both good and bad sides.

There are three main reasons why people use social media for news. Firstly, it is fast. When something important happens, people can read about it on their phones in a few minutes. Secondly, it is free, but many newspapers cost money. Thirdly, it is easy, because people check their phones many times every day, so they see the news without looking for it.

This change has some good points. People can learn about events very quickly, and they can also see videos and photos from ordinary people who were there. For example, during floods, people often share useful information about dangerous roads on social media. Also, people can comment and share their own opinions.

However, I think this development is mostly negative. The biggest problem is that a lot of news on social media is not true. Anyone can write a post, and many people share it without checking it. Another problem is that apps show us the same kind of stories again and again, so we only see one side of the story. In addition, many posts are very short, so people do not understand the whole situation.

In conclusion, people use social media for news because it is fast, free and easy. However, I believe this is a negative development because the news is often false or one-sided. People should also read news from reliable newspapers and TV channels.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 283 words

Over the past decade, social media platforms have become the main source of news for a large proportion of the population, especially young people. This essay will explain the reasons for this shift and argue that, despite some benefits, it is largely a negative development.

The popularity of social media as a news source can be explained by convenience and cost. Most people carry a smartphone at all times and check their social media accounts many times a day, so news reaches them without any effort. Unlike many newspapers, this information is also free. In addition, social media offers speed: eyewitnesses can post photos and videos of events within minutes, long before traditional journalists arrive.

These features do bring some advantages. Breaking news, such as warnings about natural disasters, can spread rapidly and potentially save lives. Social media also gives a voice to ordinary people and to communities whose stories are often ignored by mainstream media.

However, the disadvantages are more serious. Firstly, information on social media is rarely checked before it is published, so false or misleading stories can reach millions of users before they are corrected. Secondly, platforms use algorithms that show people content similar to what they have already liked. As a result, users may become trapped in "filter bubbles", seeing only opinions that confirm their existing beliefs. Finally, news on social media is often reduced to short headlines or videos, which encourages quick reactions rather than careful understanding.

In conclusion, people turn to social media for news because it is fast, free and convenient. Nevertheless, I believe the trend is mainly negative, since it increases the spread of misinformation and divides society into groups that rarely hear opposing views.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 279 words

The migration of news consumption from newspapers and television to social media platforms is one of the defining media shifts of the past two decades. It is driven largely by convenience, but its consequences extend far beyond it, and on balance I consider it a negative development — though not an irreversible one.

The reasons are not difficult to identify. Smartphones have made social media a constant presence in daily life, so news now arrives unbidden rather than having to be sought out. It is free at the point of use, it is instantaneous, and it is personalised. For younger audiences in particular, the idea of paying for a newspaper or waiting for an evening bulletin increasingly seems outdated.

It would be unfair to deny the benefits. Social media has democratised news production, allowing eyewitnesses to document events that traditional outlets might have overlooked, and enabling emergency information to spread with unprecedented speed.

Yet these gains are outweighed by structural weaknesses. Platforms are designed to maximise engagement rather than accuracy, and their algorithms consequently reward content that is sensational, emotive or divisive. Falsehoods, which are often more novel and dramatic than the truth, can circulate widely before any correction appears. More insidiously, personalised feeds tend to confirm users' existing views, gradually eroding the shared body of facts on which public debate depends.

The implication is not that social media news should be abandoned, which is neither possible nor desirable, but that its weaknesses must be actively countered — through media literacy education, greater transparency about how algorithms work, and continued support for professional journalism. Without such measures, a population that is constantly informed may paradoxically become less well informed than ever.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 283 words

For most of the twentieth century, the news arrived at fixed times, in fixed places: the morning paper, the evening bulletin. Today it arrives everywhere, all the time, sorted by software for each of us individually. The reasons for the change are obvious; its consequences are not, and on balance they are troubling.

People turn to social media for news because it asks nothing of them. It is free, it is instant, and it is already in their pocket, woven into the same feed as their friends' photographs and their family's messages. Seeking out a newspaper now feels like effort; being informed by a feed feels like breathing.

The gains are real, and it would be dishonest to dismiss them. A farmer's video of a flood can reach thousands before any television crew is dispatched; communities long ignored by editors can finally tell their own stories. The gatekeepers who once decided what counted as news were never as neutral as they claimed.

But the new gatekeeper is worse, because it does not care about truth at all. A platform's algorithm does not ask whether a story is accurate or important, only whether it will hold attention. Each user's feed becomes a private newspaper, assembled for them alone, and the common ground on which citizens once argued slowly disappears. A society can survive disagreement about what the facts mean; it struggles to survive disagreement about what the facts are.

The shift to social media news, then, is less a change of channel than a change of editor. We have replaced people who were sometimes biased with a system that is indifferent to accuracy, and we have not yet learned how to compensate for it.

## 12. AI Tools for Schoolwork

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Technology and media

> **Question:** Some people think that students should be allowed to use artificial intelligence tools, such as chatbots, to help with their schoolwork. Others believe these tools should be banned in schools. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** artificial intelligence (AI) · chatbot · academic honesty · plagiarism · critical thinking · personal tutor

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: used correctly, AI can work like a personal tutor for every student.*

**🟡 B1:** AI tools can help students like a private teacher. When students do not understand a lesson, they can ask a chatbot to explain it in simple words. They can also ask it for more examples or to check their grammar. For example, I sometimes ask an AI tool to explain difficult English words to me. This is very useful, especially for students who cannot pay for extra classes.

**🔴 B2:** Used responsibly, AI tools can function as a personal tutor that is available at any time. A student who is confused by a maths problem late at night can ask a chatbot to explain each step, request additional examples or check their reasoning. This is particularly valuable for students whose families cannot afford private lessons, which means that AI could help to reduce educational inequality. However, these benefits depend on students using the tools to understand their work rather than to complete it for them.

**🟣 C1:** At its best, artificial intelligence offers something education has rarely been able to provide at scale: individual, patient and immediate explanation. A student struggling with an essay structure or a chemical equation can receive tailored guidance at any hour, rephrased as many times as necessary, without the embarrassment of repeatedly asking a teacher. For learners whose families cannot afford private tutors, this represents a potentially significant levelling of opportunity. The challenge for schools is therefore not whether to permit such tools, but how to ensure they support thinking rather than replace it.

**⚫ C2:** Private tuition has always been one of the quieter engines of inequality: the wealthy child who is stuck at nine in the evening calls a tutor, while the poor child simply stays stuck. AI tutors threaten to abolish that particular advantage. A patient explainer, available at any hour and untroubled by how many times the same question is asked, is exactly what most students have always lacked. Banning it would not restore fairness. It would merely return the advantage to those who could always pay for a human version.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 274 words

Today, many students use AI tools, such as chatbots, to help them with homework and assignments. Some people think this is useful, but others think AI should be banned in schools. In this essay, I will discuss both views and give my opinion.

On the one hand, some people believe that AI tools should be banned. They think students will use AI to cheat. For example, a student can ask a chatbot to write a whole essay in one minute and then give it to the teacher. If students do this, they do not learn anything, and they do not practise thinking or writing. Also, AI tools sometimes give wrong answers, and students may believe them.

On the other hand, other people think that AI can help students to learn. A chatbot can explain difficult lessons in simple words, give examples and answer questions at any time. For example, when I do not understand a grammar rule, I can ask an AI tool to explain it again and again. This is very helpful for students who cannot pay for extra classes. In addition, AI will be important in many jobs in the future, so students should learn how to use it.

In my opinion, schools should not ban AI tools, but they should make clear rules. Teachers can allow students to use AI for explanations and practice, but not for writing their homework. Teachers can also ask students to write some essays in class.

In conclusion, AI tools can be used for cheating, but they can also help students to learn. I believe that schools should teach students to use AI correctly and honestly.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 299 words

The rapid development of artificial intelligence has created a new dilemma for schools. While some educators want to ban AI tools because they make cheating easier, others believe these tools can improve learning. In my view, a total ban would be both unrealistic and unwise, but clear rules are essential.

Those who support a ban raise legitimate concerns. AI chatbots can now produce essays, solve mathematical problems and write computer code in seconds, which makes it extremely easy for students to submit work that is not their own. If students rely on these tools to do their thinking for them, they may never develop essential skills such as writing, problem-solving and critical analysis. Furthermore, AI tools sometimes produce information that sounds convincing but is incorrect, and young learners may not be able to identify these errors.

On the other hand, supporters of AI in education argue that these tools can act as personal tutors. A student who does not understand a topic can ask for a simple explanation, request more examples or receive instant feedback on a draft. This kind of individual support has traditionally been available only to families who could afford private lessons. Moreover, since AI is likely to be used in most workplaces in the future, schools have a responsibility to teach students how to use it effectively and ethically.

In my opinion, the benefits of AI outweigh the risks, provided that its use is carefully managed. Schools could allow AI for research and explanation while requiring important assignments to be completed in class. Teachers might also assess students through presentations and discussions, which are more difficult to fake.

In conclusion, although AI tools can encourage cheating, banning them completely would deprive students of a valuable learning resource. Teaching responsible use is the more effective approach.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 306 words

Few technologies have entered classrooms as abruptly as generative artificial intelligence. Its capacity to produce fluent essays and solutions within seconds has led some educators to call for outright bans, while others see it as the most powerful learning aid ever created. Both positions contain some truth, but I would argue that prohibition is ultimately self-defeating, and that schools should instead redesign teaching and assessment around the tools' existence.

The case for banning AI rests on a genuine threat to learning. Writing an essay is not merely a means of producing a text but a process through which students learn to organise ideas, weigh evidence and express themselves precisely. When that process is outsourced, the product may be flawless while the learning is absent. Moreover, AI systems can produce plausible-sounding inaccuracies, and inexperienced learners are poorly equipped to detect them.

Yet a ban faces serious practical and principled objections. Detecting AI-generated text is notoriously unreliable, so prohibition risks penalising honest students while doing little to deter dishonest ones. More fundamentally, it would deny students access to a potentially transformative resource: an individual tutor available at any hour, capable of explaining a concept repeatedly and in different ways. For students without access to private tuition, this could significantly narrow educational inequality.

The more productive response, therefore, is adaptation. If take-home essays can no longer reliably demonstrate learning, assessment must place greater weight on in-class writing, oral examinations and the documented development of ideas. At the same time, students should be explicitly taught how to question AI output rather than merely consume it.

In conclusion, artificial intelligence poses a serious challenge to traditional schooling, but banning it would address the symptom rather than the underlying problem. Schools that learn to integrate it thoughtfully are likely to produce more capable learners than those that pretend it does not exist.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 326 words

Every powerful educational technology has arrived accompanied by predictions of intellectual decline. Writing, Socrates warned, would weaken memory; calculators, teachers feared, would abolish arithmetic. Artificial intelligence is the latest candidate, and this time the anxiety is not entirely misplaced. Even so, banning it from schools would be a mistake.

The case for prohibition deserves to be taken seriously, because it is not simply about cheating. An essay is less a product than a process: the struggle to arrange one's thoughts on a page is itself the learning. A chatbot that produces polished paragraphs on demand does not merely help a student skip the assignment; it helps them skip the thinking the assignment was designed to provoke. And because these systems deliver confident errors in the same fluent prose as accurate answers, the students least able to spot a mistake are the most likely to be misled.

Yet a ban would fail on its own terms. Tools that are free, invisible and installed on every phone cannot be policed in any meaningful sense, and software that claims to detect AI-written text is too unreliable to justify accusing a student of dishonesty. A prohibition would therefore punish the scrupulous, reward the discreet, and teach everyone that rules and reality have parted company.

More importantly, a ban would waste an extraordinary opportunity. For the first time, a student without money for private lessons can receive individual explanation on demand, in their own words and at their own pace. To withhold that from everyone is not neutrality; it is a quiet subsidy to the families who can still afford a human tutor.

The sensible course is to change what schools ask students to do. Assess thinking where it can be seen — in conversation, in class, in drafts that show their working — and teach students to interrogate the machine rather than obey it. Education has absorbed disruptive tools before, and it has never done so by pretending they did not exist.

## 13. A Cashless Society

**Type:** Advantages and disadvantages (outweigh) · **Theme:** Technology and media

> **Question:** In many countries, people are using cash less and less, and paying by card or mobile phone instead. Do the advantages of a cashless society outweigh the disadvantages?

**Useful vocabulary:** digital payments · QR code · transaction · financial inclusion · cybercrime · privacy

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: every digital payment leaves a record — good for fighting crime, worrying for privacy.*

**🟡 B1:** When people pay by card or phone, every payment is recorded. This makes it difficult for thieves and criminals to use money in secret. It also helps people to check how much they spend every month. For example, my banking app shows me all my payments, so I can see that I spend too much on coffee! However, some people do not like the bank knowing everything about them.

**🔴 B2:** An important advantage of digital payments is that every transaction is recorded. This makes it much harder for criminals to hide illegal income and for businesses to avoid paying tax, because money can be traced. At the same time, individuals can monitor their own spending more easily through banking apps. However, this transparency also raises privacy concerns, since banks, technology companies and governments can potentially see where, when and how people spend their money.

**🟣 C1:** The most significant advantage of a cashless economy may lie not in convenience but in transparency. Because every digital transaction leaves a record, the shift away from cash makes it considerably harder to evade taxes, pay bribes or launder the proceeds of crime — activities that depend on money that cannot be traced. For governments, the resulting increase in tax revenue can fund public services; for citizens, a reduction in petty corruption can transform everyday dealings with officials. The very feature that critics fear — traceability — is, from this perspective, its greatest public benefit.

**⚫ C2:** Cash has one property that no digital system can replicate: it forgets. A banknote passes from hand to hand without recording who held it, which is precisely why it is loved by tax evaders, bribe-takers and money launderers — and, it must be said, by ordinary people who would simply rather not be watched. A cashless society trades that forgetfulness for a perfect memory. Whether this is progress depends entirely on who controls the memory, and on what they are permitted to do with it.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 276 words

Nowadays, many people pay by card or mobile phone instead of using cash. In some countries, it is possible to live for weeks without touching a banknote. I think the advantages of a cashless society are bigger than the disadvantages.

The first advantage is that digital payment is fast and easy. People only need to scan a QR code or tap a card, and they do not need to carry money or wait for change. In my country, for example, many shops, restaurants and even small street sellers accept payment by phone. It is also easy to send money to family members in other provinces.

The second advantage is safety. If people do not carry a lot of cash, thieves cannot steal it. Also, every payment is recorded, so it is more difficult for criminals to hide money. Banking apps also help people to see how much they spend, so they can save money more easily.

However, there are some disadvantages. Firstly, some older people do not know how to use banking apps, and some poor people do not have a bank account or a smartphone. Secondly, if the internet or the electricity stops working, people cannot pay for anything. Thirdly, criminals can steal money online by hacking accounts or sending fake messages.

In my opinion, these problems can be solved. Banks can teach older people how to use apps, and shops can still accept some cash for people who need it. People should also be careful with their passwords.

In conclusion, a cashless society has some problems, but I believe its advantages are greater, because digital payment is faster, easier and safer for most people.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 281 words

Over the past decade, digital payments have spread rapidly across the world, and in some countries cash has almost disappeared from everyday transactions. Although this trend creates certain risks, I believe that the advantages of a cashless society outweigh its disadvantages.

The most obvious benefit is convenience. Paying by card or by scanning a QR code is quicker than counting cash, and it allows people to send money instantly to friends, relatives or businesses. This has been particularly valuable for migrant workers who need to send part of their income to their families in the countryside. Furthermore, digital payments are generally safer than carrying large amounts of cash, which can be lost or stolen.

Another significant advantage is transparency. Since every electronic payment is recorded, it becomes much more difficult for individuals and companies to hide income from the tax authorities or to conduct illegal business. As a result, governments may be able to collect more tax and reduce corruption.

Nevertheless, there are genuine disadvantages. Elderly people and those living in poverty may lack the smartphones, bank accounts or digital skills needed to participate, which risks excluding them from the economy. In addition, a cashless system is vulnerable to technical failures and cybercrime, and it raises concerns about privacy, since banks and technology companies can track how people spend their money.

However, these problems can be reduced through sensible policies, such as requiring essential services to continue accepting cash, providing digital training for older citizens and introducing strong data protection laws.

In conclusion, while a completely cashless society carries some risks, its benefits in terms of convenience, safety and transparency are considerable. With appropriate safeguards, the advantages clearly outweigh the disadvantages.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 288 words

The decline of cash is proceeding at remarkable speed. In countries as different as Sweden and China, digital payments now dominate everyday transactions, and in much of Southeast Asia QR-code payments have spread from shopping centres to street stalls within a few years. I would argue that the advantages of this transition outweigh its disadvantages, although a complete abolition of cash would be unwise.

The benefits extend beyond mere convenience. Digital payments reduce the costs and risks associated with handling physical money, and they enable instant transfers that are particularly valuable for households dependent on remittances. Mobile money has even extended basic financial services to people who have never held a bank account, as Kenya's M-Pesa system famously demonstrated. Perhaps most significantly, the traceability of electronic transactions makes tax evasion, bribery and money laundering considerably more difficult — a substantial public benefit in economies where the informal sector deprives governments of revenue.

The disadvantages, however, should not be understated. A fully cashless system risks excluding those without smartphones, digital literacy or stable internet access, disproportionately the elderly and the rural poor. It also concentrates vulnerability, since a cyberattack or network failure could paralyse commerce. Most troubling of all, it creates a comprehensive record of citizens' private lives that could be exploited by corporations or abused by governments.

These risks, though, are arguments for regulation rather than resistance. Guaranteeing access to cash for essential services, investing in digital inclusion and enforcing strong privacy protections would preserve most of the benefits while mitigating the dangers.

On balance, then, the shift towards digital payments is beneficial, but cash should be preserved as a safeguard — a backup for emergencies and a refuge for those who value privacy or lack access to digital systems.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 304 words

Money has always been a technology, and cash is merely an old version of it — one that happens to be anonymous, tangible and independent of electricity. The question of a cashless society is therefore less about convenience than about which of those properties we are willing to give up. My answer is that the advantages of digital payment outweigh the losses, but only if we refuse to let cash disappear entirely.

The advantages are substantial. A QR code on a noodle stall abolishes the tedium of change; a mobile transfer lets a factory worker in the city support parents in a distant village within seconds. Kenya's M-Pesa showed that a phone could bring financial services to millions whom banks had never bothered to serve. And because digital money leaves a trail, it quietly undermines the tax evader, the bribe-taker and the money launderer, whose trades depend on notes that tell no tales.

Yet the trail cuts both ways. Digital money remembers everything — every purchase, every donation, every late-night payment — and that record belongs to someone other than the person who made it. In the hands of a careful regulator, it is a tool against crime. In the hands of an overreaching state or a careless corporation, it is a map of private life. Nor is the digital system as robust as it appears: a network failure can freeze an entire city's commerce in a way that no shortage of banknotes ever could.

The sensible conclusion is not to resist the digital shift but to insist on a right to remain partly outside it. Keep cash as a legal option for essential purchases; enforce strict limits on how payment data may be used. A society can enjoy the efficiency of digital money without surrendering the privacy that cash provided — but only if it decides to.

## 14. Video Games and Children

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Technology and media

> **Question:** Some people believe that playing video games is harmful to children, while others think it can be beneficial. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** screen time · addiction · violent content · problem-solving · in-game purchases · moderation

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: many games develop problem-solving and teamwork.*

**🟡 B1:** Some video games can help children to learn. In many games, players must think carefully, make plans and solve problems to win. Other games help children to practise English or maths in a fun way. Also, when children play online with friends, they learn to work as a team. For example, my brother learned many English words from playing games. So video games are not always bad.

**🔴 B2:** Contrary to popular belief, many video games require complex thinking skills. Strategy and puzzle games, for example, ask players to plan several steps ahead, analyse problems and adapt quickly when their first approach fails. Online multiplayer games also involve cooperation and communication, since players must coordinate with teammates to achieve a shared goal. Some research even suggests that certain games can improve hand-eye coordination and spatial awareness. Therefore, when chosen carefully, video games can support children's development rather than harm it.

**🟣 C1:** The assumption that video games are intellectually empty is difficult to sustain. Many popular titles demand sophisticated reasoning: players must manage limited resources, anticipate opponents' moves and revise strategies in response to failure — a cycle of hypothesis and experiment not unlike that of scientific inquiry. Collaborative online games, meanwhile, require coordination and communication under pressure. None of this means that all games are beneficial or that unlimited play is harmless; it does suggest, however, that the medium itself is not the problem, but rather the content chosen and the time devoted to it.

**⚫ C2:** Watch a child absorbed in a well-designed strategy game and you will see something teachers spend careers trying to produce: sustained concentration, experiment, failure, adjustment and renewed attempt, all undertaken voluntarily. The game has solved a problem that schools often cannot — making difficulty enjoyable. That does not make every game valuable, nor every hour well spent. But it should make us wary of condemning a medium whose central achievement is persuading children to persist at something hard.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 269 words

Video games are very popular with children today. Some people think that games are bad for children, but other people believe games can be good for them. In this essay, I will discuss both views and give my opinion.

On the one hand, many parents think video games are harmful. Firstly, some children play for many hours every day, so they do not have enough time for homework, sleep or sport. This can make them unhealthy and tired at school. Secondly, some games are violent, and parents worry that children may copy bad behaviour. In addition, some children become addicted to games and feel angry when they cannot play.

On the other hand, some people think video games can be useful. Many games help children to think quickly, make plans and solve problems. Some games teach English, maths or history in a fun way. For example, my younger brother learned a lot of English words from playing online games. Also, when children play with friends online, they learn to communicate and work in a team.

In my opinion, video games are not always bad, but children should play them in the right way. Parents should choose games that are suitable for their children's age, and they should make rules about time. For example, children can play for one hour after finishing their homework. Parents should also encourage children to do sports and other activities.

In conclusion, video games can be harmful if children play too much or play violent games. However, if parents control the time and choose good games, games can help children to learn and have fun.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 290 words

Video games have become one of the most popular forms of entertainment for children, which has led to a heated debate about their effects. While some people regard them as a serious threat to children's health and development, others argue that they offer important benefits. In my opinion, video games are neither good nor bad in themselves; their effects depend on how they are used.

Those who consider video games harmful usually highlight three concerns. The first is excessive screen time, which can reduce the amount of time children spend on physical activity, homework and sleep. The second is addiction: many games are deliberately designed to keep players engaged for as long as possible, for example by offering rewards for playing every day. The third is violent content, which some parents fear may make children more aggressive or less sensitive to violence.

On the other hand, supporters of video games point out that many of them require complex thinking. Strategy and puzzle games encourage players to plan ahead and solve problems, while online team games develop communication and cooperation. Some educational games also make learning subjects such as mathematics or foreign languages more enjoyable. Furthermore, for many children, playing games is an important way of socialising with friends.

In my view, the key issue is moderation. When children play age-appropriate games for a limited amount of time, the benefits are likely to outweigh the risks. Parents therefore have an important role to play in setting clear limits and choosing suitable games, rather than simply banning them.

In addition, some websites deliberately publish misleading headlines because they earn money from advertising every time a page is viewed. Finally, many users share stories without checking them, particularly when the information confirms their existing opinions.

Several measures could reduce the problem. Firstly, social media companies should take greater responsibility by labelling false content, reducing its visibility and working with independent fact-checking organisations. Secondly, governments could require platforms to be more transparent about how their algorithms work, while being careful not to restrict freedom of speech. Most importantly, however, schools should teach media literacy, so that young people learn how to evaluate sources, recognise manipulated images and think critically before sharing. This would address the problem at its root, since misinformation can only spread if people are willing to believe and share it.

In conclusion, false information spreads online because of a lack of editorial control, attention-seeking algorithms, financial incentives and careless sharing. A combination of platform responsibility, sensible regulation and education offers the best chance of reducing it.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 285 words

The ease with which false information now circulates online represents one of the more insidious side effects of the digital revolution. Its causes are partly technological and partly human, and any serious response must address both dimensions rather than relying on a single solution.

The technological causes are well documented. Social media platforms have removed the gatekeepers who once filtered information before publication, and their algorithms — designed to maximise engagement — systematically favour content that provokes strong emotional reactions. Since falsehoods are often more novel and sensational than the truth, they enjoy a structural advantage in this environment. Financial incentives compound the problem: advertising revenue rewards traffic regardless of accuracy, encouraging the production of misleading content.

The human causes are no less significant. People are more inclined to believe and share information that confirms their existing beliefs, and in a fast-moving feed they rarely pause to verify what they read. Declining trust in established institutions, moreover, has made some audiences more receptive to alternative sources, however unreliable.

Addressing these causes requires action at several levels. Platforms should be obliged to reduce the algorithmic amplification of content identified as false and to be transparent about how their recommendation systems operate. Independent fact-checking, though unable to keep pace with every falsehood, can at least expose the most damaging ones. The most durable remedy, however, lies in education: media literacy programmes that teach students to question sources, identify manipulation and recognise their own biases address the problem where it ultimately succeeds or fails — in the mind of the reader.

In short, misinformation thrives on a combination of engagement-driven technology and human cognitive shortcuts. Regulation can restrain the former, but only education can meaningfully strengthen our defences against the latter.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 306 words

False information is not new; rumour is as old as speech. What is new is its speed, its scale and the efficiency with which it can now be manufactured and distributed. To understand why misinformation flourishes online, and what might be done about it, we have to look at the machines and at ourselves.

The machines are designed to maximise attention, and attention is not a measure of truth. A platform whose algorithm rewards whatever provokes the strongest reaction will, with mechanical indifference, amplify the outrageous over the accurate, since invention can always be more shocking than fact. Add advertising models that pay for clicks regardless of content, and one has built an industry in which deception is not an accident but a viable business.

Yet the machines only amplify what we choose to share, and here the uncomfortable truth concerns human nature. We believe most readily what we already suspect; we share most eagerly what makes us feel angry, righteous or afraid; and we rarely pause to check a story that flatters our convictions. Misinformation does not so much deceive us as recruit us.

The remedies must therefore work on both fronts. Platforms should be required to stop amplifying demonstrably false content and to open their recommendation systems to independent scrutiny — measures that regulate distribution without licensing governments to decide what may be said. Fact-checking, though always a step behind, remains worth funding. But the most durable defence is cultural: an education that teaches citizens, from childhood, to treat their own certainty with suspicion and to ask of any claim not only whether it is true, but why it was put in front of them.

No society will ever eliminate falsehood. The realistic goal is a population that is harder to recruit — one in which a lie needs more than a catchy headline to travel.

## 19. The Future of Printed Books

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Technology and media

> **Question:** Some people believe that printed books and newspapers will disappear in the future because of digital technology. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** e-book · e-reader · print edition · digital subscription · screen fatigue · publishing industry

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: printed books help people concentrate in a way screens do not.*

**🟡 B1:** I think printed books will not disappear because many people enjoy them. They like the feeling of paper and the smell of a new book. Reading a paper book is also better for our eyes than looking at a screen for a long time. For example, I read e-books on my phone, but I always feel tired after one hour. With a paper book, I can read for longer and remember more.

**🔴 B2:** Printed books are likely to survive because they offer a reading experience that many people still prefer. Unlike phones and tablets, a paper book has no notifications, advertisements or links to distract the reader, which makes it easier to concentrate for long periods. Many readers also value physical books as objects that can be collected, given as gifts or passed on to others. Some studies even suggest that people understand long texts better when they read them on paper. For these reasons, printed books are likely to remain popular.

**🟣 C1:** The persistence of printed books owes less to nostalgia than to the distinctive kind of attention they sustain. A screen is a gateway to everything else — messages, notifications, the temptation of a quick search — whereas a book offers nothing but itself. For extended reading, this limitation is precisely its strength. There is also some evidence that readers comprehend long, complex texts more fully on paper, perhaps because the physical sense of progress through a volume helps them map its structure. Far from being obsolete, the printed book may become more valuable as other media grow more distracting.

**⚫ C2:** A screen is a doorway to everything; a book is a doorway to one thing. That, rather than any sentimental attachment to the smell of paper, is why printed books endure. The reader who opens a novel on a phone is always one notification away from abandoning it, while the reader holding a paperback has, in effect, locked the other doors. In an economy built on interrupting us, an object that refuses to interrupt has quietly become a luxury.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 298 words

Predictions of the death of print have been made with remarkable confidence for several decades, and they have proved at least half wrong. I would argue that the fates of printed newspapers and printed books are likely to diverge sharply: the former may well become a niche product, while the latter seems set to endure.

The case against the printed newspaper is compelling. Its economic model depended on classified and display advertising, much of which has migrated to digital platforms, and its central product — the news — is by nature perishable. A medium that is out of date before it reaches the reader cannot compete with continuously updated digital coverage. It is therefore unsurprising that many newspapers have reduced or abandoned their print editions, reinventing themselves as digital subscription services.

Books, however, occupy a fundamentally different position. Their content is not time-sensitive, and the experience of reading them has proved remarkably resistant to digital substitution. Despite the rapid early growth of e-books, print has remained the dominant format in many markets. This resilience appears to reflect not mere nostalgia but practical advantages: printed books impose no screen fatigue, offer no distracting notifications and, according to some research, support better comprehension of long, complex texts. They also possess a cultural and aesthetic value as objects that digital files cannot replicate.

It would be unwise, admittedly, to assume that current preferences are permanent; readers who grow up entirely with screens may feel differently. Yet the printed book has survived the arrival of radio, television and the internet, and its core virtues remain intact.

In conclusion, the prediction that print will disappear is largely accurate for newspapers but misguided for books. The latter's survival suggests that technological change does not always replace older media; sometimes it simply clarifies what they do best.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 289 words

Few prophecies have been made as often, or with as much confidence, as the death of print. The internet was supposed to kill the book; before it, television; before that, radio. The book has attended all of their predicted funerals as a guest. The newspaper, however, may not be so lucky, and the difference between their fortunes reveals something about what technology actually replaces.

The printed newspaper is dying for a simple reason: its content has a short life. News is perishable, and a medium that delivers yesterday's events to today's reader cannot compete with one that updates every minute. Worse, the advertising that once paid for the printing presses has migrated to digital platforms. Print editions will linger for a loyal and ageing readership, but their decline is structural rather than temporary.

The book is another matter, because it was never merely a container for information. It is a technology for sustained attention, and its great virtue is that it can do only one thing. It cannot ping, update, recommend or tempt its reader towards something shorter and louder. In an age engineered to fracture concentration, that limitation looks increasingly like an advantage, which may explain why, after an early rush of enthusiasm, e-books have settled into coexistence with print rather than conquest.

One should not be complacent. Habits formed in childhood shape adult preferences, and a generation raised on screens may one day find paper as quaint as vinyl records. Yet even vinyl, it is worth noting, refused to die.

So I agree only in part. The printed newspaper is likely to fade into a specialist product. The printed book, having outlived every rival that was meant to replace it, seems likely to outlive this one too.

## 20. Celebrities as Role Models

**Type:** Two-part question · **Theme:** Technology and media

> **Question:** Many young people today see celebrities, such as sports stars and social media influencers, as role models. Why is this? Is this a positive or negative development?

**Useful vocabulary:** role model · influencer · followers · curated image · materialism · self-esteem

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: many influencers show unrealistic lives that make young people feel insecure.*

**🟡 B1:** Many influencers show a perfect life on social media. They have expensive clothes, beautiful homes and holidays in beautiful places. When young people see these photos every day, they may feel that their own life is boring or not good enough. Some of them want to buy expensive things to look like their favourite influencer. For example, some of my classmates spent a lot of money on a phone just because a famous person had one.

**🔴 B2:** A major concern is that many influencers present an unrealistic image of life. Their photos and videos are carefully selected and often edited to show perfect bodies, luxury products and exciting holidays, while problems and failures are hidden. Young people who compare themselves with these images may develop low self-esteem or feel pressure to spend money on fashionable products. Furthermore, many posts are actually paid advertisements, although this is not always made clear. As a result, following influencers can encourage materialism rather than hard work or kindness.

**🟣 C1:** The difficulty with influencers as role models lies partly in the nature of what they model. Whereas an athlete's fame rests, at least in principle, on demonstrable achievement, an influencer's success often depends on the curated display of an enviable lifestyle — one that is frequently subsidised by the very brands it promotes. Young followers are thus encouraged to admire not effort or skill but visibility and consumption. Combined with the relentless comparison that social media invites, this can foster both insecurity and a distorted understanding of how success is actually achieved.

**⚫ C2:** An athlete becomes famous for doing something; many influencers are famous for being seen. The distinction matters, because a role model teaches not only how to behave but what to value. The young footballer who idolises a champion learns that excellence is built through years of unglamorous practice. The teenager who idolises a lifestyle account learns, more subtly, that the goal of life is to be looked at — and that the look is for sale.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 303 words

Celebrities have always attracted admiration, but social media has transformed the relationship between the famous and their audiences, turning distant idols into apparently intimate companions. This essay examines why young people increasingly adopt such figures as role models and argues that the development is, on balance, a cause for concern — though the picture varies considerably depending on the kind of celebrity involved.

The principal reason lies in the architecture of social media. Platforms allow followers to observe celebrities' daily routines, reply to their posts and occasionally receive responses, creating what psychologists term parasocial relationships — one-sided bonds that nevertheless feel personal. For adolescents, who are actively constructing their identities, such figures offer vivid models of confidence, success and belonging. The effects are mixed. Some public figures, particularly athletes, exemplify discipline and resilience, and their example can inspire genuine effort. #### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 319 words

There was a time when fame was a by-product: one became famous for scoring goals, singing songs or discovering something. For a growing number of today's celebrities, fame is the product itself. Understanding this shift explains both why young people now choose such figures as role models and why the trend should worry us.

The reasons are largely technological. A teenager in 1990 might admire a footballer from the stands or through a television screen; a teenager today can follow a favourite influencer from breakfast to bedtime, reply to their posts and occasionally receive a reply. Such one-sided intimacy — psychologists call it parasocial — feels like friendship, and friendship is precisely what adolescents, uncertain of who they are, most want. Add the fading presence of older sources of guidance, and the celebrity steps easily into an empty space.

Not all of this is harmful. A champion athlete's example can teach a child more about discipline than a hundred lectures, and some celebrities use their reach to draw attention to causes that deserve it.

Yet the most visible role models of the digital age are increasingly admired not for what they do, but for how they live — or rather, for how they appear to live. Their feeds offer a gallery of flawless faces, sponsored handbags and effortless holidays, carefully curated and frequently paid for by the very brands on display. The lesson absorbed by an audience of fourteen-year-olds is not that success requires effort, but that it looks a particular way and can be bought. It is hard to imagine a more efficient recipe for insecurity.

On balance, then, the development is negative — not because young people admire the famous, which they always have, but because the famous are now so often admired for nothing more than being admired. The task for parents and schools is not to forbid such admiration, but to teach the difference between achievement and its advertisement.
