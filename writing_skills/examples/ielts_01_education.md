## 1. Free University Education

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Education

> **Question:** Some people believe that university education should be free for all students, with the costs paid by the government. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** tuition fees · student debt · equal opportunity · public funding · skilled workforce · taxpayers

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: free university education is an investment that pays back.*

**🟡 B1:** Free university education is good for the whole country, not only for students. When more young people go to university, the country gets more doctors, engineers and teachers. These people usually earn good salaries, so they pay more tax later. For example, a doctor works for many years and pays tax every year. In this way, the government gets its money back. So free university is a good investment.

**🔴 B2:** Free higher education should be seen as an investment rather than an expense. Graduates tend to earn higher salaries, which means they pay more income tax throughout their careers, so a large part of the original cost is eventually returned to the state. In addition, a highly educated workforce attracts foreign companies and encourages new businesses, creating jobs for others. Therefore, although free tuition is expensive in the short term, it is likely to strengthen the economy in the long term.

**🟣 C1:** Public spending on university education is better understood as an investment than as a subsidy. Graduates typically command higher earnings and therefore contribute more in taxation over their working lives, so much of the initial outlay is recovered indirectly. More significantly, a highly skilled workforce generates benefits that extend well beyond the individual: it attracts investment, drives innovation and raises productivity across entire industries. Viewed in this light, charging tuition fees is a false economy, saving money in the short term at the expense of long-term national prosperity.

**⚫ C2:** To treat free tuition as a cost is to read only half of the ledger. The state that pays for a nurse's training does not simply lose the fees; it gains forty years of care, taxes and experience passed on to younger colleagues. Economists call such benefits positive externalities — gains enjoyed by people who never paid for them — and higher education produces them in abundance. A country that rations degrees by price saves money today, only to pay for the shortage tomorrow.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 269 words

Nowadays, university education is very expensive in many countries. Some people think that the government should pay for all students to study at university. I mostly agree with this idea because it is fair and it helps the whole country.

Firstly, free university gives every young person the same chance. In many countries, students from poor families cannot pay high fees, so they have to find a job after high school. This is not fair, because some of them are very clever and hard-working. For example, in my country, many good students work in shops or factories instead of studying. If university is free, they can study to become teachers, doctors or engineers.

Secondly, free education is good for the country's future. When more people go to university, the country has more skilled workers. These workers can get better jobs and earn more money, so they also pay more tax to the government. For example, one doctor can help thousands of people during their career and pay tax for many years. In this way, the money that the government spends on students comes back later.

On the other hand, free university costs a lot of money, and all taxpayers have to pay for it. Some students may also choose a course that they do not really need, or they may study slowly because it is free. Because of this, students should work hard and finish their studies on time.

In conclusion, I believe that university education should be free because it gives everyone an equal chance and helps the country to develop. However, students must use this chance well.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 273 words

Whether higher education should be funded entirely by the state is a question that divides opinion in many countries. While I accept that free tuition places a considerable burden on public finances, I believe that the benefits for individuals and for society as a whole justify the cost.

The main argument in favour of free university education is that it guarantees equal opportunities. When students are required to pay high fees, talented young people from low-income families are often discouraged from applying, either because they cannot afford the fees or because they are unwilling to take on years of debt. Consequently, the chance to study depends on wealth rather than ability. In Germany, for example, most public universities charge little or no tuition, allowing students to choose a course according to their interests and talents.

Furthermore, investing in higher education brings long-term economic returns. Graduates tend to earn higher salaries and therefore pay more tax over their working lives, which means that much of the original investment is eventually recovered. A well-educated workforce also attracts foreign companies and encourages innovation, both of which contribute to economic growth.

Admittedly, critics argue that free tuition is unfair to taxpayers who never attended university, and that it may encourage some students to enrol without a clear purpose. However, these problems could be reduced by linking funding to academic progress or by offering free places mainly in subjects where the country faces skills shortages.

In conclusion, although making university free is expensive, I strongly believe it is a worthwhile investment, since it gives every capable student a fair chance and strengthens the economy in the long run.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 273 words

Few policy questions expose competing ideas of fairness as clearly as the funding of higher education. Although universal free tuition is costly and imperfectly targeted, I largely agree that university should be publicly funded, provided that the system is designed to widen participation rather than simply to subsidise those who would have enrolled anyway.

The most compelling argument for free provision concerns access. Where tuition fees are high, participation becomes determined less by ability than by family income, and the fear of debt disproportionately deters students with the least financial security, even when loans are available. The resulting loss of talent is not a private misfortune but a collective one: every capable student who never enrols represents skills from which the economy will never benefit.

There is also a strong economic rationale. Graduates typically earn more and contribute more in taxation over their careers, so public spending on degrees is better understood as an investment than as a subsidy. Nations competing in knowledge-intensive industries can ill afford to ration higher education by price.

Critics nevertheless raise a legitimate concern. Free tuition can be regressive in practice, since middle-class students are more likely to reach university and therefore capture much of the benefit, while the cost is shared by all taxpayers. Yet this objection is an argument for better targeting — through maintenance grants for poorer students and outreach programmes in under-represented areas — rather than for fees that would compound existing inequalities.

In short, the principle of free university education is sound, but its success depends on design. A system that removes financial barriers while actively widening participation would deliver both fairness and long-term prosperity.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 277 words

Tuition fees are usually presented as a price. In practice, they function as a filter — and an inefficient one, since what they sift out is not a lack of talent but a lack of money. For that reason, I agree that university education should be free, though not for the reason most often given.

The familiar case rests on economics: graduates earn more, pay more tax and, in effect, repay the state's investment. This is true, but it concedes too much, because it implies that education deserves funding only when it pays for itself. The stronger argument concerns what a society chooses to waste. A gifted student from a poor family and an equally gifted one from a wealthy family do not face the same decision when the bill arrives, and the difference has nothing to do with ability. No one records the engineer who never enrolled; the loss is silent, but it is paid by everyone.

The objection deserves to be stated at its strongest. Free tuition, critics note, often benefits the already advantaged, whose children are more likely to reach university in the first place; the bus driver's taxes may end up subsidising the lawyer's son. This is a serious point, but it indicts the pipeline rather than the principle. The answer is to widen the entrance — through better schools, living grants and outreach — not to reinstate a toll at the door.

Ultimately, the question is not whether a nation can afford free higher education, but whether it can afford to go on selecting its future professionals by their parents' income. Put that way, free tuition looks less like an expense than an overdue correction.

## 2. Practical Life Skills at School

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Education

> **Question:** Some people think that schools should spend more time teaching practical life skills, such as managing money and cooking, even if this means less time for academic subjects. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** financial literacy · household budget · academic subjects · curriculum · independence · well-rounded education

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: learning to manage money at school prevents problems with debt later.*

**🟡 B1:** I think schools should teach students how to manage money. Many young people do not know how to make a budget, so they spend too much and get into debt. For example, some of my friends used credit cards at university and could not pay the money back. If students learn about saving and spending at school, they will make better choices as adults. This is a very useful skill for everyone.

**🔴 B2:** One of the most valuable practical skills schools could teach is financial literacy. Many young adults leave education without understanding basic ideas such as budgeting, interest rates or loans, which makes them vulnerable to debt. For instance, credit cards and "buy now, pay later" apps mean that students can quickly spend money they do not have. If these topics were included in the curriculum, young people would be better prepared to make responsible financial decisions. Therefore, money management deserves a regular place in the school timetable.

**🟣 C1:** Of all the practical skills that could be taught in schools, financial literacy arguably has the most far-reaching consequences. Young people now face complex financial decisions — from student loans to instant credit offered through smartphone apps — at an ever earlier age, yet many leave school unable to calculate interest or draw up a basic budget. The costs of this gap are borne not only by individuals trapped in debt but also by society as a whole. Teaching money management is therefore less a distraction from academic learning than an application of it: few mathematical skills are used more often than working out what one can afford.

**⚫ C2:** Schools teach teenagers to solve quadratic equations they may never meet again, yet send them into a world of instant loans and one-click purchases without explaining how compound interest quietly works against them. The omission is not neutral. Ignorance has a price, and it is paid in late fees and debts that can shadow a young adult for a decade. Financial literacy, properly taught, is not a break from mathematics. It is mathematics with consequences.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 277 words

These days, most schools spend a lot of time on academic subjects such as maths, science and history. Some people think that schools should teach more practical skills, like cooking and managing money, even if there is less time for other subjects. I partly agree with this idea.

Firstly, practical skills are very useful in everyday life. Every adult needs to cook healthy food, pay bills and save money. However, many young people finish school without these skills. For example, when my friend moved to the city for university, she ate instant noodles every day because she could not cook. She also spent all her money in the first two weeks of each month.

Secondly, some parents do not have time to teach these skills at home. Many parents work long hours, so their children do not learn how to cook or plan a budget. If schools teach these skills, all students can learn them, not only children whose parents have free time.

On the other hand, academic subjects are still very important. Students need maths, science and languages to pass exams, go to university and get good jobs. For example, doctors and engineers need many years of academic study. If schools reduce these lessons too much, students may not be ready for higher education.

In my opinion, the best idea is to add one or two practical lessons every week. Teachers can also connect these lessons to other subjects. For example, students can use maths to plan a family budget.

In conclusion, I believe practical life skills should be taught at school, but they should not replace academic subjects. Both are necessary for a good future.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 265 words

It is often argued that schools focus too heavily on academic knowledge and neglect the practical skills that young people need in adult life. While I agree that subjects such as money management and cooking deserve more attention, I do not believe they should replace a significant amount of academic learning.

There are strong reasons to teach practical skills at school. Many young adults leave education without knowing how to prepare a healthy meal, manage a monthly budget or understand a loan agreement. As a result, they may rely on unhealthy fast food or fall into debt, particularly now that credit is available instantly through smartphone apps. Furthermore, not all parents have the time or knowledge to teach these skills, so leaving the responsibility to families means that some children are far better prepared than others.

However, academic subjects remain essential. Mathematics, science and languages provide the foundation for higher education and for many well-paid careers, and reducing them could limit students' future opportunities. In countries where university places are highly competitive, parents are also likely to resist any change that might lower exam results.

In my view, the most effective solution is to integrate practical skills into existing subjects rather than replacing them. Budgeting, for instance, can be taught within mathematics, while nutrition and cooking fit naturally into science or health education. In this way, students would gain useful life skills while still receiving a strong academic education.

In conclusion, practical skills should certainly have a place in the school curriculum, but they are best taught alongside academic subjects rather than instead of them.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 271 words

Critics of modern schooling frequently complain that young people can solve equations but cannot cook a meal or read a bank statement. The complaint is justified, but the proposed remedy — trading academic time for practical instruction — rests on a false opposition. I agree that life skills deserve a far more prominent place in schools, but the most effective way to secure it is through integration rather than substitution.

The case for teaching practical skills is compelling. Young people now face consequential financial decisions, from student loans to instant credit, at an ever earlier age, and the consequences of poor choices can follow them for years. Crucially, the opportunity to acquire such skills at home is unevenly distributed: children whose parents are financially literate absorb that knowledge informally, while others do not. Leaving life skills to families therefore tends to reproduce existing inequalities.

Nevertheless, academic subjects should not be treated as dispensable. Mathematics, science and language provide the conceptual foundations on which many practical competences depend; it is difficult, after all, to understand compound interest without a firm grasp of percentages. Reducing academic content would thus risk undermining the very skills the reform seeks to promote.

The more productive approach is to teach academic knowledge through practical application. Budgeting exercises can anchor abstract mathematics, while cooking can illustrate principles of chemistry and nutrition. Such integration not only saves curriculum time but also answers the perennial student question: "When will I ever use this?"

In short, the goal of preparing students for adult life is sound, but it is best achieved by making academic learning more practical, not by making it less academic.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 284 words

Every generation of adults seems to discover that the young cannot do something it considers basic — write a letter, change a tyre, cook a meal. The current complaint concerns money and food, and it has more substance than most. Yet the question as posed invites a false bargain. The choice is not between practical skills and academic subjects; it is between knowledge that stays on the page and knowledge that is put to work.

The case for practical instruction is strongest where the stakes are highest. A teenager today can borrow money with a single tap, often from companies that understand the arithmetic of debt far better than their customers do. Leaving financial education to families sounds reasonable until one notices which families are best equipped to provide it: those who need it least. School, for all its flaws, is the only institution that reaches every child.

But the critics of academic study overplay their hand. Percentages, probability and the chemistry of heat are not obstacles to practical competence; they are its foundations. A student who cannot calculate interest cannot budget, however many budgeting lessons they attend. To cut the theory in order to teach the practice is to saw off the branch one is sitting on.

The sensible course, then, is not subtraction but translation. Teach fractions through recipes, statistics through household bills and chemistry through the kitchen, and the supposed conflict dissolves. Students gain the skills; the subjects gain a reason to exist in the students' minds.

I agree, therefore, that schools should do far more to prepare young people for ordinary life — but not by making them less educated. The aim should be a curriculum in which nothing is merely academic.

## 3. Studying Abroad

**Type:** Advantages and disadvantages · **Theme:** Education

> **Question:** An increasing number of students are choosing to study at universities in other countries. What are the advantages and disadvantages of this trend?

**Useful vocabulary:** international students · culture shock · homesickness · independence · brain drain · living costs

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: studying abroad develops independence and the ability to live among different cultures.*

**🟡 B1:** Studying in another country helps young people become more independent. When students live far from their families, they must cook, pay bills and solve problems by themselves. For example, my friend studied in Australia, and at first she felt homesick. However, after one year she was more confident and could do everything alone. These skills are very useful in life and at work.

**🔴 B2:** One major advantage of studying abroad is that it develops independence and intercultural skills. Students who live in a foreign country have to manage their own finances, adapt to unfamiliar customs and communicate with people from very different backgrounds. Although this can be challenging at first, it builds confidence and flexibility, which are highly valued by employers. For example, a graduate who has worked on group projects with classmates from many countries is likely to feel comfortable in an international company. Therefore, the experience often benefits students long after they graduate.

**🟣 C1:** Perhaps the most enduring benefit of an overseas education lies not in the degree itself but in the personal development it demands. Removed from familiar support networks, international students must negotiate everything from tenancy agreements to unfamiliar academic conventions, often in a second language. The resilience and intercultural competence acquired in the process are precisely the qualities that employers in a globalised economy find hardest to teach. In this sense, the challenges of living abroad are not an unfortunate side effect of international study but an integral part of its value.

**⚫ C2:** Independence cannot be taught in a lecture hall; it has to be forced on a person by circumstance. The international student who must open a bank account in a second language, argue with a landlord and cook a first meal from unfamiliar ingredients is receiving an education that appears on no transcript. It is often the most durable part of the degree. Long after the details of a module have faded, the knowledge that one can survive — even flourish — among strangers remains.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 258 words

Today, more and more students go to study at universities in other countries, such as Australia, the USA or Japan. This trend has some clear advantages, but it also has some disadvantages.

The first advantage is the quality of education. Some foreign universities have modern equipment, famous teachers and very good libraries. Students can learn new knowledge that is not available in their own country. Another advantage is language. When students live abroad, they use a foreign language every day, so they can improve it very quickly. Also, they become more independent because they must cook, pay bills and solve problems by themselves.

However, studying abroad also has disadvantages. The biggest problem is money. Tuition fees, rent and food in rich countries are very expensive, so many families have to borrow money or use all their savings. In addition, some students feel lonely and homesick. The food, the weather and the culture are different, and at first it is difficult to make friends. For example, my cousin studied in Canada and felt very sad during her first winter there.

There is also a problem for the home country. Some students find good jobs abroad and never come back. As a result, their country loses clever and skilled people.

In conclusion, studying abroad can give students a better education, better language skills and more independence. However, it is expensive and can be difficult emotionally. I think it is a good choice if students prepare well and plan to use their skills to help their own country in the future.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 273 words

In recent decades, the number of students enrolling at universities abroad has risen considerably. While this trend offers significant benefits to the students themselves, it also creates financial, personal and national challenges that should not be overlooked.

The most obvious advantage is access to high-quality education. Leading universities in countries such as the United Kingdom or Australia often have better facilities and research opportunities than those available at home. Moreover, living in a foreign country allows students to become fluent in another language and to develop independence, since they must manage their own finances and daily lives. These experiences are highly valued by employers, particularly multinational companies that need staff who can work comfortably with people from different cultures.

On the other hand, studying abroad is extremely expensive. In addition to tuition fees, which for international students are usually much higher than for local students, families must pay for accommodation, travel and living costs. Many students also experience culture shock and loneliness, which can affect both their mental health and their academic performance. From a national perspective, a further drawback is "brain drain": when talented graduates choose to stay abroad, their home country loses the skills it invested in developing.

Nevertheless, this last problem can be reduced if governments create attractive opportunities for graduates to return. Scholarships that require recipients to work in their home country for a number of years are one example of such a policy.

In conclusion, studying abroad brings valuable academic and personal benefits, but it is costly for families and potentially harmful for developing countries. With careful planning and sensible government policies, however, the advantages can be maximised.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 275 words

The steady growth in international student mobility reflects both the globalisation of higher education and the aspirations of an expanding middle class in many developing countries. The benefits of studying abroad accrue largely to individuals, while its costs are shared more widely; whether the trend is positive overall therefore depends largely on what happens after graduation.

For students themselves, the advantages are considerable. Beyond access to well-resourced institutions, an overseas education typically delivers fluency in a second language and a degree of intercultural competence that is difficult to acquire in any other way. The experience of navigating an unfamiliar society — its bureaucracy, academic conventions and social codes — fosters a resilience that employers increasingly prize.

The disadvantages, however, are equally real. The financial burden is substantial, particularly as international students commonly pay far higher fees than domestic ones, and it can place considerable strain on families. Isolation and culture shock may also undermine academic performance, especially during the first year. More significantly, when graduates choose to settle permanently abroad, their home countries lose skilled workers whose early education they funded — a loss felt most acutely in sectors such as healthcare.

Yet the notion of "brain drain" may be too pessimistic. Graduates who eventually return often bring not only expertise but also international networks and investment; Taiwan's technology industry, for instance, is frequently said to have been built partly by engineers returning from American universities. The challenge for sending countries is to make return an attractive option.

Overall, studying abroad offers clear benefits to individuals, and its costs to home countries are not inevitable. With policies that encourage graduates to return, brain drain can become brain circulation.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 274 words

Every year, millions of students board planes with a suitcase, a visa and a family's savings. Most return with more than a degree. Whether their departure represents a gain or a loss, however, depends less on the students themselves than on the countries they leave — and on whether those countries give them a reason to come back.

The advantages for the individual are difficult to overstate. An overseas education offers not only academic resources that may be unavailable at home but also an education in being foreign: in misreading the joke, mispronouncing the street name and learning, sometimes painfully, to ask for help. Few experiences teach adaptability so efficiently, and none can be replicated in a classroom at home. Employers who prize "international experience" are, whether they realise it or not, paying for precisely this.

The disadvantages are less evenly distributed. Families may spend a lifetime's savings on a single degree, and the student who arrives in an unfamiliar winter without friends or fluent English can find the experience isolating rather than enriching. The heaviest cost, though, may fall on the home country, which loses the doctors and engineers it educated at public expense when they settle abroad.

Yet brain drain is a description of a moment, not a destiny. Talent that leaves can return, and often does, carrying skills, capital and connections that no domestic university could have supplied. The countries that have benefited most from their emigrants are those that made coming home worthwhile.

Studying abroad, then, is neither simply an opportunity nor simply a loss. It is a bet — placed by the student, but settled by the country they left behind.

## 4. Single-Sex or Mixed Schools

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Education

> **Question:** Some people think that boys and girls should be educated in separate schools, while others believe that mixed schools are better for children. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** single-sex school · co-educational (mixed) school · gender stereotypes · social skills · distraction · academic performance

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: mixed schools prepare children for a mixed adult world and challenge stereotypes.*

**🟡 B1:** I think mixed schools are better because they are more like real life. In the future, students will work with both men and women, so they need to learn how to communicate with each other. In a mixed class, boys and girls can share ideas and become friends. For example, in my school, we often did group projects together, and this helped us understand different opinions. This is an important skill for the future.

**🔴 B2:** The main advantage of co-educational schools is that they reflect the society in which students will eventually live and work. By studying alongside the opposite sex, children learn to communicate, cooperate and resolve disagreements with people who may think differently from them. These social skills are essential in almost every modern workplace. Moreover, mixed classrooms can challenge gender stereotypes, since students see for themselves that both boys and girls can excel in subjects such as physics or literature. Consequently, mixed schools arguably provide a more realistic preparation for adult life.

**🟣 C1:** The strongest case for co-education is that school is not merely a place of academic instruction but a rehearsal for adult society. Workplaces, families and communities are mixed, and the ability to collaborate across gender lines is most naturally acquired early, through daily interaction rather than occasional contact. Separating children may also reinforce the very stereotypes it seeks to avoid, encouraging the assumption that boys and girls learn in fundamentally different ways — a claim for which research offers surprisingly limited support. Mixed schools, in other words, teach equality not as a lesson but as a lived experience.

**⚫ C2:** Stereotypes thrive on distance. A boy who has never been out-argued by a girl in a debate, or a girl who has never watched a boy struggle with fractions, is free to believe whatever the culture says about the other sex. Mixed classrooms do not abolish prejudice, but they supply its natural antidote: daily, unremarkable evidence to the contrary. Such evidence cannot be scheduled into a timetable. It is simply what happens when children learn side by side.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 255 words

In some countries, boys and girls study in separate schools, but in other countries most schools are mixed. People have different opinions about which type of school is better. In this essay, I will discuss both views and give my opinion.

On the one hand, some people believe that single-sex schools are better. They say that boys and girls are less distracted when they study separately, so they can focus on their lessons. Some girls may also feel more confident to speak in class or to study subjects like science and computing when there are no boys in the room. In addition, teachers can use teaching methods that suit only boys or only girls.

On the other hand, many people think that mixed schools are better for children. In real life, men and women live and work together, so children should learn to communicate with each other from a young age. In mixed schools, boys and girls can become friends, share ideas and learn to respect each other. For example, in my school, we often worked in mixed groups, and this helped us to understand different opinions.

In my opinion, mixed schools are better. Although some students may be distracted, good teachers can control the class. I believe that social skills and respect are very important, and children learn these skills best when boys and girls study together.

In conclusion, both types of school have some advantages. However, I think mixed schools prepare children better for adult life, because the real world is mixed too.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 268 words

Whether boys and girls should be educated together or separately is a question that continues to divide parents and educators. Although single-sex schools may offer certain academic advantages, I believe that mixed schools provide a more complete preparation for life.

Supporters of single-sex education argue that it allows students to concentrate more effectively. Without the social pressures of a mixed environment, teenagers may be less concerned about impressing the opposite sex and more focused on their studies. Some also claim that girls in particular gain confidence in subjects traditionally dominated by boys, such as physics and engineering, when they are taught separately. Furthermore, teachers may be able to adapt their methods to the interests of a single group.

However, those in favour of co-education point out that school is not only about exam results. Since men and women work alongside each other in almost every profession, children need to learn how to communicate and cooperate across genders from an early age. Mixed schools also have the potential to break down stereotypes, because students can see for themselves that both boys and girls are capable of excelling in any subject. Separating them, by contrast, may reinforce the idea that they are fundamentally different.

In my view, the social benefits of mixed schools outweigh the possible academic advantages of single-sex education. Moreover, the higher results of some single-sex schools may simply reflect the fact that many of them are selective or private, rather than the absence of the opposite sex.

In conclusion, while single-sex schools may suit some individual students, mixed schools generally offer a more realistic and balanced education.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 268 words

Debates about single-sex education are often conducted as though the evidence were clear-cut. It is not. Having weighed the competing claims, I would argue that mixed schools should remain the norm, although there is a reasonable case for allowing families some degree of choice.

Advocates of single-sex schooling typically make two claims. The first is that separating boys and girls reduces distraction and social pressure, particularly during adolescence. The second is that it frees students from gendered expectations, enabling girls to pursue mathematics and science, or boys to pursue languages and the arts, without fear of judgement. Both claims have intuitive appeal, and some individual students undoubtedly thrive in such environments.

Yet the apparent academic advantage of single-sex schools largely disappears under closer scrutiny. Many such schools are selective, fee-paying or both, so their strong results often reflect the backgrounds of their students rather than the absence of the opposite sex. Once these factors are taken into account, the differences in attainment are, in many studies, modest at best.

What mixed schools offer instead is harder to measure but no less important. Adult life is conducted in mixed company, and the ability to collaborate and communicate respectfully across genders is most naturally acquired through everyday interaction. Separating children may, ironically, reinforce the very stereotypes it seeks to overcome, by implying that boys and girls require fundamentally different treatment.

It tends to energise those who believe they can win while discouraging those who suspect they cannot — and the latter group is, almost by definition, the majority. Persistent comparison can also erode intrinsic interest in learning, so that students come to value grades rather than understanding. Moreover, in an environment where success is measured relative to others, helping a classmate becomes, in effect, a strategic error.

Cooperation, by contrast, reflects how knowledge and value are actually created in modern societies. Scientific research, engineering and business are overwhelmingly collaborative, and the ability to build on others' ideas is increasingly what distinguishes effective professionals. Structured group work also allows stronger students to consolidate their understanding by explaining concepts to others.

In short, the most promising approach combines the energy of competition with the ethos of cooperation: team-based challenges in which groups compete against a standard or against other teams. In such formats, students experience the excitement of a contest while learning that their success depends on the people around them.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 286 words

Few school rituals are as familiar as the ranking: the list on the classroom wall, the prize for first place, the quiet humiliation of last. Its defenders say it teaches children how the world works. The question worth asking is which world they mean.

The case for competition should be put fairly. Children, like adults, often work harder when something is at stake, and the chance to win can transform a dull exercise into a contest worth caring about. Life beyond school, moreover, is not a cooperative game: universities select, employers reject, and markets reward those who outperform their rivals. A school that pretends otherwise, the argument runs, is not protecting children but misleading them.

Yet competition motivates selectively. It energises the few who expect to win and quietly demoralises the many who expect to lose, and it does so at precisely the age when children are deciding what kind of learners they are. Worse, it teaches a subtle lesson about knowledge itself: that understanding is a private possession, and that explaining something to a classmate is a gift to a rival.

The world most students will actually work in rewards the opposite habit. Hospitals, laboratories and companies depend on people who share what they know, and even fiercely competitive firms are, internally, exercises in cooperation. The skill that matters is not outscoring the person at the next desk but helping the whole group think better.

Schools need not abolish competition; they need to aim it outwards. Let teams compete against a standard, a deadline or another school, and children gain the thrill of the contest without learning to see each other as obstacles. That is not a softer education. It is a more accurate one.

## 9. Taking a Gap Year

**Type:** Two-part question · **Theme:** Education

> **Question:** Some young people take a year off between finishing school and starting university, in order to travel or to work. Why do they do this? Is this a positive or negative development?

**Useful vocabulary:** gap year · work experience · maturity · career direction · lose momentum · broaden horizons

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: a gap year helps students choose the right course.*

**🟡 B1:** Many young people take a gap year because they are not sure what to study. After twelve years at school, they feel tired and need time to think. If they work or travel for one year, they can learn about different jobs and find out what they really like. For example, my cousin worked in a hotel for a year and then decided to study tourism. So a gap year can help students choose the right course.

**🔴 B2:** One of the main reasons students take a gap year is to gain clarity about their future. Many school leavers are expected to choose a university course at seventeen or eighteen, often before they have any real experience of the working world. By spending a year working or volunteering, they can explore different fields and discover where their interests lie. As a result, they are more likely to choose a course that suits them and less likely to drop out or change subjects later, which saves both time and money.

**🟣 C1:** For many young people, a gap year is less an escape from education than a deliberate attempt to make better use of it. Asked to commit to a degree — and often to substantial debt — at an age when few have any first-hand experience of work, some understandably seek a period of exploration first. Time spent in employment or volunteering can turn vague interests into concrete goals, and students who arrive at university with a clear sense of purpose tend to be more motivated than those who enrolled simply because it was the expected next step.

**⚫ C2:** We ask eighteen-year-olds to choose a career at the precise moment they know least about the world of work, and then express surprise when they choose badly. The gap year is, in part, a quiet protest against this arrangement. A year behind a café counter or on a building site will not tell a young person what they want to be. It will, very often, tell them what they do not want to be — which, at eighteen, is the more useful discovery.

After that, she chose to study business, and she really enjoys it.

In conclusion, students take a gap year to rest, to think about their future and to save money. I believe it is a positive development if students have a clear plan and use the year well.

Students are often required to choose a degree course at seventeen or eighteen, before they have any real experience of the working world, and many are unsure whether their chosen subject is right for them. A year spent working or volunteering allows them to explore their interests before committing to several years of study. In addition, rising tuition fees mean that some students need to work in order to save money for university. Others simply want to travel and experience different cultures while they have few responsibilities.

In my view, a gap year is generally beneficial. Students who have worked or travelled independently tend to return more mature, confident and motivated. Having a clearer sense of direction, they are also less likely to drop out or change courses, which saves both time and money. #### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 296 words

The gap year, once associated mainly with affluent students backpacking abroad, has become an increasingly mainstream choice. Its growing popularity reflects both the pressures placed on school leavers and a wider questioning of the assumption that education should proceed without interruption. On balance, I regard it as a positive development, although its value depends heavily on how the year is spent.

The most significant driver is uncertainty. School leavers are asked to commit to a degree — and often to substantial debt — at an age when few have any first-hand experience of work. A year of employment or volunteering can transform vague interests into informed decisions. Financial necessity plays a part as well: for some students, a year of full-time work is the only realistic way to fund their studies. For others, the motivation is simply the desire for independence after more than a decade of structured schooling.

The benefits are considerable. Students who arrive at university after a purposeful gap year often display greater maturity and clearer goals, and they may be less likely to abandon their courses. The skills acquired through work — reliability, teamwork, the ability to deal with customers or colleagues — are also valued by employers.

Nevertheless, two reservations are worth noting. First, an unstructured year can lead to a loss of academic momentum, making the return to study harder than anticipated. Second, the more glamorous forms of the gap year, such as international travel or unpaid volunteering abroad, are largely accessible only to the wealthy, which risks turning a developmental opportunity into another marker of privilege.

In short, the gap year is a positive development when it is purposeful and accessible. The challenge is to ensure that its benefits are available to all students, not only to those who can afford to travel.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 300 words

The gap year is often described as a pause, but for many young people it is the first decision they have made entirely for themselves. That, I think, is the key to understanding both why it has become popular and why, on the whole, it should be welcomed.

The reasons are not hard to find. Education systems typically require eighteen-year-olds to commit to a subject, an institution and, in many countries, a sizeable debt, before they have had any meaningful contact with adult working life. Some take a year out because they need to earn the fees; others because they are exhausted after twelve years of uninterrupted assessment; many because they are simply not sure, and have the good sense to admit it.

The benefits of that honesty are substantial. A year spent serving customers, labouring on a building site or volunteering abroad offers something schools find hard to provide: consequences that are real rather than graded. Young people return, very often, with sharper motives and a clearer sense of which doors they want to open, and universities tend to find them more focused students.

There are, of course, risks. Momentum can be lost; a year without structure can drift into two. And the version of the gap year that appears in brochures — trekking across continents, volunteering in distant villages — is a luxury good, available chiefly to those whose families can pay for it. A developmental opportunity that only the affluent can afford risks becoming one more way in which advantage compounds itself.

The gap year, then, is a positive development with a fairness problem. Its existence is a quiet admission that the conveyor belt from school to university runs too fast. The better response is not to discourage the pause, but to make a meaningful one available to everyone.

## 10. Art and Music in Schools

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Education

> **Question:** Some people believe that subjects such as art and music are a waste of time in school, and that students should focus on subjects like mathematics and science. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** creativity · self-expression · well-rounded education · STEM subjects · creative industries · well-being

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: art and music develop creativity, which is useful in every job.*

**🟡 B1:** Art and music help students to be creative. In these lessons, students can use their imagination and find new ideas. This skill is useful in many jobs, not only for artists. For example, engineers and designers need creative ideas to make new products. Also, drawing or playing music can help students relax after difficult lessons. So art and music are not a waste of time.

**🔴 B2:** Far from being a waste of time, arts subjects develop creativity, a skill that is increasingly valued in the modern economy. When students compose a piece of music or design a painting, they learn to experiment, take risks and find original solutions — abilities that are just as useful in business or engineering as in the arts. Many successful technology products, for instance, depend as much on good design as on technical performance. Therefore, removing art and music from the curriculum could leave students less prepared for the future.

**🟣 C1:** The assumption that arts subjects are a luxury rests on a narrow conception of what the economy values. As routine technical tasks are increasingly automated, the capacities that remain distinctively human — imagination, aesthetic judgement, the ability to frame problems in new ways — are becoming more rather than less important. These are precisely the capacities that art and music cultivate. To cut the arts in the name of employability is therefore to misread the direction in which employment is moving.

**⚫ C2:** It is a curious kind of realism that prepares children for the future by removing the subjects machines find hardest to imitate. Calculation, once the proof of a sharp mind, is now the cheapest thing a computer does; judging whether a melody moves an audience, or whether a design feels right in the hand, remains stubbornly human. Art and music are not the decorative edge of the curriculum. In an automated economy, they may turn out to be close to its centre.

As governments seek to strengthen their competitiveness in science and technology, arts subjects are often the first to be reduced when timetables come under pressure. The argument that art and music are a waste of time, however, rests on a narrow and, I would argue, outdated conception of what education is for. I therefore disagree with it strongly.

It would be disingenuous to deny that scientific and mathematical literacy are essential. Economies increasingly depend on technical expertise, and students who leave school without a firm grounding in these subjects are at a genuine disadvantage. Yet the value of mathematics and science does not imply that the arts are dispensable; the two are complementary rather than competing.

The most compelling case for the arts is that they cultivate capacities which other subjects tend to neglect. Composing, drawing and performing require students to generate original ideas, tolerate uncertainty and refine their work through repeated revision. As routine technical tasks are increasingly automated, these distinctively human capacities are becoming more rather than less valuable. The creative industries — from film and design to game development — also represent a substantial source of employment in their own right.

Equally important is the contribution of the arts to students' well-being and sense of identity. #### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 292 words

Every few years, a government discovers that its schools are falling behind in mathematics, and every few years the music room pays for it. The logic seems impeccable: if the future belongs to scientists and engineers, why spend precious hours on painting and song? The answer is that this logic mistakes the arts for decoration, when they are closer to a discipline of thought.

The case for concentrating on mathematics and science is not foolish, and it deserves a fair statement. Technical knowledge is cumulative and unforgiving; a student who falls behind in algebra at thirteen may never catch up, and national prosperity increasingly depends on scientific skill. Time spent on one subject is, inescapably, time not spent on another.

Yet the arts are not a holiday from rigour. Learning an instrument demands hours of disciplined practice; composing, drawing or directing a scene requires students to generate ideas, judge them honestly and revise them repeatedly — precisely the habits that scientific research demands. Many celebrated scientists have been passionate musicians or artists, and it seems unlikely that this is pure coincidence.

There is also the matter of what schools are for. They prepare children for work, certainly, but also for a life in which work is only one part. The adult who can lose themselves in a piece of music, or see a city differently after studying its architecture, has gained something no examination measures and no employer pays for. A school that strips this away is not producing more efficient citizens; it is producing narrower ones.

I disagree, therefore, that art and music are a waste of time. The more wasteful course would be to spend twelve years educating young people while leaving them unable to make, or even notice, anything beautiful.
