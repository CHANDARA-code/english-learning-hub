## 81. Overcrowded Tourist Destinations

**Type:** Problem and solution · **Theme:** Tourism

> **Question:** Some popular tourist destinations are becoming overcrowded. What problems does this cause, and what can be done to solve them?

**Useful vocabulary:** overtourism · visitor limits · tourist tax · local residents · environmental damage · short-term rentals

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: limiting or pricing visits protects places from too many tourists.*

**🟡 B1:** One solution is to limit the number of visitors. Some famous places now allow only a certain number of people each day, or they close for some time so nature can recover. For example, Maya Bay in Thailand was closed for several years because too many tourists damaged the beach and the coral. When it opened again, the number of visitors was controlled. This helped the environment to recover.

**🔴 B2:** An effective way to manage overcrowding is to control the number of visitors. Some destinations have introduced daily limits, advance booking systems or entry fees that rise during peak periods. Thailand's Maya Bay, made famous by a Hollywood film, was closed for several years after mass tourism severely damaged its coral reefs; it reopened with strict limits on visitor numbers. Similarly, Venice has experimented with charging day-trippers an entrance fee on busy days. Such measures protect fragile sites while still allowing tourism to continue.

**🟣 C1:** The most direct response to overtourism is to manage demand rather than simply accommodate it. Daily visitor caps, timed-entry systems and variable pricing can keep numbers within what a site can sustainably absorb, distributing visits more evenly across the day and the year. Thailand's decision to close Maya Bay for several years to allow its reef ecosystem to recover, and then to reopen it under strict limits, showed that governments are prepared to prioritise long-term preservation over short-term revenue. Venice's trial of an entry fee for day-trippers reflects a similar logic: a place that belongs to everyone may need to be rationed to survive.

**⚫ C2:** Tourism is usually priced as if every visitor imposed the same cost, whether they arrive on a quiet weekday in the rainy season or on a holiday weekend alongside fifty thousand others. They do not. A visitor at the peak of the crowd adds to congestion, wear and waste in a way that a visitor at a quiet time does not. Charging more at busy times, as Venice has begun to do for day-trippers, simply asks tourists to pay for the pressure they add — and encourages many to come when the place can breathe.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 293 words

Some famous places in the world, such as Angkor Wat in Cambodia, Venice in Italy and some beaches in Thailand, are visited by very large numbers of tourists every year. Too many tourists can cause serious problems. In this essay, I will explain these problems and suggest some solutions.

The first problem is damage to the environment and historical sites. When millions of people walk on old temples or swim at beautiful beaches, they can damage the stones, the coral and the plants. Tourists also leave a lot of rubbish.

The second problem is that life becomes difficult for local people. In some cities, there are so many tourists that local people cannot walk easily in the streets. Also, prices for houses, food and rent go up, because many homes become hotels or holiday apartments. Some local people must move away.

The third problem is that the experience is not enjoyable for tourists. When a place is too crowded, tourists must wait in long lines and cannot enjoy the beauty of the place.

There are several solutions. Firstly, the government can limit the number of visitors each day or ask them to book tickets online. For example, Maya Bay in Thailand was closed for several years so that nature could recover, and now only a certain number of people can visit. Secondly, the government can ask tourists to pay a special tax and use the money to protect the place. Thirdly, the government can advertise other places, so tourists do not all go to the same place.

In conclusion, too many tourists can damage the environment, make life difficult for local people and spoil the experience. If governments control the number of tourists and use tourist money wisely, famous places can be protected.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 279 words

The growth of international travel has brought enormous economic benefits to many destinations, but some of the world's most popular places are now suffering from overcrowding, a phenomenon often called "overtourism". This essay will examine the problems this causes and suggest measures to address them.

The most visible problem is damage to the environment and cultural heritage. Large numbers of visitors can erode ancient monuments, damage coral reefs and produce huge amounts of waste, sometimes causing irreversible harm. A second problem concerns local communities. In cities such as Venice and Barcelona, residents complain that crowds of tourists make everyday life difficult, while the conversion of homes into short-term holiday rentals pushes up housing costs and forces locals to move out. Finally, overcrowding can reduce the quality of the visitor experience itself, as long queues and packed streets make it hard to appreciate the destination.

Several measures could help to manage these problems. Firstly, authorities can limit visitor numbers through daily caps, advance booking systems or timed entry. Thailand's Maya Bay, for example, was closed for several years to allow its damaged ecosystem to recover and was reopened with strict limits. Secondly, tourist taxes or entry fees, such as the charge Venice has introduced for day-trippers on busy days, can reduce demand at peak times and fund conservation. Thirdly, regulating short-term rentals would help to protect housing for local residents. Finally, governments can promote less well-known destinations to spread the benefits of tourism more evenly.

In conclusion, overtourism damages the environment, harms local communities and spoils the visitor experience. By limiting numbers, charging appropriate fees and managing tourism more carefully, destinations can protect themselves while continuing to benefit from visitors.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 291 words

Tourism is among the world's largest industries, and for many destinations it is an economic lifeline. Yet the very success of certain places — historic cities, iconic beaches, ancient monuments — has produced a new problem: they are being visited beyond their capacity to cope. The consequences of overtourism are environmental, social and economic, and addressing them requires a shift from maximising visitor numbers to managing them.

The environmental costs are often the most visible. Fragile ecosystems and ancient structures were never designed to withstand millions of visitors, and the cumulative effects of footfall, waste and pollution can be irreversible. The social costs are less obvious but equally serious. In cities such as Venice and Barcelona, the concentration of visitors in historic centres has strained infrastructure, crowded out everyday life and, through the spread of short-term rentals, driven up rents to the point where residents are displaced. Paradoxically, overcrowding can also undermine the tourism economy itself, degrading the experience that attracted visitors in the first place.

The solutions involve managing demand, redistributing it and capturing its value more fairly. Daily caps, timed-entry systems and seasonal pricing can keep visitor numbers within sustainable limits; Thailand's closure and subsequent controlled reopening of Maya Bay demonstrated that authorities are willing to prioritise long-term preservation. Tourist taxes and entry fees, such as those Venice has trialled for day-trippers, can moderate demand while funding conservation and local services. Regulating short-term rentals can protect housing for residents, while investment in less-visited regions can spread both visitors and economic benefits more widely.

In short, overtourism is not an argument against tourism but against unmanaged tourism. Destinations that treat their capacity as finite and plan accordingly are more likely to preserve both their heritage and the livelihoods that depend on it.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 306 words

There is a cruel irony at the heart of modern tourism: the more beautiful a place is, the more likely it is to be damaged by admiration. Venice, Barcelona, the beaches of southern Thailand, the temples of Angkor — each attracts visitors precisely because of qualities that crowds gradually erode.

The damage takes three forms. The first is physical. Coral reefs are broken by careless swimmers and boat anchors; ancient stone is worn away by millions of feet; waste accumulates faster than small communities can remove it. The second is social. In the most visited cities, residents find their streets impassable, their neighbourhood shops replaced by souvenir stalls and their homes converted into holiday rentals, until the living city becomes a stage set. The third is, in a sense, self-inflicted: overcrowding degrades the very experience tourists have travelled to enjoy. Nobody's dream holiday involves queuing behind thousands of others for a photograph.

The remedies require a change of mindset — from treating visitors as an unlimited resource to treating capacity as a finite one. Some places are already making this shift. Thailand closed Maya Bay for several years to let its reef recover and reopened it with strict limits; Venice has begun charging day-trippers to enter on its busiest days. Timed tickets, visitor caps and prices that rise at peak times can spread demand more evenly, and the revenue can fund conservation and support residents. Regulating short-term rentals can keep homes for the people who live there, while promoting lesser-known destinations can share both visitors and income more widely.

None of this is anti-tourism. It is, rather, the recognition that a place which belongs to everyone must sometimes be rationed so that it can continue to belong to anyone at all. Limits may feel unwelcoming. The alternative is to welcome everyone until there is nothing left worth visiting.

## 82. Globalisation and Cultural Identity

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Culture and globalisation

> **Question:** Some people think that globalisation is causing countries to lose their cultural identity. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** globalisation · cultural identity · cultural exchange · traditions · homogenisation · soft power

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: culture now travels in many directions, not only from the West.*

**🟡 B1:** Globalisation does not only bring Western culture to Asia. Asian culture also goes to the world. For example, Korean music and TV dramas are now popular in many countries, and people everywhere eat Japanese, Thai and Vietnamese food. Some foreigners even learn Khmer dance and cooking. So globalisation can help countries to share their culture, not only lose it.

**🔴 B2:** It is a mistake to assume that globalisation means only the spread of Western culture. Cultural influences now travel in many directions. South Korean music and television dramas have gained enormous international audiences, Japanese anime is watched around the world, and Asian cuisines are enjoyed in cities everywhere. This suggests that globalisation can actually strengthen national identity by giving countries a global platform to share their traditions, music and food. Rather than disappearing, many cultures are becoming more visible than ever before.

**🟣 C1:** The assumption that globalisation is a one-way process of Westernisation looks increasingly outdated. The global success of South Korean music and film, the worldwide popularity of Japanese animation and the spread of Southeast Asian cuisine demonstrate that cultural influence now flows in multiple directions. Far from erasing national identity, global networks have enabled smaller cultures to reach audiences their predecessors could never have imagined — and, in the process, have often strengthened pride in distinctive traditions at home.

**⚫ C2:** Twenty years ago, few would have predicted that some of the most successful pop acts on the planet would sing in Korean, or that audiences from Brazil to Nigeria would pick up Korean phrases from television dramas. Globalisation, it turns out, is not a one-way road from Hollywood to everywhere else. It is a crowded crossroads, where the smaller cultures that seize the moment can reach more people than empires once did. The danger is not that local culture will be erased by global culture, but that it will fail to take part in it.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 267 words

Today, people all over the world watch the same films, eat at the same fast-food restaurants and wear the same brands of clothes. Some people think that because of globalisation, countries are losing their own culture. I partly agree with this idea.

On the one hand, globalisation can damage local culture. Many young people prefer foreign music, films and fashion to their own traditional culture. For example, in my country, many teenagers listen to Korean and American music, but they do not know much about traditional Khmer music. Also, some traditional festivals, clothes and foods are becoming less popular in big cities. In addition, English is becoming more important, and some young people do not speak their own language well.

On the other hand, globalisation does not always destroy culture. It also helps countries to share their culture with the world. For example, Korean music and TV dramas are now famous in many countries, and people everywhere enjoy Asian food, such as Thai curry and Vietnamese pho. Tourists also come to Cambodia to see Angkor Wat and traditional dance. Moreover, people can use the internet to learn about their own culture and history.

In my opinion, globalisation changes culture, but it does not make it disappear. Countries can protect their identity if they teach traditional culture at school, support local artists and celebrate traditional festivals.

In conclusion, I partly agree that globalisation can make countries lose some of their culture. However, it also gives countries a chance to share their culture with the world. If people are proud of their traditions, their cultural identity will stay strong.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 283 words

As a result of globalisation, the same brands, films and music can now be found in almost every country. This has led some people to argue that nations are losing their cultural identity. Although I accept that globalisation poses real challenges to local cultures, I believe that it does not necessarily destroy cultural identity and can even strengthen it.

There is some truth in the argument that globalisation threatens local cultures. The global dominance of powerful industries, such as Hollywood films and international fast-food chains, means that young people in many countries grow up consuming the same cultural products. As a result, traditional music, crafts, clothing and even languages may be neglected, especially in cities. Some festivals have also become commercialised, losing their original meaning.

However, globalisation is not simply a process of one culture replacing others. Cultural influences now travel in many directions. South Korea, for example, has become a global cultural power through its music, films and television dramas, while Japanese, Thai and Indian cuisines are popular throughout the world. Globalisation can therefore give smaller countries an opportunity to share their traditions with a global audience. Moreover, cultures have always changed through contact with others; adopting some foreign customs does not mean abandoning one's own identity.

In my view, the key is balance. Governments and communities can protect cultural identity by teaching traditional arts and history in schools, supporting local artists and preserving heritage sites, while still benefiting from the ideas and opportunities that globalisation brings.

In conclusion, although globalisation can weaken some traditions, I do not agree that it is causing countries to lose their cultural identity. Cultures are adapting and, in many cases, reaching new audiences around the world.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 298 words

Globalisation has undeniably made the world's cities look and sound more alike: the same coffee chains, the same smartphones, the same songs streaming from the same platforms. It is understandable, therefore, that many fear the erosion of distinctive national cultures. I would argue, however, that this fear, while not groundless, rests on an oversimplified view of both globalisation and culture.

The pessimistic case has some merit. Cultural markets are dominated by a small number of powerful producers, and the economies of scale enjoyed by global entertainment and consumer industries can marginalise local alternatives. Traditional crafts, performing arts and minority languages, in particular, may struggle to compete for the attention of young people immersed in global media. Where such forms disappear, something genuinely irreplaceable is lost.

Yet the assumption that globalisation is a one-way process of Westernisation is increasingly difficult to sustain. The international success of South Korean music and cinema, the worldwide popularity of Japanese animation and the spread of Asian cuisines demonstrate that cultural influence now flows in multiple directions. Global networks have enabled smaller cultures to reach audiences that were previously inaccessible, frequently strengthening pride in distinctive traditions at home.

More fundamentally, the premise that cultural identity is a fixed inheritance, threatened by any external influence, misunderstands how cultures function. Cultures have always evolved through trade, migration and exchange; what we now regard as traditional was often once foreign. Identity persists not through isolation but through the capacity to absorb influences selectively while retaining a sense of continuity.

In conclusion, globalisation poses real challenges to certain cultural practices, and deliberate efforts to preserve vulnerable traditions are justified. But the claim that countries are losing their cultural identity overstates the case. Cultures are changing, as they always have — and many are more globally visible than ever before.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 317 words

Every generation fears that its culture is being diluted by foreign influence. The Victorians worried about French novels; mid-century Europeans worried about American films; today, many worry that globalisation is turning the world into a single shopping mall. The fear is understandable. I nevertheless believe that it misunderstands both culture and globalisation.

The pessimists are not imagining things. Walk through the centre of almost any major city and you will find the same coffee chains, the same fashion brands and the same songs. Global industries possess marketing budgets that local artists cannot match, and some traditions — particular crafts, dances, dialects — are genuinely at risk of disappearing because young people are drawn elsewhere. Those losses are real, and they deserve active protection.

But the premise that globalisation is a one-way flow from the West to everyone else has been overturned by events. Nigerian Afrobeats fills dance floors in London and New York; Japanese animation shapes the imaginations of children on every continent; the cuisines of Thailand, Vietnam and India are ordinary in cities thousands of kilometres away. The same networks that carry Hollywood outwards carry the rest of the world back in.

More importantly, the fear rests on a mistaken picture of culture as a museum collection that must be kept sealed. Cultures have always been conversations, borrowing and adapting across borders. Much of what any nation now regards as traditional — its instruments, its dishes, even its religions — arrived at some point as a foreign import and was transformed into something distinctively local.

So I disagree, in large part. Globalisation is changing cultures, as trade and migration always have, and some treasures need deliberate protection. But identity is not lost simply because a teenager in Phnom Penh listens to Korean music. It is lost only when a people stop caring about what is theirs — and globalisation, by giving them an audience, may give them more reason to care.

## 83. English as a Global Language

**Type:** Advantages and disadvantages (outweigh) · **Theme:** Language and globalisation

> **Question:** English is becoming the main language of international communication. Do the advantages of this outweigh the disadvantages?

**Useful vocabulary:** lingua franca · international communication · linguistic diversity · access to knowledge · native-speaker advantage · bilingual education

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: a shared language opens doors to education, jobs and knowledge.*

**🟡 B1:** Knowing English opens many doors. Most information on the internet, many university courses and many good jobs use English. If people in Cambodia speak English well, they can study abroad, work for international companies and talk to tourists. For example, my cousin learned English and got a job in a hotel in Siem Reap with a good salary. So a global language helps people to have better opportunities.

**🔴 B2:** The most significant advantage of English as a global language is that it provides access to opportunities. A large proportion of academic research, online content and international business is conducted in English, so people who speak it can study at foreign universities, read the latest research and work for multinational companies. In developing countries, English skills can make a considerable difference to employment prospects, particularly in sectors such as tourism, technology and finance. A shared language therefore allows talented people from any country to take part in global opportunities.

**🟣 C1:** The chief benefit of a global lingua franca is access. Because much of the world's scientific literature, higher education and international commerce operates in English, proficiency functions as a key to resources that would otherwise remain closed. For individuals in developing economies, it can open employment in tourism, technology and international organisations, and it allows researchers to join global conversations rather than waiting for translations. A shared language, in this sense, reduces the costs of cooperation across borders — though it also confers an unearned advantage on those who happen to be born speaking it.

**⚫ C2:** A shared language is like a common currency: it makes exchange cheaper for everyone who holds it. Contracts can be negotiated, research compared and disasters coordinated without the delay and distortion of translation. But currencies are not distributed equally at birth. Those raised speaking English receive for free what everyone else must spend years earning, and they often forget that their fluency is an inheritance rather than an achievement. The benefits of a global language are real; so is the privilege it quietly confers.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 267 words

Today, English is used around the world in business, science, tourism and on the internet. It has become the main international language. In my opinion, the advantages of this are bigger than the disadvantages.

There are many advantages. Firstly, English helps people from different countries to communicate. For example, a Cambodian business person and a Japanese business person can talk to each other in English. Secondly, English gives people better opportunities. Many good jobs, scholarships and university courses need English. For example, my cousin learned English and got a good job in a hotel in Siem Reap. Thirdly, a lot of information on the internet, and many books and films, are in English. People who know English can learn more and understand the world better.

However, there are also some disadvantages. Firstly, some small languages may disappear, because young people prefer to learn English instead of their own language. Secondly, people who speak English as their first language have an advantage in international business and study, which is not fair for others. Thirdly, when everyone uses English, some cultures may lose their traditions, because language is an important part of culture.

In my opinion, these problems are real, but they can be reduced. Schools should teach English and also teach students their own language and culture well. People can speak English for international communication and their own language at home.

In conclusion, English as a global language has some disadvantages, such as the loss of small languages. However, I believe the advantages are greater, because English helps people to communicate, find good jobs and get more knowledge.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 288 words

English has become the dominant language of international business, science, technology and diplomacy, and it is now studied by hundreds of millions of people worldwide. Although this situation creates some problems, I believe its advantages clearly outweigh its disadvantages.

The most important advantage is that a common language makes international communication far easier. People from different countries can do business, collaborate on research and resolve disputes without always relying on translators. This is particularly valuable in regions such as Southeast Asia, where ASEAN uses English as its working language. Secondly, English provides access to knowledge and opportunities. A large proportion of scientific research and online content is published in English, and English skills can significantly improve people's chances of studying abroad or finding well-paid jobs, especially in tourism, technology and international companies.

However, the dominance of English also has disadvantages. One concern is the threat to linguistic diversity: as English spreads, some minority languages may be used less, particularly by young people, and eventually disappear, taking unique knowledge and traditions with them. Another issue is inequality. Native speakers of English enjoy a significant advantage in international academic and professional competition, while non-native speakers must invest years of effort and money to reach the same level. There is also a risk that the spread of English brings the cultural influence of English-speaking countries with it.

Nevertheless, these problems can be managed. Bilingual education allows people to learn English while maintaining their own language and culture, and many countries have successfully promoted both.

In conclusion, although the global dominance of English raises concerns about linguistic diversity and fairness, the benefits for communication, education and economic opportunity are greater. With policies that protect local languages, the advantages clearly outweigh the disadvantages.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 316 words

English now occupies a position without historical precedent: it is the primary language of international science, business, aviation, diplomacy and the internet, spoken as a second or foreign language by far more people than speak it natively. The consequences of this dominance are mixed, but I would argue that its advantages, on balance, outweigh its disadvantages — provided that its spread is not allowed to come at the expense of linguistic diversity.

The principal advantage is the reduction of barriers to communication and cooperation. A shared lingua franca allows scientists, businesses and governments to interact directly, lowering the costs of collaboration across borders; ASEAN's adoption of English as its working language reflects this pragmatic logic. For individuals, particularly in developing economies, English proficiency provides access to higher education, international employment and the vast body of knowledge published in the language. In this sense, a global language can function as an instrument of opportunity and mobility.

The disadvantages, however, are significant. The dominance of English contributes to the marginalisation of smaller languages, as parents and young people rationally invest in the language that offers the greatest economic return. Since languages carry distinctive knowledge, literature and ways of perceiving the world, their decline represents a genuine cultural loss. The system also entrenches inequality: native speakers enjoy an unearned advantage in academic publishing, international negotiation and employment, while non-native speakers must devote considerable resources to acquiring a proficiency that others possess by accident of birth.

These disadvantages, though, are not inevitable consequences of a lingua franca but of how it is managed. Bilingual education, institutional support for minority languages and greater tolerance of varied, non-native forms of English in international settings can preserve diversity and reduce inequity.

In conclusion, a global language offers substantial benefits for communication, knowledge and opportunity. Its costs are real but can be mitigated, and they argue for multilingualism alongside English rather than against English itself.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 318 words

Throughout history, great powers have left behind languages that outlived them: Greek across the eastern Mediterranean, Latin across Europe, Arabic across a vast arc of Asia and Africa. English is the latest and most far-reaching of these shared tongues, and like its predecessors, it brings both a gift and a cost. On balance, I believe the gift is larger.

The gift is connection. A shared language allows a Cambodian engineer, a Brazilian doctor and a Finnish researcher to read the same paper, argue over the same data and build on each other's work without waiting for a translation. ASEAN conducts its business in English not out of admiration for Britain or America but out of practicality: ten nations with a dozen major languages need a common medium. For individuals, English can be a ladder out of poverty — into a hotel in Siem Reap, a technology company in Singapore or a university abroad.

The cost is twofold. The first is diversity. Every language encodes a distinctive way of seeing the world, and as parents calculate that English will serve their children better than a regional tongue, smaller languages fall silent, taking stories, knowledge and humour with them. The second is fairness. Those born into English receive for free a competitive advantage that billions of others must purchase with years of study, and international institutions sometimes mistake native fluency for intelligence.

Neither cost, however, is an argument against a lingua franca; both are arguments for multilingualism. People can use English to speak to the world while using their own languages to speak to one another, and countries that teach both well lose nothing and gain a great deal. International institutions, for their part, could learn to value clear ideas expressed in imperfect English over polished emptiness.

The spread of English, then, is on balance a benefit — provided it adds a language to the world rather than subtracting all the others.

## 84. Disappearing Traditional Crafts

**Type:** Two-part question · **Theme:** Culture and heritage

> **Question:** In many countries, traditional skills and crafts, such as weaving and pottery, are disappearing. Why is this happening? Is it important to preserve them?

**Useful vocabulary:** handicrafts · mass production · artisans · cultural heritage · apprenticeship · handmade products

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: crafts survive when artisans can earn a fair living from them.*

**🟡 B1:** Traditional crafts can survive if they can make money. For example, in Cambodia, some organisations help silk weavers to sell their scarves to tourists and to shops in other countries. The weavers get a fair price, so young people want to learn the skill. When traditional products are beautiful and useful, many people are happy to buy them. So crafts can be both culture and business.

**🔴 B2:** Traditional crafts are most likely to survive when they can provide a decent income. In Cambodia, for instance, several social enterprises work with village silk weavers, helping them to design products for modern customers and to sell them to tourists and international buyers at fair prices. When artisans can earn a reliable living, young people are more willing to learn the skills from older generations. This shows that preservation does not have to depend on charity; it can be built on a sustainable market for high-quality handmade goods.

**🟣 C1:** The most durable way to preserve a traditional craft is to give it an economic future rather than merely a museum display. Where artisans can command fair prices — through tourism, collaboration with contemporary designers or access to international markets — the incentive for young people to learn the craft is restored. Cambodia's silk weaving, which came close to destruction in the late twentieth century, has been revived in part through social enterprises connecting village weavers with buyers who value handmade quality. Preservation that relies solely on subsidies tends to freeze a craft in place; preservation through markets allows it to evolve.

**⚫ C2:** Heritage policies often treat traditional crafts as fragile objects to be displayed. The crafts themselves suggest a different approach. Khmer silk weaving, which came close to disappearing in the late twentieth century, has been revived less by exhibitions than by customers — social enterprises linking village weavers with buyers who will pay for skill. A loom that earns an income attracts apprentices; a loom behind glass attracts only visitors. If a craft is to have a future, it needs a market as much as a memory.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 272 words

In many countries, traditional crafts, such as silk weaving, pottery and wood carving, are disappearing. Fewer and fewer young people learn these skills. There are several reasons for this, and I think it is important to protect these crafts.

There are three main reasons why traditional crafts are disappearing. Firstly, factory products are much cheaper and faster to make. For example, a machine can make hundreds of plastic bowls in one hour, but a potter needs many hours to make one bowl. Secondly, young people do not want to learn traditional skills because the work is difficult and the salary is low. They prefer to work in cities, in offices or factories. Thirdly, people's tastes have changed, and many people prefer modern products.

I believe it is important to preserve traditional crafts. Firstly, they are part of a country's culture and history. They show the skills and creativity of our ancestors. For example, Khmer silk weaving has a long history, and the patterns have special meanings. Secondly, traditional crafts can attract tourists and create jobs in villages. Many tourists like to buy handmade products as souvenirs. Thirdly, handmade products are often better for the environment than factory products.

To protect traditional crafts, the government can support craft villages, teach these skills in schools and help artisans to sell their products online and to tourists. Some organisations in Cambodia already help silk weavers to sell their scarves at a fair price.

In conclusion, traditional crafts are disappearing because factory products are cheaper and young people prefer other jobs. However, I believe they are an important part of our culture and should be protected.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 297 words

## 89. Skilled Workers Leaving Home

**Type:** Problem and solution · **Theme:** Globalisation and work

> **Question:** Many skilled workers, such as doctors and engineers, leave their home countries to work abroad. What problems does this cause for their home countries, and what can be done to solve them?

**Useful vocabulary:** brain drain · remittances · skills shortage · diaspora · return migration · working conditions

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: better conditions at home and support for returners can bring talent back.*

**🟡 B1:** One good solution is to give skilled workers good reasons to stay or come back. If doctors and engineers get fair salaries, modern equipment and good chances to grow in their jobs, many of them will choose to work in their own country. The government can also give special help to people who come back from abroad, such as help to start a business. Then their new skills and experience can help the country.

**🔴 B2:** The most effective long-term solution is to make staying or returning an attractive option. Many skilled professionals leave because of low salaries, poor working conditions, limited equipment and few opportunities for career development. If governments and employers improve these conditions, fewer people will feel the need to emigrate. In addition, special programmes can encourage emigrants to return, for example by offering tax incentives, recognising foreign qualifications or providing support to start businesses. Returning professionals often bring valuable skills, networks and investment, turning a loss into a gain.

**🟣 C1:** Rather than treating emigration purely as a loss to be prevented, sending countries can seek to convert it into "brain circulation". Improving salaries, working conditions and professional development reduces the pressures that drive skilled workers away, while targeted incentives — tax advantages, recognition of foreign qualifications, support for entrepreneurship — can encourage return. Emigrants who remain abroad, moreover, can contribute through investment, knowledge transfer and professional networks. India's technology sector, for instance, is often said to have benefited considerably from engineers who gained experience in the United States and later returned or invested at home.

**⚫ C2:** Emigration need not be a one-way exit. The engineer who leaves for Silicon Valley may return a decade later with capital, contacts and the confidence to build a company at home; the doctor who stays abroad may train colleagues remotely or fund a clinic in her home province. Whether departure becomes a loss or an investment depends largely on the country left behind — on whether it keeps the door open, recognises what was learned elsewhere and offers something worth returning to. A diaspora is only a drain if its homeland treats it as one.

This process of "glocalisation" suggests that the relationship between global corporations and local cultures is one of negotiation rather than simple domination: to sell everywhere, a brand must learn to become, at least partly, local.

**⚫ C2:** The global brand that arrives to conquer a local market often ends up being quietly conquered by it. A hamburger chain that serves no beef in India, a coffee company that adds green-tea drinks for Japanese customers, a fried-chicken chain that serves rice across Southeast Asia — each is evidence that local taste has bargaining power. Globalisation, seen from the dining table, is less a steamroller than a negotiation, and the locals do not always lose.
