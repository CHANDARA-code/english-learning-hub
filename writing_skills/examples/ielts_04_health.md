## 31. Taxing Unhealthy Food

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Health and food

> **Question:** Some people think that governments should put a tax on unhealthy foods, such as sugary drinks and fast food, in order to improve public health. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** sugar tax · obesity · reformulate a product · low-income households · public health · healthy alternatives

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: a tax makes companies put less sugar in their products.*

**🟡 B1:** A tax on sugary drinks can make companies change their products. If drinks with a lot of sugar are more expensive, companies will make new drinks with less sugar so that people still buy them. For example, after the United Kingdom started a tax on sugary drinks, many companies reduced the sugar in their drinks. This means people drink less sugar without changing their habits.

**🔴 B2:** One of the most effective aspects of taxing unhealthy products is that it encourages manufacturers to change their recipes. When the United Kingdom introduced a levy on sugary soft drinks in 2018, many companies reduced the sugar content of their products before the tax even came into force, in order to avoid paying it. As a result, consumers began to consume less sugar without necessarily changing their shopping habits. This shows that a well-designed tax can improve public health by changing what is sold, not only what people choose to buy.

**🟣 C1:** The most persuasive evidence for food taxes concerns not consumers but producers. When the United Kingdom announced its levy on sugary drinks, a substantial number of manufacturers reformulated their products to fall below the tax threshold before the levy even took effect. The result was a significant reduction in the sugar sold, achieved largely without requiring individuals to alter their behaviour. This matters, because campaigns that rely on persuading millions of people to change their diets have a notoriously poor record, whereas changing the default composition of what is sold works quietly and at scale.

**⚫ C2:** The most successful health taxes are the ones nobody pays. When manufacturers learn that a product will be taxed above a certain sugar threshold, many simply rewrite the recipe to fall below it — and millions of people drink less sugar without ever making a decision to do so. This is the quiet genius of the measure. Public health campaigns ask individuals to swim against a current of cheap, sweet, heavily advertised food. A well-designed tax changes the direction of the current itself.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 279 words

Nowadays, many people eat a lot of fast food and drink sugary drinks, and the number of people with obesity and diabetes is growing. Some people think the government should put a tax on unhealthy food to make people healthier. I mostly agree with this idea.

Firstly, a tax can make people buy less unhealthy food. If a soft drink or a burger is more expensive, some people will buy it less often. They may choose water, fruit or home-cooked food instead. This is especially true for young people, who usually do not have much money.

Secondly, a tax can make companies change their products. If drinks with a lot of sugar have a high tax, companies will try to make drinks with less sugar. For example, after the United Kingdom started a tax on sugary drinks, many companies reduced the sugar in their drinks.

Thirdly, the government can use the tax money to improve health. For example, it can build sports centres, give healthy lunches to schools or pay for hospitals.

On the other hand, this tax can be unfair to poor people. Unhealthy food is often cheaper than healthy food, and poor families may have to pay more for their shopping. Also, some people think the government should not control what people eat.

In my opinion, the government should add a tax on unhealthy food, but it should also make healthy food cheaper, for example fruit and vegetables. Then poor families can still eat well.

In conclusion, I mostly agree that unhealthy food should have a tax, because it can help people to be healthier. However, the government should also help poor families to buy healthy food.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 297 words

Rising rates of obesity, diabetes and heart disease have led many governments to consider taxing unhealthy foods and drinks. While such taxes raise legitimate concerns about fairness and personal freedom, I largely agree that they are a sensible public health measure, particularly when combined with support for healthier choices.

The main argument in favour is that taxes change behaviour. Price is one of the most important factors in people's food choices, so making sugary drinks and highly processed snacks more expensive is likely to reduce their consumption, especially among young people. Moreover, such taxes can encourage manufacturers to make their products healthier. When the United Kingdom introduced a levy on sugary soft drinks, many companies reduced the sugar content of their drinks in order to avoid the tax. In addition, the revenue raised could be used to fund health education, school meals or sports facilities.

On the other hand, critics argue that these taxes are unfair to people on low incomes, who spend a larger proportion of their money on food and may be more likely to buy cheaper, less healthy products. Others believe that individuals, rather than governments, should decide what they eat, and that taxation is an unnecessary intrusion into personal choices.

Although these concerns are understandable, they can be addressed. If the revenue is used to make fruit and vegetables cheaper or to provide free healthy meals in schools, poorer families could actually benefit overall. As for personal freedom, a tax does not ban anything; it simply ensures that prices reflect some of the costs that unhealthy diets impose on public health systems.

In conclusion, taxing unhealthy foods is not a complete solution to poor diets, but it is an effective tool that governments should use, alongside measures that make healthy food more affordable.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 302 words

Governments have long taxed tobacco and alcohol to discourage consumption and offset the costs they impose on health systems. Extending this logic to sugary drinks and heavily processed foods is controversial, but I largely agree that such taxes are justified, provided that they are carefully targeted and accompanied by measures to protect lower-income households.

The strongest argument concerns effectiveness. Price influences food choices, particularly among young people and heavy consumers, and there is growing evidence that well-designed taxes can reduce consumption of targeted products. More striking still is their effect on producers: the announcement of the United Kingdom's soft drinks levy prompted many manufacturers to reformulate their products to avoid the tax, reducing the sugar content of drinks across the market. Such changes reach consumers who might never respond to health campaigns.

Critics raise two main objections. The first is that food taxes are regressive, since poorer households spend a larger share of their income on food. This concern is valid, but it is partly offset by the fact that diet-related illness is also concentrated among lower-income groups, who therefore stand to gain most from healthier diets. Moreover, the revenue can be used to subsidise fruit and vegetables or to fund free school meals, making the overall package progressive.

The second objection is that such taxes infringe personal freedom. Yet they prohibit nothing; they merely adjust prices to reflect costs that would otherwise be borne by public health systems. In a food environment shaped by aggressive marketing and cheap, calorie-dense products, the notion of entirely free individual choice is itself somewhat idealised.

In conclusion, taxes on unhealthy food are not a cure for poor diets, but they are a proportionate and effective instrument. Combined with support for healthier alternatives, they can improve public health without placing an unfair burden on the poor.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 297 words

Nobody chooses obesity, yet millions are steered towards it by a food environment in which the cheapest, most heavily advertised and most convenient calories are also the least healthy. It is in this light that proposals to tax unhealthy food should be judged — not as an attack on personal freedom, but as a modest attempt to tilt a playing field that is already sharply tilted the other way.

The case for such taxes is stronger than their critics admit. Price shapes behaviour, particularly among the young, and targeted taxes on sugary drinks have been associated with falling consumption. More remarkable is their effect on the industry itself. Faced with a levy above a certain sugar threshold, many manufacturers have simply rewritten their recipes, reducing the sugar consumed by millions of people who never changed a single habit.

The objections deserve a serious answer. Food taxes are regressive, it is said, falling hardest on the poor, who spend more of their income on food. True — but diet-related illness is regressive too, concentrated among exactly the same households. A tax whose revenue funds cheaper vegetables and free school lunches can leave poorer families healthier and no worse off. As for freedom, a tax forbids nothing. It merely asks the price of a fizzy drink to tell a little more of the truth about what that drink will eventually cost.

What no tax can do is work alone. Without accessible alternatives — affordable fresh food, safe places to exercise, honest labels — it risks becoming merely a charge on habits that people have few means to change.

I agree, therefore, that governments should tax unhealthy food, provided the proceeds are spent making the healthy choice the easy one. Freedom of choice means little when every option has been designed by someone else.

## 32. Childhood Obesity

**Type:** Problem and solution · **Theme:** Health and food

> **Question:** The number of overweight children is increasing in many countries. What are the causes of this problem, and what solutions can you suggest?

**Useful vocabulary:** sedentary lifestyle · processed food · physical education · portion size · school meals · screen time

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: schools can change what children eat and how much they move.*

**🟡 B1:** Schools can do a lot to help children stay healthy. They can give children healthy lunches with rice, vegetables and fruit instead of fried food. They can also stop selling sweets and soft drinks in the school shop. In addition, children should do sport or exercise every day, not only once a week. For example, at my cousin's school, every class starts with ten minutes of exercise, and the children enjoy it.

**🔴 B2:** Schools are in a unique position to tackle childhood obesity, since children spend a large part of their day there. Firstly, they can provide nutritious meals and ban the sale of sugary drinks and snacks on school premises. Secondly, they can ensure that pupils take part in physical activity every day, not only during weekly physical education lessons. Short activity breaks between lessons, active playtimes and after-school sports clubs can all make a difference. Finally, schools can teach children about nutrition, so that they understand how to make healthy choices outside school as well.

**🟣 C1:** Because children spend so much of their waking lives at school, schools represent the most efficient point of intervention against obesity. Controlling the food environment — providing balanced meals and removing sugary drinks and snacks from vending machines — shapes habits without requiring children to exercise willpower. Equally important is reversing the decline of daily physical activity, which has often been squeezed out of crowded timetables by academic priorities. Crucially, school-based measures reach every child regardless of family income, making them among the few interventions capable of narrowing, rather than widening, health inequalities.

**⚫ C2:** A child's diet is shaped less by what they know than by what surrounds them. Lecture a ten-year-old about sugar in the morning and sell them a fizzy drink at lunchtime, and the vending machine will win every time. Schools that want healthier pupils should therefore spend less effort on posters and more on the environment itself: meals worth eating, water instead of soda, and a timetable that treats running around as seriously as arithmetic. Habits formed in that environment will outlast any lesson about them.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 268 words

In many countries, more and more children are overweight. This is a serious problem because these children may have health problems, such as diabetes and heart disease, when they grow up. In this essay, I will explain the causes and suggest some solutions.

The first cause is unhealthy food. Many children eat fast food, snacks and sweets every day, and they drink a lot of soft drinks. This food has a lot of sugar, salt and fat. Also, many parents are very busy, so they buy ready-made food instead of cooking healthy meals at home.

The second cause is that children do not do enough exercise. In the past, children played outside with their friends. Today, many children spend hours watching videos or playing games on phones and tablets. In big cities, there are not many parks or safe places to play, and many children go to school by car or motorbike instead of walking.

There are several solutions to this problem. Firstly, parents should cook healthy food at home and give their children fruit and vegetables instead of sweets. They should also limit screen time and encourage children to play outside. Secondly, schools should give healthy lunches and stop selling soft drinks and snacks. They should also have sport or exercise every day. Thirdly, the government can build more parks and playgrounds, and it can stop advertisements for unhealthy food on children's TV programmes.

In conclusion, children are becoming overweight because they eat unhealthy food and do not exercise enough. If parents, schools and the government work together, children can become healthier and have a better future.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 281 words

Childhood obesity has become one of the most serious public health challenges of the twenty-first century, affecting both developed and developing countries. This essay will examine the main causes of this trend and suggest some measures to address it.

The primary cause is a change in diet. Cheap, processed foods and sugary drinks are now widely available and heavily advertised to children, while traditional home-cooked meals have become less common as more parents work long hours. Portion sizes in restaurants and fast-food outlets have also increased, encouraging children to consume more calories than they need. The second major cause is a decline in physical activity. Children today spend a large amount of their free time on screens, and in many cities, heavy traffic and a lack of safe public spaces discourage outdoor play. Moreover, physical education is often given less time in school timetables, as schools focus on academic results.

To tackle this problem, action is needed at several levels. Schools should provide nutritious meals, ban the sale of sugary snacks and drinks, and ensure that children are physically active every day. Governments could restrict the advertising of unhealthy food to children and introduce taxes on sugary drinks, using the revenue to make healthy food more affordable. Urban planners should create more parks, playgrounds and safe cycling routes, so that children can play and travel actively. Finally, parents should be supported with information about nutrition and encouraged to limit their children's screen time.

In conclusion, childhood obesity is largely the result of unhealthy diets and inactive lifestyles. A combination of action by schools, governments, urban planners and families is needed to reverse the trend and ensure that children grow up healthy.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 293 words

The rapid rise in childhood obesity is frequently attributed to individual failings — children who eat too much or parents who fail to supervise them. Such explanations obscure the fact that obesity has increased across entire populations within a single generation, a pattern that points to changes in the environment rather than in personal willpower. Effective solutions must therefore target that environment.

The first set of causes concerns food. Energy-dense, highly processed products have become cheaper, more available and more aggressively marketed, often specifically to children through cartoon characters, games and social media. At the same time, time-pressed families increasingly rely on convenience foods, while portion sizes have expanded. The second set concerns activity. Children's play has migrated indoors and onto screens, partly because cities designed around cars offer few safe spaces for outdoor activity, and partly because physical education has been squeezed by academic priorities.

Solutions should correspondingly reshape the conditions in which children eat and move. Schools are a natural starting point, since they reach almost every child: providing balanced meals, removing sugary drinks from school premises and building daily physical activity into the timetable can shift habits at scale. Governments can restrict the marketing of unhealthy food to children and use fiscal measures, such as taxes on sugary drinks, to encourage manufacturers to reformulate their products. Urban policy matters too: safe walking and cycling routes and accessible parks make activity the easy choice rather than the exceptional one.

In short, childhood obesity is less a failure of individual discipline than a predictable response to an environment that makes unhealthy choices easy and healthy ones difficult. Changing that environment is not only more effective than blaming families; it is also fairer, since it benefits children regardless of their parents' income or knowledge.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 287 words

When obesity rises steadily across an entire generation, it is not because millions of children have suddenly lost their self-control. Something in their surroundings has changed, and it is there — not in lectures about willpower — that both the causes and the solutions are to be found.

Consider what has changed. The cheapest calories available to a child are now also the most heavily engineered to be irresistible: sweet, salty, fatty, and advertised through cartoon mascots and online games designed to reach children long before their parents can intervene. Meanwhile, the spaces in which children once burned off energy have steadily disappeared. Streets have been surrendered to traffic, playgrounds to car parks, and afternoons to screens that offer entertainment without movement. Even school timetables, under pressure to raise test scores, have quietly traded physical education for extra hours of mathematics.

The remedies follow from this diagnosis. If the environment is the cause, the environment must be the cure. Schools, which reach every child regardless of family circumstances, should serve food worth eating and make water, not soda, the default drink. Governments can restrict the marketing of junk food to children and tax sugary drinks, prompting manufacturers to change their recipes. Cities can reclaim streets for walking and cycling, and build parks close enough to be used daily rather than admired at weekends.

Parents matter, of course, and good information helps them. But it is unfair — and ineffective — to ask exhausted families to resist, alone, an entire industry devoted to persuading their children otherwise.

Childhood obesity, in the end, is a design problem. We have built a world in which the unhealthy choice is the easiest one. The solution is to build a world in which it is not.

## 33. Free or Paid Healthcare

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Health

> **Question:** Some people believe that healthcare should be free for everyone and paid for by the government. Others think people should pay for their own medical treatment. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** universal healthcare · health insurance · public hospitals · waiting lists · medical costs · taxation

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: nobody chooses to be ill, so treatment should not depend on money.*

**🟡 B1:** Nobody chooses to get sick. A poor person and a rich person can both have a serious disease, like cancer. If healthcare is not free, poor families may sell their land or borrow money to pay for the hospital. Some of them do not go to the doctor at all because they are afraid of the cost. For example, my neighbour waited too long before seeing a doctor because of money, and his illness became worse.

**🔴 B2:** The strongest argument for free healthcare is that illness is largely a matter of bad luck rather than personal choice. A serious disease can strike anyone, regardless of how carefully they live, and the cost of treatment can quickly exceed what an ordinary family can afford. When people have to pay directly, many delay seeing a doctor until their condition becomes serious, which is both more dangerous and more expensive to treat. Thailand's universal coverage scheme, introduced in 2002, shows that even middle-income countries can provide basic healthcare to their whole population.

**🟣 C1:** At the heart of the case for publicly funded healthcare lies a simple moral intuition: that access to treatment should depend on need rather than on the ability to pay. Illness is distributed with little regard for merit, and its costs can be catastrophic — in many low- and middle-income countries, medical bills are a major cause of households falling into poverty. Systems that require payment at the point of use also deter early treatment, allowing manageable conditions to become serious ones. Universal provision, as Thailand's widely praised coverage scheme demonstrates, need not be the preserve of wealthy nations.

**⚫ C2:** Illness is the purest form of bad luck. The healthiest runner can develop a tumour; the most careful driver can be struck by someone else's car. A system that makes treatment depend on income therefore punishes people twice — once with the disease, and again with the bill. There are many things a society can reasonably ask its members to earn. The right to be treated when their body fails them should not be one of them.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 275 words

Healthcare is very important for everyone, but it can be very expensive. Some people think the government should pay for everyone's healthcare. Other people think that people should pay for their own treatment. In this essay, I will discuss both views and give my opinion.

On the one hand, some people believe healthcare should be free. They say that everyone can get sick, rich or poor. If healthcare is expensive, poor people cannot pay for a doctor or medicine. Some families must sell their land or borrow a lot of money when a family member is very sick. For example, in some countries, people wait too long to see a doctor because they are worried about the cost, and their illness becomes worse.

On the other hand, other people think that people should pay for their own healthcare. They say that free healthcare costs the government a lot of money, so everyone must pay higher taxes. Also, when healthcare is free, some people go to the doctor for very small problems, so hospitals become crowded and patients must wait a long time. Some people also believe that if they pay, they will get better service.

In my opinion, basic healthcare should be free for everyone, especially for serious illnesses, children and old people. However, people who want extra services, such as a private room, can pay for them. I think this is fair, because no one should become poor because of illness.

In conclusion, both views have good points. However, I believe the government should pay for basic healthcare, because health is a basic need, and everyone should have the chance to see a doctor.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 306 words

How healthcare should be funded is one of the most important questions any government faces. While some argue that medical treatment should be free for everyone and funded through taxation, others believe individuals should pay for their own care. In my view, a publicly funded system is fairer and more effective, although private options can play a supporting role.

Supporters of free healthcare argue that access to treatment is a basic human right. Illness can affect anyone, and the cost of serious treatment, such as surgery or cancer care, can quickly push ordinary families into debt or poverty. When people must pay directly, many delay seeing a doctor, which often leads to more serious conditions that are more expensive to treat. A publicly funded system, such as the National Health Service in the United Kingdom, ensures that everyone can receive care regardless of their income.

On the other hand, those who favour paying for healthcare point to the high cost of public systems. Free healthcare must be funded through taxes, and as populations age, these costs continue to rise. Critics also argue that free services can lead to overuse, long waiting lists and less efficient hospitals, whereas private providers, competing for patients, may offer better quality and shorter waiting times.

In my opinion, the benefits of publicly funded healthcare outweigh its drawbacks. Health is not like other products: people cannot choose when they become ill, and poor health affects their ability to work and support their families. However, a mixed system may be the most practical solution, in which the government guarantees essential care for everyone while those who can afford it may pay for additional services.

In conclusion, although free healthcare is expensive, I believe governments should guarantee basic medical care for all citizens, since no one should be denied treatment because they cannot pay.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 303 words

The question of who should pay for healthcare reflects deeper disagreements about the relationship between individuals and the state. Those who favour user payment emphasise efficiency and personal responsibility; advocates of public funding emphasise fairness and solidarity. I would argue that essential healthcare should be publicly funded, not only on ethical grounds but also because health markets function poorly when left to themselves.

The case for individual payment is not without substance. Publicly funded systems are expensive, and their costs rise inexorably as populations age and treatments become more sophisticated. Where services are free at the point of use, demand can exceed supply, producing waiting lists that frustrate patients and clinicians alike. Proponents of private provision contend that competition drives efficiency and innovation, and that individuals who pay directly are more likely to use services responsibly.

Yet healthcare differs from ordinary goods in ways that undermine these arguments. Patients rarely choose when they need treatment, cannot easily judge its quality, and are often in no position to shop around in an emergency. Moreover, the costs of serious illness are unpredictable and potentially ruinous: in many low- and middle-income countries, out-of-pocket medical expenses push millions of households into poverty each year. Systems based on user payment also tend to deter preventive care, allowing treatable conditions to become costly emergencies.

Evidence from countries such as Thailand, whose universal coverage scheme extended basic healthcare to millions of previously uninsured people, suggests that public provision need not be the preserve of wealthy nations. Its limitations, including waiting times, are problems of management and resources rather than arguments against the principle itself.

In conclusion, while private provision may usefully supplement a public system, access to essential healthcare should depend on need, not wealth. A society that allows illness to become a cause of poverty compounds misfortune with injustice.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 328 words

Most goods are distributed according to what people can pay, and for most goods this is perfectly reasonable. The question is whether healthcare belongs in that category. I believe it does not, and that essential medical care should be funded collectively — not because markets are wicked, but because illness makes a mockery of the conditions markets require.

The case for individual payment deserves a fair hearing. Public health systems are expensive and grow more so every year as populations age and treatments advance. Services that are free at the point of use can be overused, and the resulting queues are a genuine hardship. Private providers, competing for patients, can be efficient and responsive, and it is not unreasonable to ask people to bear some responsibility for their own care.

But a market works when buyers can compare products, choose when to purchase and walk away from a bad deal. The patient on a stretcher can do none of these things. They cannot delay a heart attack until prices fall, cannot judge whether a surgeon is competent, and cannot refuse treatment without risking their life. Moreover, illness strikes without regard to income or merit, and its costs can be catastrophic: in many countries, a single hospital stay is enough to push a family from modest security into lasting poverty. Where people must pay, they postpone seeing a doctor, and minor conditions ripen into major ones.

Nor is universal healthcare a luxury reserved for rich nations. Thailand extended coverage to its entire population at a time when it was far from wealthy, demonstrating that the obstacle is often political will rather than money.

Private care can have a place alongside a public system, offering comfort and choice to those who want to pay for them. But the core of healthcare — the right to be treated when one's body fails — should not be for sale. A society is judged, in the end, by whether misfortune is shared or simply endured alone.

## 34. Prevention or Treatment

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Health

> **Question:** Some people think that governments should spend more money on preventing illness than on treating people who are already sick. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** preventive healthcare · vaccination · health education · early screening · lifestyle diseases · treatment costs

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: preventing illness is cheaper than curing it and saves more lives.*

**🟡 B1:** Preventing illness is cheaper than treating it. For example, a vaccine costs a little money, but treating a child with a serious disease in hospital costs a lot. Also, if people learn to eat healthy food and stop smoking, fewer people will get heart disease or cancer. Then hospitals will have more time and money for other patients. So I think prevention is a very good investment.

**🔴 B2:** Investing in prevention is generally far more cost-effective than paying for treatment. Vaccination programmes, for instance, cost relatively little compared with the expense of treating the diseases they prevent, and they protect entire communities. Similarly, campaigns that reduce smoking or encourage healthier diets can lower the number of people who later require expensive treatment for heart disease, diabetes or cancer. In the long term, therefore, money spent on prevention reduces pressure on hospitals and allows health budgets to be used more efficiently.

**🟣 C1:** The economic logic of prevention is compelling. Childhood vaccination is widely regarded as one of the most cost-effective interventions in public health, averting diseases whose treatment would cost many times more, while measures that reduce smoking or improve diets can prevent chronic conditions that consume a large share of health spending for decades. Prevention also yields benefits that treatment cannot: a heart attack avoided means not only lower medical costs but years of productive, healthy life retained. The paradox is that its successes are invisible — no one celebrates the illness that never occurred — which is precisely why it tends to be underfunded.

**⚫ C2:** A smoke alarm costs almost nothing and is never thanked; the fire engine costs a fortune and is cheered in the street. Health systems have fallen into the same trap, lavishing money on the fire engine while neglecting the alarm. A course of vaccinations, a tax on cigarettes, a routine blood-pressure check — none of these makes headlines, yet each prevents suffering that no hospital, however advanced, could entirely undo. Prevention is not the modest cousin of medicine. It is medicine at its most efficient.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 254 words

Every government must decide how to spend its health budget. Some people think it is better to spend more money on preventing diseases than on treating sick people. I mostly agree with this idea.

Firstly, prevention is cheaper than treatment. For example, a vaccine for a child costs very little, but treating a serious disease in hospital can cost a lot of money. Also, if people eat healthy food and exercise, fewer people will get diabetes or heart disease, which are very expensive to treat.

Secondly, prevention can save many lives. Some diseases are very difficult to cure, but they are easy to prevent. For example, vaccines have protected millions of children from dangerous diseases like measles and polio. Health education can also teach people not to smoke and to wash their hands, which stops many illnesses.

Thirdly, if fewer people are sick, hospitals will not be so crowded. Doctors and nurses can then spend more time with patients who really need help, such as people who have accidents or serious diseases.

On the other hand, we cannot stop all diseases. People will always have accidents, and some illnesses happen even to healthy people. Also, older people often need a lot of treatment. So the government must still spend money on hospitals, doctors and medicine.

In conclusion, I agree that governments should spend more money on preventing illness because it is cheaper, it saves lives and it helps hospitals. However, they should not forget people who are already sick, because treatment is still necessary.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 278 words

Health budgets in most countries are under growing pressure, and governments must decide how to divide limited resources between preventing illness and treating it. I largely agree that more funding should be directed towards prevention, although treatment services must not be neglected.

The strongest argument for prioritising prevention is that it is usually more cost-effective. Many of the conditions that consume the largest share of health budgets, such as type 2 diabetes, heart disease and some cancers, are closely linked to lifestyle factors like smoking, poor diet and physical inactivity. Public health campaigns, taxes on tobacco and sugary drinks, and regular health check-ups can reduce the number of people who develop these conditions, saving large amounts of money in the long run. Vaccination programmes are another example: they are relatively inexpensive, yet they prevent diseases that would otherwise require costly treatment.

In addition, prevention improves quality of life in a way treatment cannot. Even when a disease can be treated successfully, patients often experience pain, anxiety and lost income. Preventing illness in the first place allows people to remain healthy, active and productive for longer.

However, prevention cannot replace treatment entirely. Accidents, genetic conditions and many diseases cannot be prevented, and ageing populations will continue to need hospital care. Moreover, the benefits of prevention often take years to appear, while sick people need help now. Reducing treatment budgets too quickly could therefore cause serious harm.

*Idea: competitive sport teaches children how to lose and try again.*
