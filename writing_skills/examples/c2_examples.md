## Paragraph 1 — Working from Home (Reframing)

**Question:** *Is remote working good for employees?*

> The debate over remote work is **rarely about location at all**. **It is a proxy for** an older, unresolved question: **do organisations value presence or contribution?** A manager who insists on seeing staff at their desks is not, in most cases, concerned with the desks; **he or she is** reaching for the only measure of effort that feels reliable. Remote work **removes that crutch** — and, **in doing so, exposes** how few workplaces have ever defined what good work actually looks like. **Seen in this light,** the employees who flourish at home **are not necessarily** the most disciplined, **but** those whose output was always visible, while those who struggle are often the ones whose contribution was never properly measured in the first place. **The office, it turns out, was concealing as much as it was enabling.**

### 🔎 Analysis
| Technique | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Reframing** the question | *rarely about location at all… a proxy for…* |
| **Short sentence after longer ones** | *…do organisations value presence or contribution?* |
| **Metaphor** | *removes that crutch* |
| **Perspective shift** | *Seen in this light,…* |
| **Aphoristic ending** with antithesis | *…concealing as much as it was enabling.* |

➡️ Compare: [B1](b1_examples.md#paragraph-1--working-from-home-opinion) lists pros/cons → [B2](b2_examples.md#paragraph-1--working-from-home-peel) develops one advantage → [C1](c1_examples.md#paragraph-1--working-from-home-nuanced-argument) qualifies *for whom* → **C2 changes the question itself**.

## Paragraph 2 — Technology (Rhythm & Rhetoric)

> Every generation greets its new technology with the same two stories. **In the first,** the invention will liberate us: from drudgery, from distance, from ignorance. **In the second,** it will corrupt us: our attention, our morals, our children. The printing press, the novel, the radio and the television have each been cast in both roles, **often in the same breath**. **What is striking is not that** these fears were wrong — **some were not** — **but that** they were so consistently aimed at the tool rather than at the uses to which it was put. **The smartphone is no different.** **It will neither save us nor ruin us.** **It will, as its predecessors did, amplify whatever we already are.**

### 🔎 Analysis
- **Parallel tricolons:** *from drudgery, from distance, from ignorance / our attention, our morals, our children*
- **Historical framing:** printing press → novel → radio → TV → smartphone
- **Concession inside a cleft:** *What is striking is not that… — some were not — but that…*
- **Rhythm:** long sentences build up, then **three short sentences** land the point
- **Idiom used precisely:** *in the same breath*

## Paragraph 3 — Steel-manning the Opposition

> **The strongest case for** mass surveillance **is not** the one usually made. **It is not** that the innocent have nothing to hide — a slogan that collapses **the moment** one considers who decides what counts as innocence. **It is, rather, that** surveillance frequently works: attacks are prevented, networks are disrupted, lives are saved, and **none of this is visible** precisely because it succeeded. **This is a serious argument, and it deserves a serious answer.** **That answer is not** that surveillance fails, **but that** its effectiveness is **the very source of** its danger. A tool powerful enough to find a terrorist is powerful enough to find a journalist, a dissident, or an inconvenient opponent; **its capacity does not change with the intentions of whoever inherits it.**

### 🔎 Analysis
- **Steel-manning:** dismisses the *weak* version of the opposing view, then presents the *strongest* one
- **Anaphora:** *It is not… It is, rather,… That answer is not…*
- **Tricolon + climax:** *attacks are prevented, networks are disrupted, lives are saved*
- **Turning the argument:** *its effectiveness is the very source of its danger*
- **End-weight** on the key idea: *…whoever inherits it.*

## Essay — Online Learning vs Classroom Learning (Discursive, Reframed)

**Question:** *"Online learning will make the classroom obsolete." Discuss. (380–420 words)*

> ### The Room Is Not the Point
>
> **¶1** Predictions of the classroom's demise **have a long and undistinguished history**. Radio was to bring the finest lecturers into every home; television, then the personal computer, then the internet, each promised the same. **That none of these delivered** should give us pause before we declare that online platforms will succeed where their predecessors failed. **Yet** the more interesting question is not whether the classroom will survive, **but what, exactly, we imagine it is for.**
>
> **¶2** If education is the transfer of information from an expert to a learner, **then the classroom is indeed an inefficient technology.** It is bound to a fixed time and place, it moves at the pace of its slowest members, and it depends on the uneven talent of a single teacher. **Measured against these constraints,** a well-designed online course — searchable, repeatable, available at midnight — **looks less like a substitute than an upgrade.** **For motivated adults, it often is.**
>
> **¶3** **But information transfer was never the whole of it, and for the young it is barely half.** The classroom is also where children learn to wait their turn, to lose an argument gracefully, to work with people they did not choose, and to sustain attention on something that does not reward them instantly. **These are not incidental by-products of schooling; they are, arguably, its most durable outcomes** — and they are precisely the ones that a screen, optimised for individual engagement, is least equipped to cultivate. **When schools closed** during the pandemic, **what many parents discovered was not** that their children had stopped learning facts, **but that** they had stopped learning **each other**.
>
> **¶4** **None of this is an argument for nostalgia.** A classroom in which a teacher reads from slides that could have been emailed **deserves to be made obsolete**. The real opportunity lies in a division of labour: let digital tools do what they do well — deliver, drill, and measure — and free the room for what only a room can do. **The classroom will not disappear. It may, if we are wise, finally be used for the right things.**

*(≈ 400 words)*

### 🔎 Analysis
| Paragraph | Move | C2 technique |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ¶1 | **Historicises** and **reframes** | *have a long and undistinguished history* (understatement) → *what, exactly, we imagine it is for* |
| ¶2 | **Steel-mans** the opposing view, fully | Conditional logic: *If… then…*; ends with short concession: *For motivated adults, it often is.* |
| ¶3 | Core argument | Tricolon/list of social skills; *not incidental… they are, arguably,…*; memorable reversal *learning each other* |
| ¶4 | Synthesis + resonant ending | *None of this is an argument for nostalgia* (pre-empts objection); final **two short sentences** |
| Title | *The Room Is Not the Point* | A title that states the thesis |

## Opinion Column — The Cult of Productivity

**Task:** *Write a column for a quality newspaper about modern attitudes to work and rest. (350–400 words)*

> ### In Praise of Wasted Time
>
> I have a confession to make: I own four apps designed to make me more productive, and I spend **a not inconsiderable amount of** time managing them. **I suspect I am not alone.**
>
> **Somewhere along the way,** productivity stopped being a means and became a virtue. We track our sleep so that we can work better, optimise our mornings so that we can work longer, and schedule our holidays so that we return **"recharged"** — **as though** a human being were a battery **whose only purpose is to be drained again**. Rest, in this vocabulary, **is not a good in itself but an input.**
>
> **The irony is that** the evidence **does not support** the ideology. Research on long working hours has repeatedly found that output per hour **tends to** fall as hours rise, and that fatigue **carries real costs** in errors and health. Many of the ideas we most admire **arrived**, by their creators' own accounts, **not at the desk but on a walk, in the bath, or while staring out of a train window** — in precisely the kind of unstructured time we are now encouraged to eliminate.
>
> **None of this is to romanticise idleness.** Work matters, and for many people the problem is **not too much ambition but too little security**. **But there is a difference between** working hard and treating every unmeasured minute as a small moral failure. **The first is a choice; the second is a condition** — and it is one that the tools we have built to liberate us have **done a remarkable job of** spreading.
>
> So here is a modest proposal. **Waste an hour this week.** Not on your phone, which is another form of work, but on nothing in particular. Watch a river. Read something useless. Notice what your mind does when no one is asking it for anything.
>
> **You may find, as I did, that it has been waiting quite a while to be asked.**

### 🔎 Analysis
- **Journalistic voice:** first person, confession, direct address (*So here is a modest proposal*)
- **Litotes / understatement:** *a not inconsiderable amount of*
- **Parallel structure:** *track… so that… / optimise… so that… / schedule… so that…*
- **Extended metaphor:** human being as a **battery**
- **Anticipating objection:** *None of this is to romanticise idleness.*
- **Antithesis:** *The first is a choice; the second is a condition.*
- **Imperatives + short sentences** for a persuasive close
- **Ironic ending** that circles back to the reader
- **Careful with evidence:** general claims are hedged (*tends to*) and anecdotes are presented as *creators' own accounts*, not invented statistics

## Before & After: C1 → C2

### 🟣 C1 version
> It could be argued that economic growth is essential for reducing poverty. Admittedly, growth has lifted millions of people out of extreme poverty in recent decades. However, it is increasingly evident that growth alone is insufficient, since its benefits are often unevenly distributed and it frequently comes at considerable environmental cost. Therefore, a more balanced approach that prioritises sustainable and inclusive development is required.

### ⚫ C2 version
> **Growth has done more to reduce poverty than any policy ever designed for the purpose; this is not in serious dispute.** What is in dispute is whether the tool that built the ladder can also be trusted to keep it standing. Growth distributes its gains **according to** existing advantage, and it bills its environmental costs **to** people who were never consulted — many of them not yet born. **To defend growth is therefore not to settle the argument but to begin it.** The question is not whether an economy should grow, **but for whom, at whose expense, and for how long.**

**What changed:**
- Opens with a **strong concession stated as fact**, rather than a hedged *It could be argued*
- **Metaphor** (*the tool that built the ladder*) carries the argument
- **Personification** (*bills its environmental costs*) and a striking detail (*not yet born*)
- **Antithesis:** *not to settle the argument but to begin it*
- **Tricolon ending:** *for whom, at whose expense, and for how long*
- **Fewer connectors** (*admittedly, however, therefore*): the logic is carried by structure

## One Idea, Four Levels

**Idea:** *Reading books is still important in the digital age.*

| Level | Sentence(s) |
| :--- | :--- |
| 🟡 **B1** | *I think reading books is still important because it helps us learn new words and relax. For example, I read before bed every night.* |
| 🔴 **B2** | *Although digital media is increasingly popular, reading books remains valuable, as it not only improves vocabulary but also develops concentration — a skill that is often weakened by constant screen use.* |
| 🟣 **C1** | *Far from being rendered obsolete by digital media, sustained reading is arguably more valuable than ever, since it cultivates precisely the capacity for deep, uninterrupted attention that fragmented online consumption tends to erode.* |
| ⚫ **C2** | *We read books not despite the internet but, increasingly, because of it. In an environment engineered to interrupt us, the ability to follow a single argument for three hundred pages has become less a pastime than a form of resistance.* |
