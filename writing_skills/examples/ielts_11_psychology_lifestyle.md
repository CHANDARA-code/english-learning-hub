## 101. Constant Connectivity and Work-Life Boundary

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Psychology, Mental Health & Modern Lifestyle

> **Question:** In many countries today, smartphones and portable computers mean that employees can be reached by their employers at any time of day or night. Some people believe this continuous connectivity harms personal wellbeing and family life, and that laws should be passed to prevent after-hours work communication. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** work-life equilibrium · constant availability · digital intrusion · occupational burnout · right to disconnect · cognitive fatigue

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: constant digital communication prevents genuine psychological detachment from work.*

**🟡 B1:** When workers receive emails and messages late at night, they cannot truly rest. Their mind stays busy with work problems, and they feel stressed even when sitting with their family. For example, my older brother frequently answers phone calls during dinner, which makes everyone feel annoyed. If workers never stop thinking about their job, their health will suffer. Therefore, people need clear boundaries between work and home.

**🔴 B2:** The main danger of constant connectivity is that it deprives employees of essential recovery time. When workers are expected to monitor notifications after hours, the physiological stress response never completely deactivates. Studies have demonstrated that continuous digital accessibility leads to occupational burnout, chronic insomnia, and marital conflict. Unless employees have a guaranteed window in which they are completely unreachable, their physical and psychological resilience will steadily deteriorate.

**🟣 C1:** Pervasive connectivity undermines wellbeing by systematically dismantling the boundary between professional obligation and private recuperation. When communication platforms remain active around the clock, workers enter a state of chronic anticipatory anxiety, constantly primed for urgent demands. This perpetual hypervigilance precludes deep cognitive rest, ultimately accelerating burnout while eroding the emotional availability essential for nurturing family relationships. Legal safeguards against after-hours intrusion are therefore not an indulgence, but a vital public health intervention.

**⚫ C2:** The pocket-sized computer was marketed as an instrument of liberation; in practice, it has transformed every living room into an unremunerated annex of the office. By eliminating the friction of physical distance, round-the-clock messaging demands an emotional tethering that makes psychological detachment impossible. A human being cannot genuinely recuperate while listening for the chime of an impending obligation; to deny workers silence is to exhaust their capacity for both productive labour and meaningful domestic life.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 268 words

In recent years, modern technology has changed how people work. Because of smartphones and portable computers, employers can send messages and emails to their staff at any time. Many people believe this damages personal health and family life, and they want governments to introduce laws to stop it. I completely agree with this view.

Firstly, receiving work messages at home causes a lot of stress. In the past, when employees left the office, their working day was completely finished. Today, workers check their phones during dinner or before going to bed. Because they are always thinking about their job duties, their minds never relax. For example, many of my friends feel tired and nervous because their managers expect quick replies on weekends. Over time, this constant pressure makes workers sick.

Secondly, this habit destroys valuable family time. When parents look at their phone screens all evening, they cannot pay proper attention to their children or talk with their husband or wife. Children need to feel that their parents are listening to them. If parents are always answering work emails, relationships inside the home become weak. A healthy family requires uninterrupted time together without professional distractions.

Admittedly, some managers argue that emergency communication is necessary to compete in the international market. However, true emergencies are very rare, and most tasks can easily wait until the next morning.

In conclusion, I strongly believe that being constantly available to employers damages human wellbeing and family harmony. Governments should establish clear laws that protect workers' right to disconnect when their official working hours end.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 284 words

The widespread adoption of smartphones and laptops has made remote connectivity ubiquitous, enabling companies to contact their personnel at any hour. While some executives defend this flexibility as essential for modern commerce, I firmly agree that relentless digital accessibility damages individuals' mental health and domestic stability, justifying statutory restrictions.

The primary objection to continuous connectivity is its catastrophic impact on psychological wellbeing. When employees are expected to monitor incoming messages outside official hours, they remain in a state of perpetual vigilance. Psychologists refer to this phenomenon as workplace intrusion, where the autonomic nervous system is denied the opportunity to downregulate. Over prolonged periods, this chronic tension manifests in elevated cortisol levels, sleep disorders, and severe emotional exhaustion. France recognized this crisis by enacting "right to disconnect" legislation, illustrating that without explicit legal protection, corporate cultures inevitably exploit workers' downtime.

Furthermore, domestic relationships require sustained emotional presence, which constant interruptions effectively destroy. An employee who is physically present in the living room but cognitively engaged with corporate email is essentially absent from family life. Children who observe their parents perpetually captivated by screens often experience feelings of neglect, while spousal communication deteriorates. In the long run, this dynamic increases marital strain and degrades the quality of domestic relationships.

Opponents frequently contend that international industries operate across differing time zones, necessitating flexible communication. However, flexibility should not degenerate into limitless servitude. Organizations can easily implement scheduled shifts and handover protocols rather than intruding into the personal lives of off-duty staff.

In conclusion, uninterrupted work communication severely compromises mental stability and undermines family bonds. Governments ought to introduce enforceable regulations that guarantee workers the legal right to disconnect from professional duties outside designated hours.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 288 words

Technological convergence has dissolved the physical and chronological parameters that once circumscribed the working day. With smartphones serving as portable workstations, personnel are increasingly susceptible to managerial demands long after office hours. I unequivocal endorse the proposition that this pervasive accessibility inflicts grave harm upon psychological equilibrium and domestic cohesion, and that legislative remedies are imperative.

The gravest consequence of constant availability is the systematic erosion of psychological detachment. True recuperation requires an absolute cognitive separation from vocational pressures. When employees are tethered to corporate messaging platforms, they succumb to anticipatory stress, endlessly bracing for incoming crises. This persistent activation of the sympathetic nervous system precludes deep, restorative rest and fuels unprecedented rates of clinical burnout. Far from enhancing enterprise efficiency, this insidious encroachment eventually degrades cognitive acuity and creative problem-solving, rendering the practice economically self-defeating in the long term.

Equally pernicious is the fragmentation of domestic and interpersonal life. Genuine human intimacy demands sustained, undivided attention. When domestic sanctuaries are repeatedly infiltrated by work notifications, personal relationships are reduced to interstitial moments squeezed between professional duties. This chronic cognitive distraction impoverishes parental engagement, leaving children emotionally neglected, while stoking interpersonal resentment between partners. The home ceases to function as a restorative retreat from the marketplace and becomes instead an ambient workspace.

Critics occasionally object that formal statutory intervention introduces rigidity into flexible knowledge economies. Nevertheless, left unregulated, competitive market dynamics invariably generate a race to the bottom, where boundaries are interpreted as lack of commitment. Only clear statutory protections, such as statutory disconnect protocols, can establish an equitable baseline that safeguards personal sovereignty against corporate overreach.

In conclusion, unrestricted after-hours communication exacts an intolerable toll on mental health and familial vitality. Enacting statutory protections is not anti-business sentiment; it is a fundamental societal prerequisite for human sustainability.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 289 words

The digital tether that binds the contemporary professional to the enterprise is frequently celebrated as an emblem of convenience; in truth, it represents the colonial conquest of private time by economic utility. Because pocket-sized devices allow instantaneous communication across time zones, the implicit expectation has arisen that an employee's attention is permanently on retainer. I unreservedly agree that this unbroken servitude devastates human interiority and domestic life, and that the state must intervene to restore sanity.

The fundamental damage inflicted by round-the-clock availability is ontological: it abolishes the boundary between being and producing. To recuperate from labour, the mind must step outside the theatre of competition; it requires intervals of authentic silence and psychological refuge. Under the regime of continuous notification, however, employees inhabit a condition of ambient hypervigilance. Even when no directive arrives, the mere possibility of its arrival poisons leisure with anticipation. This silent friction does not merely cause exhaustion; it hollows out contemplative life, trading genuine rejuvenation for a brittle, jittery alertness that modern medicine diagnoses as chronic burnout.

Domestically, the repercussions are no less calamitous. Intimacy cannot flourish in snippets of stolen attention between urgent emails. When parents offer their children a divided consciousness, they communicate that external economic demands take precedence over familial devotion. The home, historically conceived as an emotional haven insulated from commercial pressures, degenerates into an unremunerated outpost of the corporate machine, breeding alienation among those who dwell within it.

Laissez-faire apologists maintain that boundary management is an individual virtue rather than a legislative matter. Yet this perspective willfully ignores the profound power asymmetry between employer and employee; in competitive sectors, the worker who unilaterally unplugs commits career sabotage. Only binding statutory prohibitions can neutralise this coercive dynamic. Reclaiming the night is ultimately a defense of human dignity against infinite extraction.

## 102. Loneliness and Social Isolation in Modern Cities

**Type:** Problem and solution · **Theme:** Psychology, Mental Health & Modern Lifestyle

> **Question:** Despite living in densely populated urban centres with millions of residents, an increasing number of city dwellers report feeling chronic loneliness and social alienation. What are the primary causes of this phenomenon, and what effective measures could be implemented by municipal authorities and community groups to resolve it?

**Useful vocabulary:** urban atomisation · social alienation · communal cohesion · third places · transient population · superficial interaction

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: modern urban architecture and car-centric design isolate people from casual community contact.*

**🟡 B1:** Many modern apartment buildings do not have places where neighbours can meet and talk. People take the elevator straight to their flat, lock their door, and never speak to the person living next to them. In addition, cities are designed for cars rather than pedestrians, so people do not walk in streets where they can greet others. Because of this design, urban life feels very cold and lonely.

**🔴 B2:** A significant factor contributing to urban loneliness is the architectural layout of contemporary cities. High-rise apartment complexes often function as vertical silos, lacking communal gardens, shared kitchens, or common rooms where informal interactions could spontaneously occur. Furthermore, urban planning that prioritises vehicular speed over pedestrian plazas discourages public lingering. Consequently, city dwellers can spend entire weeks navigating crowded streets without participating in a single meaningful human exchange.

**🟣 C1:** Urban alienation is largely engineered by spatial arrangements that prioritize commercial efficiency over communal socialization. Modern residential towers encourage extreme privacy, insulating occupants from incidental encounters with their neighbors. Meanwhile, the aggressive commercialization of public space has eliminated non-transactional "third places" where citizens could congregate without financial expenditure. As streets become thoroughfares designed solely for rapid vehicular transit rather than social convergence, the civic fabric disintegrates into atomised isolation.

**⚫ C2:** The paradox of urban isolation is structural rather than psychological. Modern metropolitan planning has systematically engineered out the serendipitous encounter: high-density residential towers mimic filing cabinets for human bodies, where acoustic insulation and private foyers ensure that proximate existence never curdles into fellowship. When public squares are commodified into consumption corridors and pedestrian walkways are subordinated to arterial roads, the city ceases to be an agora and becomes an apparatus of transit, leaving the solitary soul marooned in a sea of indifferent strangers.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 265 words

In many big cities around the world, millions of people live side by side, yet more and more citizens say they feel lonely and isolated. This is a very sad problem in modern society. In this essay, I will discuss the main reasons for this situation and suggest some practical solutions.

The primary cause of urban loneliness is the lifestyle of modern city life. People in large cities often work very long hours and have difficult commutes on crowded trains or buses. When they arrive home in the evening, they are completely exhausted and only want to sleep or watch videos on their phones. Furthermore, people in cities move houses very often for work or education, which means they do not have permanent neighbours or old friends nearby. Therefore, they live surrounded by strangers and lack deep emotional support.

To solve this growing problem, city governments and local communities must take action. First, town planners should build more public spaces such as community gardens, library reading rooms, and free sports facilities. When people have green parks where they can walk dogs or sit on benches, they can meet their neighbours easily without spending money. Second, neighbourhood committees should organise regular social events, such as weekend food festivals, music clubs, or volunteer groups for cleaning streets. These activities give lonely residents a clear purpose and a reason to connect with others.

In conclusion, urban loneliness is caused by exhausting work routines and the lack of stable communities. By creating welcoming public parks and organising local social activities, cities can become much warmer and happier places to live.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 282 words

One of the great ironies of contemporary life is that people residing in metropolitan hubs teeming with millions of inhabitants frequently experience profound loneliness. This social alienation stems from architectural fragmentation and fast-paced working lifestyles, but it can be mitigated through proactive urban redesign and community-led initiatives.

The primary catalyst for this phenomenon is the relentless erosion of community infrastructure in favour of individualised living. In expanding metropolises, high-density residential buildings are engineered for privacy rather than connection. Residents frequently enter their apartments via secure parking garages and keycard elevators without exchanging a single word with neighbours. Additionally, the fast-paced nature of urban employment demands extensive overtime and lengthy transit, leaving individuals with minimal time or emotional energy to cultivate genuine friendships. Social interactions are consequently relegated to superficial exchanges on social media apps, which often intensify feelings of inadequacy rather than alleviating loneliness.

Addressing this urban malaise requires concerted efforts from municipal authorities and grassroots organizations. Firstly, local councils should mandate the inclusion of communal amenities in new residential developments, such as shared rooftop gardens, co-working lounges, and communal cooking areas. These communal zones provide low-pressure environments for incidental, face-to-face conversations. Secondly, municipalities should invest heavily in creating vibrant "third places"—spaces separate from home and work that do not require commercial consumption, such as public libraries, civic centres, and public squares. Finally, subsidising community interest clubs and local volunteering networks would enable isolated individuals to forge meaningful relationships around shared values.

In conclusion, urban isolation is driven by hyper-individualistic lifestyles and fragmented urban environments. By intentionally designing communal public infrastructure and fostering neighborhood civic groups, modern cities can transform from alienating concrete jungles into genuinely supportive communities.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 284 words

The pervasive epidemic of chronic loneliness in contemporary metropolises represents an acute civilisational contradiction. Despite unprecedented spatial proximity to millions of fellow citizens, urbanites increasingly report acute emotional alienation. This estrangement is primarily driven by the commercialisation of communal spaces and the transient nature of modern employment, necessitating systemic architectural and municipal interventions.

The root of urban alienation lies in the socio-spatial configuration of modern cities. Urban environments are increasingly optimized for commercial throughput and capital mobility rather than civic congregation. Public squares and commons have been systematically privatised or subordinated to arterial road networks, extinguishing accessible "third places" where diverse demographics once intermingled without the prerequisite of monetary expenditure. Concurrently, the hyper-mobility demanded by modern corporate employment fosters a transient urban populace. When individuals view their tenancy in an apartment or neighbourhood as transitory, they exhibit little motivation to invest in local social capital, producing atomised residential towers devoid of genuine neighbourliness.

To counteract this fragmentation, municipal planning must undergo a fundamental philosophical realignment. Urban authorities should enforce zoning mandates that require high-density developments to integrate non-commercial communal infrastructure, including communal workshops, urban agriculture cooperatives, and shared childcare hubs. Such shared utilities convert passive proximity into collaborative engagement. Moreover, municipal councils should fund community-building facilitators who orchestrate neighbourhood-scale mutual aid networks, cultural exchanges, and intergenerational mentorship programs. By activating disused public buildings for artistic and social pursuits, local governments can reconstitute spaces of collective belonging.

In short, urban loneliness is not an inevitable psychological flaw of city dwellers, but a structural consequence of market-driven urbanism. By restoring accessible public domains and fostering sustained civic networks, society can reconstruct meaningful social solidarity within the modern metropolis.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 287 words

The spectacle of millions of human beings moving in close physical proximity yet severed by profound psychological distance is the signature tragedy of the modern metropolis. Urban loneliness is neither an accidental byproduct of temperament nor an incurable condition of population density; it is the deliberate output of an economic ethos that prizes privatised consumption over public communion. Overcoming it demands a profound spatial and civic reclamation.

At the core of this malaise is the commodification of urban geography. The contemporary city has been restructured into an engine of frictionless circulation: highways slice through historic quarters, and public benches are removed to deter lingering. The non-transactional spaces of conviviality—the village square, the public hall, the unpatrolled park—have been replaced by shopping malls and franchised cafes where one’s presence is tolerated only so long as one continues to purchase. In such an ecosystem, the stranger is no longer a potential neighbor, but an obstacle in transit or a competitor for scarce space. Wrapped in noise-canceling headphones, the urbanite retreats into a bespoke digital cocoon, rendering solitude impenetrable.

Remediating this existential desert requires daring municipal intervention rather than sentimental appeals to friendliness. Urban planners must abandon vehicular supremacy and pedestrianise urban centres, carving out public agoras that invite stillness, play, and unscripted conversation. Furthermore, local governments should pioneer civic housing models—such as cooperative co-housing and mixed-generational complexes—where communal domestic chores and shared recreational commons naturally cultivate organic interdependencies. When society shares physical responsibilities, empathy ceases to be an abstract virtue and becomes a daily practice.

Ultimately, urban alienation will persist as long as cities are designed merely as hubs of labour extraction. When municipal leaders dare to treat human fellowship as a vital civic infrastructure, the metropolis may finally fulfill its ancient promise: not merely as a machine for living, but as a crucible of community.

## 103. Cosmetic Procedures and Beauty Standards

**Type:** Discussion (discuss both views and give your opinion) · **Theme:** Psychology, Mental Health & Modern Lifestyle

> **Question:** In recent years, elective cosmetic surgery and non-surgical aesthetic enhancements have become increasingly mainstream and accessible. Some people believe that these procedures represent an empowering exercise of personal autonomy that enhances self-esteem. Others argue that this trend reflects toxic societal pressures and promotes unattainable beauty ideals that harm mental health. Discuss both sides and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** elective aesthetic procedures · personal autonomy · unrealistic beauty standards · body dysmorphic tendencies · algorithmic curation · psychological self-acceptance

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: social media algorithms bombard users with digitally altered faces, creating deep psychological insecurity.*

**🟡 B1:** Today, social media apps show young people perfect pictures of celebrities every minute. Many of these photos are heavily edited using digital filters. Young people look at these fake pictures and feel very bad about their own natural bodies and faces. Because they want to look like online stars, they decide to spend money on cosmetic surgery to fix normal features.

**🔴 B2:** A primary concern surrounding the boom in aesthetic procedures is the influence of digital image manipulation. Social media platforms promote algorithmic feeds saturated with filtered selfies and curated perfection. Consequently, adolescents and young adults are continuously subjected to unattainable aesthetic standards, which often induces severe physical insecurity. Under this pervasive pressure, cosmetic intervention is increasingly perceived not as an optional luxury, but as an urgent necessity to cure perceived physical flaws.

**🟣 C1:** The proliferation of cosmetic interventions cannot be disentangled from the algorithmic visual culture that dominates modern communication. Digital platforms weaponise visual insecurity by flooding users with digitally manipulated physiognomies that masquerade as natural perfection. This relentless exposure distorts perceptual norms, fostering widespread body dissatisfaction and dysmorphic anxieties. In this context, aesthetic surgery ceases to be an authentic expression of personal agency and becomes instead a desperate psychological capitulation to commercialised bodily homogenisation.

**⚫ C2:** The aesthetic clinic does not manufacture beauty so much as it commodifies anxiety. Bathed in the filtered glow of curated feeds, contemporary youth are conditioned to gaze upon their own unmediated faces with disgust. When digital algorithms systematically punish natural idiosyncrasy and reward symmetry, the decision to undergo surgical alteration is less an act of self-authorship than a negotiated surrender. The scalpel is enlisted to chisel away identity until it conforms to the homogenised aesthetic of the digital marketplace.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 267 words

In modern society, cosmetic surgery and beauty treatments have become very popular and affordable for ordinary people. While some individuals believe that changing one's appearance is a personal right that boosts self-confidence, others claim that it causes severe mental health issues by promoting impossible standards. In this essay, I will discuss both perspectives and explain my own point of view.

On the one hand, supporters argue that cosmetic procedures give people control over their bodies. If a person has felt unhappy for many years because of a specific feature, such as a crooked nose or deep scars from an accident, minor surgery can dramatically improve how they feel. When these individuals look in the mirror after treatment, they often feel much happier and more outgoing in social situations. In this sense, modern medical technology allows people to overcome insecurities and live more confident lives.

On social media platforms like Instagram and TikTok, users see thousands of heavily edited photos with perfect skin and artificial body shapes. This constant exposure makes normal teenagers feel ugly and inadequate. Instead of learning to accept their natural appearance, young people feel pressured to spend thousands of dollars on painful operations that carry medical risks.

In my opinion, although reconstructive surgery is beneficial for people with severe injuries, the general obsession with cosmetic enhancement is dangerous. True self-esteem comes from inner character and achievements, not from chasing unrealistic standards created by commercial advertisers and social media algorithms.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 278 words

The normalization of elective cosmetic surgery and non-invasive aesthetic treatments has generated fierce public debate. While proponents champion these procedures as legitimate exercises of personal freedom that enhance psychological wellbeing, critics contend that they represent a capitulation to unrealistic beauty standards that exacerbate mental distress. Both viewpoints hold merit, but I believe the broader cultural consequences are overwhelmingly negative.

Advocates of aesthetic enhancement emphasize the principle of bodily autonomy and the tangible psychological benefits of corrective procedures. For individuals afflicted by severe physical anomalies, congenital defects, or noticeable facial asymmetry, undergoing a targeted intervention can alleviate years of debilitating self-consciousness. Professional studies frequently document measurable improvements in self-esteem, social confidence, and career assertiveness following successful cosmetic interventions. From this perspective, denying individuals the medical freedom to align their exterior appearance with their desired self-image would be an unwarranted paternalistic limitation.

Conversely, skeptics correctly identify the coercive societal forces driving this burgeoning industry. The cosmetic boom does not exist in a vacuum; it is aggressively stimulated by social media platforms that reward homogenous facial features. Young people are inundated with digitally altered representations of reality, creating widespread body dysmorphia and chronic dissatisfaction. Under such commercial pressure, the decision to seek cosmetic surgery is rarely fully autonomous; rather, it is a defensive reaction to impossible aesthetic benchmarks. Furthermore, aesthetic interventions can become addictive, as modifying one facial feature frequently reveals another imagined flaw, trapping vulnerable consumers in an endless cycle of dissatisfaction.

In conclusion, although aesthetic procedures can provide genuine emotional relief to certain individuals, their widespread commercial normalization poses a grave threat to collective mental health. Society should foster authentic self-acceptance rather than validating the predatory monetization of physical insecurity.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 286 words

The mainstreaming of elective aesthetic enhancements represents a pivotal battleground between individual libertarian agency and commodified cultural oppression. While champions of the cosmetic revolution frame surgical modification as an empowering triumph of self-invention, detractors argue that it reflects a profound crisis of collective self-worth orchestrated by visual media. While acknowledging the subjective relief such interventions may offer, I maintain that their cultural dominance reflects a deeply disempowering capitulation to corporate-manufactured ideals.

The case for cosmetic intervention rests predominantly upon bodily sovereignty and subjective utility. In a liberal society, individuals possess the inherent right to modify their physique according to their own aesthetic preferences. For people bearing severe congenital disfigurements, traumatic scarring, or disproportionate features that attract persistent ridicule, cosmetic surgery can function as an emancipatory technology. By rectifying physical attributes that trigger psychological trauma, surgical interventions can dismantle crippling social inhibitions and restore personal agency, enabling patients to engage with the world on their own terms.

Nevertheless, framing aesthetic modification solely as personal empowerment is profoundly naive. The soaring demand for injectable fillers, rhinoplasty, and body contouring is overwhelmingly fueled by an unregulated digital ecosystem that commodifies insecurity. Algorithmic platforms continuously amplify digitally manipulated images, normalizing unattainable facial geometries and somatic proportions. Consequently, normal biological variation is pathologized as a cosmetic defect requiring medical remediation. This commercial dynamic generates acute body dysmorphic distress, disproportionately targeting adolescent females who internalize the message that their social capital is entirely dependent upon conformity to an algorithmic monoculture.

In summary, while individual cosmetic interventions may occasionally mitigate deep-seated personal distress, the systemic proliferation of aesthetic medicine signifies a disturbing subjugation to commercialized beauty norms. Genuine empowerment lies not in surgically conforming to an artificial standard, but in expanding our cultural capacity to celebrate unstandardised human diversity.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 284 words

The ascendancy of cosmetic surgery from a discreet medical specialty into a ubiquitous consumer ritual exposes the central illusion of modern autonomy. Proponents applaud the surgical modification of the self as the ultimate realization of personal freedom—a triumph of will over biological circumstance. Yet this narrative of self-authorship collapses upon closer scrutiny: when millions of people across continents voluntarily purchase the same narrow set of surgically sculpted features, they are not expressing individuality, but executing a dictated standard.

The defense of aesthetic enhancement relies upon the rhetoric of therapeutic liberation. It is undeniably true that for the disfigured or traumatized, the restorative craft of the reconstructive surgeon performs a profound moral good, re-knitting the relationship between person and mirror. Furthermore, in an atomised culture where aesthetic capital yields tangible dividends in romance and employment, taking control of one's visage is a rational tactical investment.

Yet to interpret this dynamic as empowerment is to mistake the adaptation to coercion for freedom. The cosmetic industry does not respond to pre-existing human desires; it actively manufactures physical shame to cultivate captive consumers. By weaponizing digital platforms that filter human variation through the homogenizing lens of the camera algorithm, modern visual culture cultivates a chronic, ambient dysmorphia. The body is no longer experienced as an organism to inhabit, but as a defective raw material that must be continually disciplined, filled, and tightened to maintain societal currency.

Ultimately, the normalization of aesthetic modification achieves something sinister: it medicalizes normal human aging and natural idiosyncrasy, transforming human diversity into an embarrassing failure of consumer compliance. Real autonomy consists not in the freedom to alter one's flesh until it satisfies the algorithmic gaze, but in the courage to refuse its jurisdiction altogether.

## 104. Material Possessions and Genuine Happiness

**Type:** Two-part question · **Theme:** Psychology, Mental Health & Modern Lifestyle

> **Question:** In contemporary consumer societies, many individuals measure their personal worth and happiness by the quantity and prestige of the material goods they own. Why is this attitude so prevalent today? Do you consider this a positive or negative development for personal wellbeing?

**Useful vocabulary:** conspicuous consumption · hedonic treadmill · status anxiety · consumerist ethos · ephemeral gratification · intrinsic fulfilment

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: buying luxury items offers temporary pleasure, but quickly forces people onto an endless cycle of wanting more.*

**🟡 B1:** When people buy expensive new clothes or the latest phone, they feel excited for a few days. However, this happy feeling disappears very quickly. Soon, a newer product comes out in shops, and they feel unsatisfied again. Because they want to feel that excitement once more, they spend more money on another item. This endless shopping never brings true, long-lasting happiness.

**🔴 B2:** A major psychological flaw in consumerism is the phenomenon known as the hedonic treadmill. Purchasing prestige goods produces a transient surge of dopamine, creating the illusion of heightened satisfaction. Nevertheless, individuals rapidly adapt to their upgraded possessions, treating their new luxury items as the baseline normal. Consequently, they are driven into an exhausting cycle of perpetual acquisition, constantly seeking the next luxury product to recapture a momentary feeling of accomplishment that inevitably fades.

**🟣 C1:** The equating of material accumulation with happiness traps individuals on an insidious hedonic treadmill. While acquiring luxury commodities provides a fleeting neurological reward, this transient euphoria quickly dissipates as the novelty wanes and the possession is assimilated into daily routine. The resulting psychological void inevitably generates renewed status anxiety, compelling consumers to purchase ever more extravagant signifiers of wealth. This perpetual cycle displaces the pursuit of intrinsic goods—such as authentic friendships and creative mastery—which empirical psychology demonstrates are the true foundations of enduring wellbeing.

**⚫ C2:** The tragedy of the consumerist illusion is that material acquisition possesses an inherently diminishing emotional return. The thrill of the purchase is ephemeral by design; the market economy depends upon the rapid obsolescence not merely of products, but of the satisfaction they provide. Once the initial novelty cools, the commodity ceases to delight, leaving its owner with an empty ledger and a renewed hunger. By tethering psychological security to an ever-receding horizon of luxury, the consumer transforms what was intended as a feast into an insatiable famine.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 263 words

In modern society, many people believe that having a lot of money and owning luxury items, such as expensive cars, designer clothes, and large houses, is the key to a happy life. In this essay, I will explore the reasons why this belief is so widespread and explain why I consider it a very negative trend for individual wellbeing.

There are two main reasons why this attitude is so common today. First of all, advertising is everywhere. Every day, people see thousands of advertisements on television, billboards, and social media. These commercials convince people that buying a new product will make them look attractive, successful, and admired by others. Secondly, social media allows people to show off their best moments.
