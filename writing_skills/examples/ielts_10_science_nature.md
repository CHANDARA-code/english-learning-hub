## 91. Spending on Space Research

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Science

> **Question:** Some people think that the money spent on space exploration should be used to solve problems on Earth, such as poverty and disease. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** space exploration · satellites · technological spin-offs · budget priorities · climate monitoring · inspiration

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: satellites and space research help to solve problems on Earth.*

**🟡 B1:** Space research helps people on Earth every day. Satellites in space help us to forecast the weather, warn people about storms and use GPS maps on our phones. Farmers can also use satellite information to know when to plant their crops. For example, when a big storm is coming, satellites can show where it will go, so people have time to move to a safe place. So space money is not wasted.

**🔴 B2:** Much of the money spent on space is actually used to solve problems on Earth. Satellites provide the data needed for accurate weather forecasts, which allow authorities to warn communities about approaching storms and floods. They are also used to monitor climate change, deforestation and crop conditions, helping governments and farmers to plan more effectively. Moreover, satellite navigation and communication systems support everything from shipping to emergency services. Without investment in space technology, many of these essential services would not exist.

**🟣 C1:** The opposition between space exploration and earthly problems is, to a considerable extent, a false one, since much space activity is directed at Earth itself. Weather satellites underpin the forecasts that allow populations to evacuate before cyclones strike; Earth observation missions track deforestation, glacier retreat and sea-level rise, providing much of the evidence on which climate policy rests; and satellite navigation has become embedded in agriculture, logistics and emergency response. Cutting space budgets in the name of solving terrestrial problems would, in many cases, remove the very instruments with which those problems are measured.

**⚫ C2:** The strongest argument for looking up is that it helps us see down. Some of the most revealing images of our planet — shrinking ice sheets, the scars of illegal logging, the swirl of an approaching cyclone — are taken from orbit. A satellite that tells rice farmers when the rains will arrive, or shows officials which villages a flood has cut off, is not a luxury competing with human needs; it is part of meeting them. Much of what is dismissed as space spending is really spending on Earth, conducted from a better vantage point.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 282 words

Every year, some countries spend a lot of money on space research, such as sending rockets and satellites into space or exploring the Moon and Mars. Some people think this money should be used to help poor people and cure diseases on Earth. I partly disagree with this idea.

On the one hand, I understand why people think this. There are still many poor people who do not have enough food, clean water or good healthcare. If the money from space programmes was used for schools, hospitals and food, many lives could be improved. Some people also think that sending people to Mars is not important when there are so many problems on Earth.

On the other hand, space research also helps people on Earth. Firstly, satellites help us to forecast the weather and warn people about storms and floods, which saves many lives. Secondly, satellites help farmers, ships and drivers, for example with GPS maps. Thirdly, satellites help scientists to watch climate change and forests from space. In addition, many useful inventions, such as better water filters and medical equipment, came from space research.

Also, space research is only a small part of government spending. Most countries spend much more money on other things, such as defence and roads, than on space.

In my opinion, governments should continue to spend money on space research, but they should focus on projects that help people on Earth, such as satellites for weather and climate. They should also spend enough money on poverty and health.

In conclusion, I partly disagree that money for space should be used only for problems on Earth. Space research helps people in many ways, so it is also important.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 304 words

Space exploration is extremely expensive, and some people argue that the money would be better spent on urgent problems on Earth, such as poverty, hunger and disease. While I understand this view, I largely disagree with it, since space research brings significant benefits to people on Earth.

It is true that the costs of space programmes are high, and images of rockets being launched can seem difficult to justify while millions of people lack clean water, healthcare or education. From this perspective, spending billions on exploring distant planets may appear to reflect the wrong priorities.

However, much of the money spent on space directly benefits life on Earth. Satellites provide weather forecasts and early warnings of storms, which save many lives in countries affected by typhoons and floods. They also allow scientists to monitor climate change, deforestation and agricultural conditions, while satellite navigation and communication systems are essential for transport, business and emergency services. Furthermore, research carried out for space missions has led to many useful technologies, including improvements in water purification, medical imaging and materials.

In addition, space budgets represent only a small proportion of government spending in most countries, so eliminating them would not solve problems such as poverty. Such problems are often caused by political and economic issues rather than a lack of money alone. Space exploration also inspires young people to study science and engineering, which contributes to future innovation.

In my view, governments should continue to invest in space research, particularly in projects that bring clear benefits to people on Earth, while also ensuring that sufficient resources are dedicated to reducing poverty and improving healthcare.

In conclusion, although space exploration is costly, I disagree that its funding should be redirected entirely to earthly problems. Space research contributes to solving many of those problems and brings long-term benefits for humanity.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 294 words

The images are powerful: a rocket consuming millions of dollars of fuel in seconds, while elsewhere children lack clean water. It is understandable that some regard space exploration as an extravagance that should be sacrificed to more pressing needs. I would argue, however, that this view misunderstands both the nature of space spending and the causes of the problems it proposes to solve.

It should be acknowledged that the moral intuition behind the argument is sound: resources are finite, and public spending should reflect priorities. Prestige projects driven by national rivalry, or the growing industry of space tourism for the very wealthy, are difficult to defend when urgent human needs remain unmet.

Yet much space expenditure is directed at Earth itself. Meteorological satellites underpin forecasts that allow populations to evacuate before cyclones strike; Earth observation missions provide much of the evidence on climate change, deforestation and crop yields on which policy depends; and satellite navigation has become embedded in agriculture, logistics and emergency response. Research undertaken for space missions has also generated technologies with wide terrestrial applications, from medical imaging to water purification.

Moreover, the premise that redirecting space budgets would substantially reduce poverty or disease is questionable. Civil space programmes typically account for a small fraction of public expenditure, and persistent poverty is rarely caused by a simple lack of funds; it reflects governance, inequality and economic structures that money alone cannot resolve. Abolishing space research would therefore sacrifice its benefits without delivering the promised gains.

In conclusion, while specific space projects deserve scrutiny, the general claim that space funding should be diverted to earthly problems rests on a false opposition. Space research is, in large part, one of the ways humanity addresses those problems, and a modest investment in it remains justified.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 322 words

It is an old rhetorical move: set the cost of a rocket beside the cost of a hospital, and let the comparison speak for itself. The move is effective because it contains a moral truth — that public money should serve human need. But it is misleading, because it assumes that the rocket and the hospital are rivals, and that the rocket serves no human need at all.

Neither assumption survives scrutiny. Much of what we call space spending is aimed not at distant planets but at our own. Weather satellites give coastal communities the hours of warning that separate an orderly evacuation from a catastrophe; Earth-observation missions measure the melting of ice sheets and the destruction of forests, supplying much of the evidence on which climate policy rests; satellite navigation quietly guides ships, harvesters and ambulances. The technologies developed for space have found their way into medical imaging, water purification and countless everyday devices.

The comparison also flatters the idea that poverty persists merely for want of money. Civil space budgets are usually a small fraction of national spending, and cancelling them would release sums modest by comparison with the scale of global poverty — which is sustained less by empty treasuries than by corruption, conflict and inequality. Money redirected without addressing those causes would change little.

None of this means that every space project deserves public money. National prestige races and joyrides for billionaires are fair targets for criticism. But the case against them is specific, not general.

There is, finally, something harder to measure. Generations of children have become scientists because they once watched a launch or saw the photograph of Earth as a fragile blue sphere hanging in darkness. That image, taken from space, did more to make people care about this planet than many a lecture.

Space exploration, then, is not a rival to Earth's needs. Much of it is one of the tools with which we meet them.

## 92. Testing Medicines on Animals

**Type:** Discussion (both views + your opinion) · **Theme:** Science and ethics

> **Question:** Some people think that testing new medicines on animals is necessary, while others believe it is cruel and should be banned. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

**Useful vocabulary:** animal testing · clinical trials · side effects · animal welfare · alternative methods · replace, reduce, refine

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: new scientific methods can replace many animal tests.*

**🟡 B1:** Today, scientists have new ways to test medicines without using animals. They can use human cells in a laboratory or computer programs to see if a medicine is safe. These methods are sometimes faster and cheaper, and they do not hurt animals. For example, in the European Union, companies cannot sell cosmetics that were tested on animals. So I think scientists should use these new methods more.

**🔴 B2:** Scientific progress is making it increasingly possible to reduce animal testing. Researchers can now test many substances on human cells grown in laboratories, use computer models to predict how drugs will behave and even develop "organs-on-chips", which imitate the functions of human organs. Such methods can sometimes predict human reactions more accurately than animal tests, since animals do not always respond to drugs in the same way as people. The European Union has already banned the sale of cosmetics tested on animals, showing that alternatives can work in practice.

**🟣 C1:** The ethical dilemma of animal testing is being reshaped by scientific innovation. Techniques such as cultured human tissues, computer modelling and "organ-on-a-chip" devices, which reproduce aspects of human organ function in miniature, offer the prospect of replacing many animal experiments — and, in some cases, of producing more reliable results, since findings in animals frequently fail to translate to humans. The widely adopted principle of the "3Rs" — replacing, reducing and refining animal use — reflects this shift. The European Union's ban on marketing cosmetics tested on animals demonstrates that, where alternatives exist, regulation can accelerate their adoption.

**⚫ C2:** For decades, the question of animal testing was framed as a grim choice: animal suffering or human risk. Science is gradually dissolving that choice. Cultured human tissues, computer simulations and tiny devices that imitate the workings of human organs can now answer some of the questions that once required a living animal. Where such tools exist, the law can insist on them, as the European Union did when it banned cosmetics tested on animals. The more of these alternatives we build, the less often we will face the old dilemma at all.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 283 words

Before new medicines are sold, scientists must test them to check that they are safe. Some people think that testing medicines on animals, such as mice and rabbits, is necessary. Other people think it is cruel and should be stopped. In this essay, I will discuss both views.

On the one hand, some people believe animal testing is necessary. New medicines can be dangerous, and scientists need to know if they are safe before people use them. Animals have bodies that are similar to human bodies in some ways, so tests on animals can show possible problems. Many important medicines and vaccines were developed with the help of animal tests, and they have saved millions of human lives.

On the other hand, other people think animal testing is cruel. Animals can feel pain and fear, and many of them suffer or die in experiments. Also, animals are not the same as humans, so a medicine that is safe for a mouse may not be safe for a person. In addition, scientists now have new methods, such as testing on human cells in a laboratory or using computer programs. For example, the European Union has banned cosmetics that were tested on animals.

In my opinion, animal testing is still necessary for some important medicines now, but it should be reduced as much as possible. Scientists should use new methods when they can, and they should treat animals kindly and reduce their pain. Animal testing for cosmetics, like make-up, should be banned everywhere.

In conclusion, animal testing has helped to develop important medicines, but it causes suffering to animals. I believe we should reduce it step by step and use new methods in the future.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 305 words

Animal testing has long played a role in the development of new medicines, but it remains highly controversial. While some argue that it is essential for protecting human health, others believe that it is cruel and should be banned. In my view, animal testing is still necessary in certain cases, but it should be strictly limited and gradually replaced.

Those who support animal testing point out that it has contributed to many medical advances, including vaccines and treatments for serious diseases. Before a new drug is tested on humans, researchers need to understand how it affects a whole living organism, including its possible side effects on organs such as the heart and liver. Animal studies, they argue, provide this information in a way that cell cultures cannot fully replicate, and they protect human volunteers in clinical trials from unnecessary risks.

On the other hand, opponents argue that animal testing causes considerable suffering to millions of animals, which experience pain, stress and confinement. They also question its scientific value, since animals often react differently to drugs than humans, meaning that results do not always apply to people. Furthermore, new technologies, such as computer modelling and experiments using human cells and tissues, are making some animal tests unnecessary. The European Union's ban on cosmetics tested on animals shows that alternatives can be adopted successfully.

In my opinion, animal testing cannot yet be completely abolished for medicines, as some safety questions still require it. However, it should only be permitted when no alternative exists, under strict welfare standards, and governments should invest heavily in developing alternative methods. Testing for non-essential products, such as cosmetics, should be banned worldwide.

In conclusion, although animal testing has contributed to important medical advances, its ethical costs are significant. The goal should be to reduce and eventually replace it as scientific alternatives improve.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 317 words

Few scientific practices provoke such strong moral disagreement as the use of animals in medical research. Defenders regard it as a regrettable necessity that has underpinned many of modern medicine's achievements; opponents regard it as indefensible cruelty. I would argue that, while an immediate ban on all animal testing would endanger human safety, the practice should be treated as a temporary necessity to be progressively replaced.

The case for animal testing rests on the complexity of living organisms. A new drug may affect multiple organ systems in ways that cannot be predicted from isolated cells, and regulatory authorities have traditionally required evidence from animal studies before permitting trials in human volunteers. Historically, such research contributed to the development of numerous vaccines and treatments, and abandoning it abruptly could expose trial participants to unacceptable risks.

The case against, however, is both ethical and scientific. Animals used in research can experience pain, fear and distress, and the scale of their use raises serious moral questions. Moreover, the predictive value of animal studies is imperfect: species differ in physiology and metabolism, and a considerable proportion of drugs that appear safe and effective in animals subsequently fail in human trials. This suggests that animal models are sometimes not only ethically costly but scientifically unreliable.

These considerations support the principle known as the "3Rs": replacing animal use wherever possible, reducing the number of animals used, and refining procedures to minimise suffering. Advances in cultured human tissues, computational modelling and "organ-on-a-chip" technology are making replacement increasingly feasible, and regulatory change — such as the European Union's ban on marketing cosmetics tested on animals — can accelerate adoption.

In conclusion, animal testing remains, for now, a necessary element of medical safety, but it should be permitted only where no alternative exists and under rigorous welfare standards. The long-term goal should be its replacement by methods that are both more humane and more relevant to human biology.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 293 words

Every modern medicine cabinet contains, invisibly, the history of countless laboratory animals. That fact is uncomfortable, and it should be. The question is not whether animal testing has helped humanity — it plainly has — but whether its continued use can be justified now that alternatives are emerging.

The case for animal research remains stronger than its fiercest critics admit. A drug does not act on a single cell but on a whole organism, with its interacting systems of heart, liver, brain and blood. Before exposing human volunteers to a new compound, regulators have long demanded evidence of how it behaves in a living body, and many vaccines and treatments on which we now depend passed through that stage. To ban all animal testing tomorrow would be to transfer the risk from animals to people.

Yet two developments weaken that case year by year. The first is moral: our growing understanding of animal sentience makes it harder to dismiss the suffering of creatures capable of pain and fear as a mere cost of doing business. The second is scientific, and perhaps more decisive. Animals are imperfect models of human beings, and many drugs that appear promising in animal trials fail when tested on people. Techniques using human tissues, computer modelling and devices that mimic human organs on a chip promise tests that are not only kinder but, in some cases, more relevant to human biology.

The sensible course, therefore, is neither indignant abolition nor complacent continuation, but deliberate transition: allow animal testing only where no alternative exists, enforce strict welfare standards, and invest heavily in methods that make the practice unnecessary.

Animal testing, in short, belongs to the history of medicine. Our task is to make sure it does not also belong to its future.

## 93. Protecting Endangered Species

**Type:** Opinion (agree / disagree) · **Theme:** Nature

> **Question:** Some people think that spending money to protect endangered animal species is a waste, because there are more important problems facing humans. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

**Useful vocabulary:** endangered species · biodiversity · habitat loss · ecosystem services · conservation · extinction

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: protecting rare animals can create income for local people.*

**🟡 B1:** Protecting rare animals can also help local people. In Kratie province in Cambodia, many tourists come to see the Irrawaddy dolphins in the Mekong River. Local people earn money from boat trips, guesthouses and restaurants. If the dolphins disappear, these jobs will also disappear. So protecting animals can help both nature and people.

**🔴 B2:** Conservation can also bring direct economic benefits to local communities, particularly through eco-tourism. In Cambodia, for example, the rare Irrawaddy dolphins of the Mekong River near Kratie attract visitors from around the world, creating income for boat operators, guides, guesthouses and restaurants. In many parts of Africa and Asia, wildlife tourism is a major source of employment in rural areas where other opportunities are limited. When local people benefit from protecting wildlife, they have a strong incentive to support conservation efforts.

**🟣 C1:** The claim that conservation competes with human welfare overlooks the many cases in which the two are aligned. Wildlife tourism generates substantial income in regions where alternative livelihoods are scarce; along the Mekong near Kratie, for instance, visitors come to see the critically endangered Irrawaddy dolphin, supporting boat operators, guides and guesthouses. Where communities share in these benefits, conservation becomes a source of livelihood rather than a restriction imposed from outside — and local people often become the most effective guardians of the species on which their income depends.

**⚫ C2:** Along a quiet stretch of the Mekong near Kratie, fewer than a hundred river dolphins do something remarkable: they support a local economy. Visitors travel across the world to glimpse them, and their money pays for boats, guides, meals and beds. The dolphins' survival and the villagers' incomes have become the same project. This is conservation at its most persuasive — not a luxury imposed by distant donors, but an investment whose returns arrive in the pockets of the people who live alongside the animals.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 259 words

Many animals in the world, such as tigers, elephants and rare birds, are in danger of disappearing. Some people think that spending money to protect them is a waste, because people have more important problems. I disagree with this idea.

Firstly, animals are important for nature and for people. Every animal has a role in nature. For example, bees help plants to grow fruit and vegetables, and fish are important food for millions of people. If some animals disappear, other animals and plants can be affected, and this can harm people too.

Secondly, when a species disappears, it is gone forever. We cannot bring it back. Our children and grandchildren will never be able to see these animals. I think we have a responsibility to protect them for the future.

Thirdly, protecting animals can help people to earn money. For example, in Kratie province in Cambodia, many tourists come to see the rare Irrawaddy dolphins in the Mekong River. Local people earn money from boat trips and guesthouses.

On the other hand, some people say that the government should spend money on poor people, hospitals and schools first. I understand this idea, because these problems are very important. However, protecting animals does not cost very much compared with other government spending. Also, protecting forests and rivers helps both animals and people.

In conclusion, I disagree that protecting endangered animals is a waste of money. Animals are important for nature, for our future and for local jobs. I believe governments can help people and protect animals at the same time.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 284 words

Human activities, such as deforestation, hunting and pollution, have pushed many animal species towards extinction. Some people argue that spending money on protecting these species is wasteful when humans face urgent problems like poverty and disease. I strongly disagree with this view, as protecting endangered species also benefits people.

The first reason is that humans depend on healthy ecosystems. Every species plays a role in its environment, and the loss of one can affect many others. Bees and other insects, for example, pollinate a large proportion of the crops we eat, while healthy fish populations provide food and income for millions of people. Protecting endangered species usually means protecting their habitats, such as forests, rivers and coral reefs, which also provide clean water, prevent floods and store carbon.

Secondly, extinction is irreversible. Once a species disappears, it cannot be brought back, and future generations lose the chance to benefit from it. Many medicines have been developed from compounds found in plants and animals, so losing species may also mean losing potential cures.

Thirdly, conservation can support local economies. In Cambodia, the rare Irrawaddy dolphins of the Mekong River near Kratie attract tourists, providing income for local communities. When people benefit from wildlife, they are more likely to protect it.

Admittedly, governments must prioritise human needs such as healthcare and education. However, conservation budgets are usually small compared with other areas of public spending, so protecting wildlife does not prevent governments from addressing human problems. In fact, the two goals often support each other.

In conclusion, spending money on endangered species is not a waste. It protects the ecosystems on which humans depend, preserves irreplaceable natural heritage and can create sustainable livelihoods for local communities.

#### 🟣 C1 essay · IELTS 7–8 · 310 words

The argument that conservation is an unaffordable luxury in a world of human suffering has a certain moral force. Yet it rests on a false dichotomy: in most cases, protecting endangered species is not an alternative to addressing human needs but a means of doing so. I therefore strongly disagree that such spending is wasteful.

The most fundamental reason is that human welfare depends on biodiversity. Ecosystems provide services on which societies rely but rarely account for: pollination of crops, purification of water, regulation of floods, maintenance of fisheries and storage of carbon. Endangered species are frequently indicators of ecosystems under stress, and conserving them typically requires protecting the habitats that deliver these services. The collapse of a species can trigger cascading effects throughout an ecosystem, with consequences that ultimately reach human communities.

A second consideration is irreversibility. Most public spending decisions can be revisited, but extinction cannot be undone. Species represent millions of years of evolution and an unknown reservoir of potential value, including compounds of medical importance. To allow them to disappear is to close off options that future generations might have valued highly.

Third, conservation can support rather than compete with development. Wildlife tourism generates income in regions with few alternatives; along the Mekong near Kratie, the critically endangered Irrawaddy dolphin sustains a local tourism economy. Community-based conservation, in which local people share in the benefits of protecting wildlife, has often proved more effective than enforcement alone.

It must be conceded that resources are finite and that immediate human needs deserve priority. However, spending on conservation is small relative to public budgets as a whole, and the costs of ecological degradation — from collapsing fisheries to worsening floods — frequently exceed the costs of prevention.

In conclusion, protecting endangered species is not a sentimental indulgence but a prudent investment in the natural systems on which human prosperity ultimately rests.

#### ⚫ C2 essay · IELTS 8.5–9 · 320 words

To ask whether we should spend money on endangered animals when people are suffering is to frame the question as a choice between compassion and sentiment. It is a powerful framing, and a misleading one — because the natural world is not a separate charity competing for our donations. It is the ground on which every human economy stands.

Consider what disappears when species do. Insects pollinate a large share of the crops that feed us; mangroves and wetlands shield coastlines and filter water; fish stocks, once collapsed, may take decades to recover, taking livelihoods with them. Endangered species are often the visible symptom of an ecosystem in trouble, and saving them usually means saving the forests, rivers and reefs that quietly perform these services for free. Their disappearance is rarely an isolated loss; it is an early warning.

There is also the matter of finality. Almost every other policy mistake can be corrected — a tax lowered, a law repealed, a budget restored. Extinction is permanent. A species that took millions of years to evolve, and that might have held the key to a medicine or simply enriched the lives of generations to come, cannot be brought back by any later change of heart.

Nor is conservation always a cost. Along a stretch of the Mekong in Cambodia, a small population of river dolphins supports boat operators, guides and guesthouses. Where local people share in such benefits, they often become the species' most determined protectors.

It would be dishonest to pretend that every conservation project is a sound investment, or that human needs should not come first when lives are at stake. But the budgets involved are modest, and the costs of ecological collapse are anything but.

Protecting endangered species, then, is not a luxury we indulge once human problems are solved. It is part of solving them — and, unlike most of our choices, one we cannot postpone and revisit later.

## 94. Genetically Modified Food

**Type:** Advantages and disadvantages (outweigh) · **Theme:** Science and food

> **Question:** Scientists can now change the genes of plants to create new types of crops. Do the advantages of genetically modified food outweigh the disadvantages?

**Useful vocabulary:** genetically modified (GM) crops · crop yields · pest resistance · food security · biodiversity · seed patents

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: GM crops can resist pests and bad weather, so farmers grow more food.*

**🟡 B1:** Genetically modified crops can help farmers to grow more food. Scientists can make plants that are strong against insects, diseases or dry weather. Then farmers do not lose their crops and can use fewer chemicals. For example, farmers in India grow a type of cotton that insects do not like to eat. This is very helpful in poor countries where many people do not have enough food.

**🔴 B2:** One of the main advantages of genetically modified crops is that they can help to increase food production. Scientists can develop plants that resist insects, diseases or drought, which reduces crop losses and allows farmers to grow food in difficult conditions. In India, for example, insect-resistant cotton has been widely adopted by farmers because it protects the crop from a major pest. As climate change brings more extreme weather, crops designed to tolerate heat and drought could become increasingly important for global food security.

**🟣 C1:** The most compelling argument for genetic modification concerns food security in a changing climate. Conventional breeding can take many years to produce a new variety, whereas genetic engineering can introduce specific traits — resistance to pests, tolerance of drought or salinity — with greater speed and precision. Insect-resistant cotton, widely adopted in India, illustrates the approach's capacity to reduce losses to pests. As rising temperatures and erratic rainfall threaten harvests across the tropics, the ability to develop resilient crops quickly may prove not a luxury but a necessity.

**⚫ C2:** Humans have been changing the genes of plants for ten thousand years; we simply used to do it slowly and blindly, by choosing which seeds to replant. Modern maize bears little resemblance to its wild ancestor. Genetic engineering is, in one sense, only a faster and more precise version of an ancient practice. In a century of rising temperatures and failing rains, speed and precision may matter enormously: a crop that survives a drought is not a scientific curiosity, but the difference between a harvest and a famine.

### 📝 Model essays

#### 🟡 B1 essay · IELTS 4–5 · 268 words

Today, scientists can change the genes of plants to make new types of crops. This food is called genetically modified, or GM, food. Some people think it is very useful, but others worry about it. In my opinion, the advantages are bigger than the disadvantages.

There are several advantages. Firstly, GM crops can grow better. Scientists can make plants that are strong against insects, diseases and dry weather. So farmers lose fewer crops and can grow more food. This is very important because the world population is growing, and many people still do not have enough food. Secondly, GM crops can reduce the use of chemicals. If plants can protect themselves from insects, farmers do not need to spray so many pesticides. Thirdly, scientists can make food healthier, for example by adding more vitamins to rice.

However, there are also some disadvantages. Firstly, some people worry that GM food may not be safe to eat, although many scientists say that approved GM food is safe. Secondly, GM plants may spread into nature and affect other plants and animals. Thirdly, big companies own the seeds, and poor farmers may need to buy new seeds every year, which is expensive.

In my opinion, these problems can be controlled. Governments should test GM food carefully, and they should make rules to protect nature and help poor farmers. They should also label GM food, so people can choose.

In conclusion, GM food has some risks, but it can help farmers to grow more food, use fewer chemicals and make food healthier. I believe the advantages are greater, if governments control it well.

#### 🔴 B2 essay · IELTS 5.5–6.5 · 294 words

Genetic modification allows scientists to alter the DNA of crops in order to give them useful characteristics, such as resistance to pests or tolerance of drought. Although this technology raises understandable concerns, I believe that its advantages outweigh its disadvantages, provided that it is carefully regulated.

The most important advantage is the potential to improve food security. By creating crops that resist insects, diseases and extreme weather, genetic modification can reduce crop losses and increase yields, which is particularly important as the global population grows and climate change threatens harvests. ## 99. Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare

**Type:** Advantages and disadvantages (outweigh) · **Theme:** Science and health

> **Question:** Artificial intelligence is increasingly being used to help diagnose diseases and recommend treatments. Do the advantages of this outweigh the disadvantages?

### ✍️ One paragraph, four levels
*Idea: AI can bring diagnosis to places with few specialist doctors.*

**🟡 B1:** AI can help people in places without enough doctors. In many rural areas, there are very few specialist doctors. This can save people's sight and lives, especially in poor countries.

**🔴 B2:** One of the most important advantages of artificial intelligence in healthcare is its potential to improve access to diagnosis in areas with few specialists. AI systems can analyse medical images, such as X-rays and eye scans, and identify signs of disease quickly. In some countries, AI tools are already being used to screen patients with diabetes for eye damage that can lead to blindness. Such technology could allow nurses and local health workers to detect serious conditions early and refer patients for treatment, which is especially valuable in rural and low-income regions.

**🟣 C1:** Perhaps the greatest promise of medical AI lies not in wealthy hospitals but in places where specialists are scarce. Algorithms trained to interpret retinal photographs, chest X-rays or skin lesions can extend screening to populations that might otherwise wait months or years to see an expert. AI systems for detecting diabetic eye disease, for example, have been tested and used in several countries, enabling early referral for a condition that frequently causes preventable blindness. Used in this way, AI does not replace clinicians so much as extend their reach to those who currently have little access to them.

This is artificial intelligence at its most defensible: not replacing the doctor in a well-staffed hospital, but standing in, imperfectly and provisionally, where no doctor would otherwise be. The measure of medical AI should be less what it can do in the world's best hospitals than what it can do in the villages that specialists never reach.
