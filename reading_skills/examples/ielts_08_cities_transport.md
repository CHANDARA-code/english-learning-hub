### 📖 Adaptive Reading Passages (អត្ថបទអានសម្របតាមកម្រិតទាំង ៤: B1 · B2 · C1 · C2)

#### 🟡 B1 Level — Intermediate (IELTS 4.5–5.0 · ~150 words)
> In many modern cities, residents spend up to two hours every day commuting in cars or crowded buses to reach their offices, grocery supermarkets, doctors, and schools. This heavy reliance on automobiles creates terrible traffic jams, loud street noise, and dangerous air pollution that harms public health. To solve this problem, urban planners created an exciting new model called the 15-minute city.
>
> In a 15-minute city, neighborhoods are designed so that every resident can reach all essential daily needs—such as grocery markets, health clinics, primary schools, public parks, and workplaces—within a short 15-minute walk or bicycle ride from their front door. Pioneered in cities like Paris and Melbourne, this design replaces giant highways with wide pedestrian sidewalks and safe bicycle lanes. By reducing automobile traffic, 15-minute neighborhoods create cleaner air, strengthen community friendships, and give citizens more free time to enjoy with their families.
>


#### 🔴 B2 Level — Upper-Intermediate (IELTS 5.5–6.5 · ~200 words)
> The '15-minute city' urban planning framework represents a radical paradigm shift from twentieth-century car-centric suburban zoning toward hyper-local, human-scale proximity. Conceptualized by urbanist Carlos Moreno, the model posits that urban quality of life is inversely proportional to time spent in vehicular transit. By reintegrating residential, commercial, educational, and recreational land uses within a fifteen-minute pedestrian or cycling radius, cities can substantially reduce automobile dependency and urban greenhouse gas emissions.
>
> The implementation of chrono-urbanism principles requires transforming municipal infrastructure. Cities like Paris have actively repurposed roadway space into protected bicycle networks, transformed schoolyards into community public parks after hours, and incentivized decentralized local commercial amenities. Furthermore, by fostering dense, mixed-use neighborhoods, the 15-minute model revitalizes local retail economies, stimulates daily physical cardiovascular activity, and mitigates the urban heat island effect through expanded urban street canopies.
>


#### 🟣 C1 Level — Advanced (IELTS 7.0–8.0 · ~250 words)
> The theoretical architecture of the 15-minute city challenges the foundational tenets of modern urban zoning established by the 1933 Charter of Athens, which enforced the rigid spatial separation of residential, industrial, and commercial zones. That modernist paradigm locked cities into high-carbon automobile dependency, resulting in sprawling suburban expanses and congested arterial transit corridors that exacerbated socioeconomic spatial segregation.
>
> Chrono-urbanism proposes an ecological reorganization of urban space based on temporal proximity and functional polycentrism. However, urban geographers caution that deploying 15-minute frameworks without robust equity safeguards risks exacerbating spatial gentrification. In practice, high-amenity walkable neighborhoods often become financial enclaves affordable only to affluent cohorts, while marginalized working-class populations are pushed to peripheral, transit-starved peripheries where car dependency remains an economic necessity. Achieving equitable chrono-urbanism demands massive public investments in peripheral social housing, universal transit connectivity, and decentralized municipal public services.
>


#### ⚫ C2 Level — Proficiency (IELTS 8.5–9.0 · ~250 words)
> The 15-minute city is not a radical modern invention; it is the courageous rediscovery of the historic human habitat. For thousands of years before the invention of the internal combustion engine, the city was built for the human foot: a vibrant, walkable tapestry of homes, bakeries, workshops, and squares where neighbors met daily and children played safely on the streets. In surrendering our cities to the private automobile, we carved up our communities with asphalt highways, poisoned our air with toxic exhaust, and condemned our citizens to hours of soul-crushing traffic jams.
>
> To reclaim the 15-minute city is an act of profound civilizational restoration. It means declaring that the street belongs to the playing child, the walking elder, and the cycling worker, not the two-ton steel machine. When every essential need of human life is within a peaceful walk through tree-lined streets, the city ceases to be an alienating concrete jungle and becomes once again a living, breathing community. We must tear up the asphalt, plant the gardens, and rebuild cities where human beings can live with dignity, tranquility, and community connection.
>

### 📖 Adaptive Reading Passages (អត្ថបទអានសម្របតាមកម្រិតទាំង ៤: B1 · B2 · C1 · C2)

#### 🟡 B1 Level — Intermediate (IELTS 4.5–5.0 · ~150 words)
> In modern transportation systems, traveling between major cities has traditionally meant choosing between slow highway driving and expensive commercial flights. However, high-speed rail networks—featuring bullet trains traveling at speeds over three hundred kilometers per hour—have transformed regional travel across Japan, China, and Western Europe.
>
> High-speed trains provide immense benefits for both passengers and the natural environment. Unlike commercial jet airplanes that emit huge quantities of greenhouse gases into the upper atmosphere, electric bullet trains run on clean electricity. Passengers can walk directly onto a train in the center of one city and arrive in downtown of another city two hours later, avoiding airport security lines and highway traffic jams. Furthermore, high-speed rail lines bring new businesses, tourist money, and jobs to smaller secondary towns located along the rail corridor.
>


#### 🔴 B2 Level — Upper-Intermediate (IELTS 5.5–6.5 · ~200 words)
> The strategic deployment of high-speed rail (HSR) infrastructure serves as a powerful catalyst for regional economic integration and transport decarbonization. Operating at velocities exceeding three hundred kilometers per hour on dedicated grade-separated track alignments, HSR systems provide competitive transit times that dramatically outcompete domestic short-haul commercial aviation over distances up to eight hundred kilometers. Because electric high-speed trains emit up to ninety percent less greenhouse gas emissions per passenger-kilometer than commercial aviation, HSR constitutes a cornerstone of sustainable transportation statecraft.
>
> Economically, HSR networks stimulate regional agglomeration economies. By collapsing intercity travel times, high-speed corridors integrate secondary and tertiary cities into unified megaregional labor markets. Smaller regional municipalities along the transit corridor experience capital influxes, tourism growth, and corporate office relocations, effectively counterbalancing economic overconcentration in primary capital metropolises. However, HSR projects require colossal upfront capital expenditures, extensive tunneling, and multi-decade debt amortization, demanding sustained federal fiscal commitments.
>


#### 🟣 C1 Level — Advanced (IELTS 7.0–8.0 · ~250 words)
> The spatial economics of high-speed rail networks reveals complex territorial dynamics between central metropolises and peripheral regions. Classical spatial equilibrium models suggest that transport infrastructure reduces transaction costs and equalizes regional economic disparities. In operational practice, HSR often exhibits what regional economists designate as the 'tunnel effect': trains accelerate through intermediate peripheral territories, consolidating financial and technological capital in primary terminal metropolises while bypassing intermediate rural communities.
>
> To ensure HSR catalyzes inclusive polycentric regional growth rather than core-periphery drain, infrastructural planning must integrate robust secondary rail feeder networks. High-speed rail terminal hubs must be designed as multimodal transit-oriented development (TOD) districts integrated with municipal subways, light rail, and regional bus fleets. Without synchronized intermodal connectivity, high-speed terminals degenerate into isolated spatial islands, failing to diffuse economic benefits into surrounding regional economies.
>


#### ⚫ C2 Level — Proficiency (IELTS 8.5–9.0 · ~250 words)
> The high-speed bullet train is the triumph of public transportation over individual atomization: a glorious ribbon of steel that ties distant cities, landscapes, and human lives into an interconnected regional community. To step onto a high-speed train is to experience the beauty of civilized travel: watching the countryside glide past the window in serene silence, moving smoothly at the speed of an airplane while resting comfortably on the earth.
>
> In an era of accelerating climate chaos, high-speed rail is a technological miracle of ecological redemption. It proves that human mobility does not require the deafening roar of jet engines burning kerosene or the suffocating exhaust of highway gridlock. In connecting small regional towns to great metropolises, high-speed rail breathes economic vitality into the forgotten provinces, allowing citizens to live in peace near nature while working in the great centers of culture. High-speed rail is an investment in the common wealth of the nation, reminding us that true progress moves together, on tracks built for all people.
>

### 📖 Adaptive Reading Passages (អត្ថបទអានសម្របតាមកម្រិតទាំង ៤: B1 · B2 · C1 · C2)

#### 🟡 B1 Level — Intermediate (IELTS 4.5–5.0 · ~150 words)
> In major metropolitan cities around the world, finding an affordable place to live has become an agonizing struggle for working-class families. In cities like New York, London, and Sydney, monthly apartment rents have doubled over the last decade, while workers' wages have remained flat. Firefighters, nurses, grocery clerks, and teachers frequently spend more than half of their monthly take-home pay simply to rent a modest apartment.
>
> This housing crisis is often worsened by gentrification. When wealthy investors and high-income professionals move into historic, low-income working-class neighborhoods, they renovate old brick apartment buildings and open expensive cafes and boutique stores. While this brings new investment and lowers crime, property values and property taxes skyrocket. Long-time residents who have lived in the neighborhood for generations are priced out and forced to move far away from their jobs and communities.
>


#### 🔴 B2 Level — Upper-Intermediate (IELTS 5.5–6.5 · ~200 words)
> The structural crisis of housing affordability and the acceleration of neighborhood gentrification constitute severe socioeconomic crises in the modern metropolis. Neoliberal economic policies have transformed residential real estate from a social necessity into a high-yield speculative global financial asset. Private equity consortia and real estate investment trusts (REITs) acquire massive residential housing portfolios, driving astronomical property inflation and rent escalations that drastically outpace median wage growth.
>
> Gentrification operates as a dual-edged spatial process. While incoming capital investment revitalizes decaying municipal physical infrastructure and expands local tax revenues, it initiates the involuntary displacement of historic lower-income residents. > Adaptive reuse models conceptualize buildings as dynamic, layered entities whose functional utility can be iteratively reconfigured across historical epochs. Converting decommissioned textile mills, dockside grain silos, or theological seminaries into contemporary mixed-use housing or research hubs requires sophisticated structural retrofitting, seismic reinforcement, and thermal envelope upgrades.
