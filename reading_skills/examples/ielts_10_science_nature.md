### 📖 Adaptive Reading Passages (អត្ថបទអានសម្របតាមកម្រិតទាំង ៤: B1 · B2 · C1 · C2)

#### 🟡 B1 Level — Intermediate (IELTS 4.5–5.0 · ~150 words)
> Inside every living cell in our bodies lies DNA, the microscopic chemical instruction manual that tells our cells how to grow and function. For decades, scientists knew that tiny spelling mistakes or mutations in this DNA code cause painful hereditary illnesses like sickle-cell anemia, cystic fibrosis, and muscular dystrophy. In the past, doctors could only treat the painful symptoms of these illnesses because changing living human genes was almost impossible.
>
> Everything changed in 2012 when scientists invented a revolutionary molecular tool called CRISPR-Cas9. Functioning like microscopic genetic scissors, CRISPR allows doctors to find a faulty gene inside living human cells, cut it out with incredible precision, and replace it with a healthy copy. Today, doctors have already used CRISPR to cure patients with severe sickle-cell blood disorders, opening a miraculous new frontier in modern clinical medicine.
>


#### 🔴 B2 Level — Upper-Intermediate (IELTS 5.5–6.5 · ~200 words)
> The development of the CRISPR-Cas9 endonuclease system—adapted from an adaptive bacterial immune mechanism against viral phages—has revolutionized molecular biology and precision therapeutics. Unlike earlier, cumbersome gene-editing methodologies such as zinc-finger nucleases, CRISPR employs a programmable synthetic guide RNA (gRNA) molecule that directs the Cas9 enzyme to execute targeted double-strand breaks at precise coordinates within the cellular genome.
>
> The therapeutic implications of precision genetic editing are profound. Clinical trials have achieved groundbreaking functional cures for monogenic blood disorders, specifically beta-thalassemia and sickle-cell disease, by editing patient hematological stem cells ex vivo before retransplantation. However, this transformative bio-technological breakthrough introduces daunting bioethical quandaries. Concerns center on inadvertent off-target cleavages, cellular mosaicism, and, most critically, the ethical perils of germline editing—altering the genetic code of human embryos in ways that permanently modify future human generations and risk resurrecting eugenic ambitions.
>


#### 🟣 C1 Level — Advanced (IELTS 7.0–8.0 · ~250 words)
> The philosophical and political economy of the CRISPR genomic revolution exposes deep tensions between democratized therapeutic liberation and commodified transhumanist biopolitics. In oncology and rare genetic pathologies, somatic CRISPR interventions offer unparalleled clinical efficacy, systematically dismantling the biological determinism of inherited affliction. By transitioning therapeutics from chronic pharmaceutical symptom suppression to permanent curative genomic rewrites, molecular medicine promises to alter global epidemiologic trajectories.
>
> However, the unregulated commercialization of genomic modification threatens to inaugurate a regime of molecular socio-biological stratification. Should germline enhancement—optimizing cognitive resilience, athletic endurance, or cosmetic traits—become privatized within market economies, socioeconomic inequality will be inscribed directly into the human biological genome. Furthermore, the intellectual property monopolies governing CRISPR patents concentrate unprecedented sovereign power over the fundamental biological code of terrestrial life in a handful of corporate conglomerates, demanding stringent global regulatory treaties to preserve human dignity.
>


#### ⚫ C2 Level — Proficiency (IELTS 8.5–9.0 · ~250 words)
> For four billion years, the great book of terrestrial life was written slowly by the patient hand of evolution—one random mutation, one season of trial, one quiet adaptation at a time. Every creature that walks the earth carries within its cells a sacred chemical poem written across geological epochs. Today, for the very first time in planetary history, one solitary species has learned how to pick up the pen and rewrite the foundational code of living things.
>
> In this newfound genetic power lies both divine compassion and terrifying danger. When CRISPR is used to cure a child of agony, to heal broken blood cells, or to banish hereditary blindness, it is the highest expression of human mercy. But if we grow arrogant, if we succumb to the hubris of designing human embryos to order or breeding children like commercial products, we step upon a precipice of monstrous hubris. We must wield the scissors of life with reverent trembling, remembering that wisdom consists not in having the power to change nature, but knowing when to bow before its mystery.
>

### 📖 Adaptive Reading Passages (អត្ថបទអានសម្របតាមកម្រិតទាំង ៤: B1 · B2 · C1 · C2)

#### 🟡 B1 Level — Intermediate (IELTS 4.5–5.0 · ~150 words)
> For thousands of years, humans have looked up into the dark night sky and wondered: Are we alone in the universe? Until just thirty years ago, astronomers did not know for sure whether other stars had planets orbiting them at all. Today, thanks to powerful space telescopes like Kepler and the James Webb Space Telescope, astronomers have discovered more than 5,000 alien worlds, known as exoplanets, orbiting distant stars across our Milky Way galaxy.
>
> Astronomers are especially eager to find rocky planets located in the 'habitable zone'—the comfortable orbital sweet spot where temperatures are neither too boiling hot nor too freezing cold, allowing liquid water oceans to exist. By analyzing the starlight shining through an exoplanet's atmosphere, scientists can search for chemical biosignatures: telltale atmospheric gases like oxygen, ozone, and methane that are produced by living microorganisms.
>


#### 🔴 B2 Level — Upper-Intermediate (IELTS 5.5–6.5 · ~200 words)
> Astrobiology has entered an empirical golden age driven by high-resolution transmission spectroscopy and transit photometry. Utilizing sophisticated orbital observatories—principally the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)—astrophysicists analyze the chemical compositions of exoplanetary atmospheres by measuring the selective absorption of stellar wavelengths as planets transit their host stars. This methodology enables the precise spectral identification of volatile gases, including water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, and sulfur dioxide.
>
> The search for extraterrestrial biological activity centers upon identifying atmospheric biosignatures in chemical disequilibrium. On Earth, the simultaneous presence of abundant molecular oxygen and methane constitutes a definitive thermodynamic disequilibrium that could only be sustained by photosynthetic vegetation and biological methanogens. However, astrophysicists exercise immense scientific caution regarding 'false-positive' biosignatures, as abiotic photochemical reactions driven by stellar ultraviolet irradiation can also synthesize molecular oxygen on desiccated desert planets.
>


#### 🟣 C1 Level — Advanced (IELTS 7.0–8.0 · ~250 words)
> The epistemological detection of exoplanetary biosignatures confronts the foundational problem of biological definition: how to identify life when constrained by a terrestrial sample size of one. Planetary atmospheres represent complex geochemical reactors governed by stellar irradiation, volcanic outgassing, and atmospheric escape dynamics. To authenticate an authentic biosignature, astrobiologists must establish that observed atmospheric deviations—such as anomalous concentrations of dimethyl sulfide or methane-ozone disequilibrium—cannot be generated through abiotic geodynamic equilibria.
>
> The discovery of extraterrestrial biology will precipitate profound scientific, philosophical, and metaphysical reassessments of anthropogenic uniqueness. Whether microbial or complex, confirmation of independent biogenesis beyond Earth would demonstrate that the emergence of life is an intrinsic cosmic imperative rather than an extraordinary terrestrial accident. The Drake equation and Fermi paradox would pivot from speculative probabilistic exercises into empirical fields of comparative planetary ecology, forever transforming humanity's cosmic self-understanding.
>


#### ⚫ C2 Level — Proficiency (IELTS 8.5–9.0 · ~250 words)
> Look up at the stars on a clear, dark night. Every twinkling point of light is a blazing sun, and around almost every sun circle worlds beyond numbering—silent ice worlds, ocean planets beneath pink skies, and ancient rocks orbiting red dwarf stars for billions of years. To stand upon this little blue oasis of Earth and believe that life arose only here is to betray the infinite abundance of the cosmos.
>
> Somewhere out across the ocean of black space, light from a distant star is filtering through the clouds of an alien world right now, carrying the chemical scent of alien oceans, alien leaves, or alien microbes breathing beneath alien suns. The discovery of even the humblest extraterrestrial bacterium will not make humanity smaller; it will bring us home. It will tell us that life is not a lonely, accidental spark in a cold, dead void, but the universe itself waking up, opening its eyes, and marveling at its own eternal grandeur.
>

> Miles beneath the ocean surface lies the abyssal plain, a pitch-black, freezing realm where sunlight never reaches and water pressure is immense. Despite these extreme conditions, the deep ocean floor is home to extraordinary wildlife: glowing bioluminescent jellyfish, ghost octopuses, and ancient glass sponges that live for thousands of years. The seabed is also scattered with dark, potato-sized rocks called polymetallic nodules.
>
> These mineral nodules contain massive amounts of rare metals—such as cobalt, nickel, manganese, and copper—that manufacturers desperately need to build electric vehicle batteries, smartphones, and solar panels. Mining companies now want to drop heavy robotic crawler machines four thousand meters underwater to scrape up these nodules. However, marine biologists warn that this industrial dredging could create giant, suffocating mud plumes and destroy fragile deep-sea ecosystems that took millions of years to form.
>


#### 🔴 B2 Level — Upper-Intermediate (IELTS 5.5–6.5 · ~200 words)
> The impending industrialization of the deep seabed for critical energy-transition minerals represents an unprecedented environmental dilemma. Vast fields of polymetallic nodules, particularly within the Clarion-Clipperton Zone (CCZ) in the central Pacific Ocean, concentrate millions of metric tons of cobalt, nickel, and rare earth elements essential for global battery manufacturing. Proponents assert that seabed extraction generates far fewer carbon emissions, avoids terrestrial deforestation, and eliminates the child labor scandals associated with terrestrial cobalt mining.
>
> Conversely, marine oceanographers emphasize that abyssal ecosystems are characterized by extreme metabolic stasis, negligible sedimentation rates, and exquisite fragility. Instead of splitting heavy uranium atoms apart—which is how conventional nuclear fission plants work—fusion forces two light hydrogen atoms to fuse together into helium, releasing astonishing amounts of clean energy. Fusion fuel comes from seawater, produces zero greenhouse gas emissions, and creates no long-lived radioactive nuclear waste. >

When the first commercial fusion plant illuminates our homes, it will mark the end of the age of burning.
