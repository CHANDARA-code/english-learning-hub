## B2 Part 6: Philosophy & Abstract Ideas (4751–5000)

### 4751. philosophy /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** 철학 / វិជ្ជា철학 / 철학
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទស្សនវិជ្ជា
1. If we study philosophy seriously, we begin to question assumptions that most people take for granted.
2. The philosophy of mind explores whether consciousness can be fully explained by physical processes.
3. Her philosophy of life, which emphasizes compassion over ambition, has shaped every decision she has made.

---

### 4752. philosopher /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អ្នកទស្សនវិជ្ជា
1. The ancient philosopher argued that virtue is the only true good and that wealth means nothing without it.
2. Had the philosopher lived longer, he might have developed his theory of knowledge into a complete system.
3. A philosopher who refuses to engage with opposing views is unlikely to arrive at genuine truth.

---

### 4753. ethics /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សីលធម៌ / គតិសីលធម៌
1. Medical ethics requires that doctors balance the patient's autonomy with the duty to do no harm.
2. If a company's ethics are compromised for profit, it risks losing the trust of both customers and employees.
3. The ethics of artificial intelligence are being debated widely, as its consequences for humanity remain uncertain.

---

### 4754. morality /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សីលធម៌ / គុណធម៌
1. Morality, which differs across cultures, raises the question of whether any universal moral standard can exist.
2. If morality is purely subjective, it becomes difficult to condemn actions that most people consider clearly wrong.
3. The relationship between law and morality is complex, since not everything legal is moral, and vice versa.

---

### 4755. virtue /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** គុណធម៌ / សីលគុណ
1. Aristotle believed that virtue is not merely an act but a habit cultivated through repeated practice.
2. If we define virtue as excellence of character, then a virtuous person acts well even when no one is watching.
3. Courage, which Aristotle considered a cardinal virtue, lies between the extremes of cowardice and recklessness.

---

### 4756. vice /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** គុណវិបត្តិ / ទោសធម៌
1. The philosopher argued that vice arises when reason fails to govern the passions and desires of the individual.
2. If we are too indulgent in any single vice, it gradually corrupts the entire structure of our character.
3. Pride, which was considered the worst of all vices in medieval moral philosophy, leads to every other sin.

---

### 4757. good /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ល្អ / ប្រពៃ
1. Philosophers have long debated what it means to be truly good, beyond simply following the rules of society.
2. If an action produces the greatest good for the greatest number, utilitarians consider it morally justified.
3. The concept of the good, as described by Plato, exists independently of any particular good thing we can observe.

---

### 4758. evil /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អំពើអាក្រក់ / ភាពអាក្រក់
1. The problem of evil challenges the belief in an all-powerful, all-knowing, and perfectly good God.
2. If evil is simply the absence of good, as some theologians argue, then it has no independent existence of its own.
3. Hannah Arendt's concept of the "banality of evil" suggests that great harm can be done by ordinary, thoughtless people.

---

### 4759. justice /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** យុត្តិធម៌
1. Justice, which demands that people receive what they are fairly owed, is a cornerstone of any stable society.
2. If justice is blind, it must apply equally to the powerful and the powerless without any distinction.
3. Rawls argued that justice requires us to design society from behind a "veil of ignorance," not knowing our place in it.

---

### 4760. injustice /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អយុត្តិធម៌
1. Injustice, wherever it is tolerated, eventually undermines the legitimacy of the entire social order.
2. If we remain silent in the face of injustice, we become complicit in perpetuating the very harm we claim to oppose.
3. The philosopher argued that injustice done to one member of society weakens the moral fabric of the whole community.

---

### 4761. truth /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សេចក្តីពិត / ការពិត
1. The pursuit of truth, even when it is uncomfortable, is one of the highest obligations of the intellectual life.
2. If truth is socially constructed, as some postmodernists claim, then objective knowledge becomes extremely difficult to defend.
3. Philosophers have proposed many theories of truth, including correspondence, coherence, and pragmatic accounts.

---

### 4762. falsehood /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការកុហក / សេចក្តីមិនពិត
1. A falsehood repeated often enough can eventually be mistaken for truth by those who have never examined it critically.
2. If we allow falsehood to go unchallenged in public discourse, it poisons the well of democratic deliberation.
3. The philosopher distinguished between innocent falsehoods and deliberate deception, arguing that only the latter is morally wrong.

---

### 4763. knowledge /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ចំណេះដឹង / ចំណេះ
1. Epistemologists define knowledge as justified true belief, though this definition has been challenged by Gettier's thought experiments.
2. If knowledge requires certainty, then very little of what we claim to know would actually qualify as genuine knowledge.
3. The distinction between propositional knowledge and practical knowledge is crucial for understanding what it means to truly know something.

---

### 4764. ignorance /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពល្ងង់ / ការខ្វះចំណេះ
1. Socrates famously claimed that true wisdom begins with the recognition of one's own ignorance.
2. If we mistake our ignorance for certainty, we are unlikely to seek the further understanding that the situation requires.
3. Willful ignorance, which involves refusing to learn uncomfortable truths, is considered morally blameworthy by many philosophers.

---

### 4765. wisdom /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ប្រាជ្ញា / សតិបញ្ញា
1. Wisdom involves not only the accumulation of knowledge but also the judgment to apply it appropriately in complex situations.
2. If wisdom could be taught in a single lesson, philosophers would not have spent millennia debating its nature and conditions.
3. The ancient Greeks distinguished between sophia, which is theoretical wisdom, and phronesis, which is practical wisdom or good judgment.

---

### 4766. foolishness /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពល្ងង់ / សភាពល្ងង់ខ្លៅ
1. Foolishness is not simply a lack of knowledge but a failure to apply the knowledge one already possesses wisely.
2. If we confuse boldness with foolishness, we risk dismissing courageous actions that challenge unjust conventions.
3. Erasmus, in "The Praise of Folly," suggested that what society calls foolishness is often closer to genuine wisdom.

---

### 4767. reason /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ហេតុផល / ការគិតដោយហេតុផល
1. Kant argued that reason, when used correctly, can arrive at universal moral principles without relying on experience.
2. If reason alone could solve all human problems, the Enlightenment might have produced a more perfect world than it did.
3. The tension between reason and emotion has been central to Western philosophy since the ancient Greeks first debated it.

---

### 4768. emotion /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អារម្មណ៍ / ចិត្ដារម្មណ៍
1. Some philosophers argue that emotion is not opposed to reason but is in fact a form of rational evaluation of the world.
2. If emotion were entirely unreliable as a guide to action, moral intuitions that feel deeply right would carry no weight at all.
3. The Stoics believed that destructive emotions arise from false beliefs and can be overcome through rational self-examination.

---

### 4769. logic /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** តក្កវិជ្ជា / ហេតុផលតក្ក
1. Logic provides the formal rules by which valid arguments can be distinguished from those that only appear valid.
2. If an argument is logically valid but based on false premises, its conclusion may still be entirely wrong.
3. Formal logic, which was systematized by Aristotle, remains the foundation of much contemporary philosophical analysis.

---

### 4770. intuition /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការយល់ដឹងដោយខ្លួនឯង / ហ្ស្វដ្ឋានចិត្ត
1. Moral intuition, even without explicit justification, often serves as an important starting point for ethical reasoning.
2. If we always defer to intuition over argument, we risk preserving biases that careful reflection would reveal as unjustified.
3. Some philosophers treat intuition as a reliable cognitive faculty, while others view it as merely the residue of cultural conditioning.

---

### 4771. rationalism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីហេតុផលនិយម
1. Rationalism, which holds that reason is the primary source of knowledge, was championed by philosophers such as Descartes and Leibniz.
2. If rationalism is correct, then the mind contains innate ideas that are not derived from sensory experience at all.
3. The debate between rationalism and empiricism shaped much of early modern philosophy and was synthesized by Kant in the eighteenth century.

---

### 4772. empiricism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីបទពិសោធន៍និយម
1. Empiricism maintains that all genuine knowledge ultimately derives from sensory experience rather than from pure reason alone.
2. If empiricism is true, then concepts that cannot be traced back to experience are either meaningless or purely formal.
3. Locke, Berkeley, and Hume are the three great British empiricists whose work challenged the rationalist tradition on the continent.

---

### 4773. idealism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីគំនិតនិយម / ឧត្ដមគតិ
1. Idealism, in its philosophical sense, claims that reality is fundamentally mental or dependent on the mind for its existence.
2. If idealism were true, the physical world as we experience it would be a kind of mental construction rather than an independent reality.
3. Hegel's absolute idealism attempted to show that all of reality is the unfolding of a single rational mind through history.

---

### 4774. realism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីពិតនិយម / ការប្រឈមមុខជាមួយចំពោះការពិត
1. Philosophical realism asserts that the world exists independently of our perception or conceptual frameworks about it.
2. If realism is correct, then scientific theories that successfully predict phenomena are genuinely describing the structure of reality.
3. The debate between realism and anti-realism in philosophy of science concerns whether unobservable entities like electrons truly exist.

---

### 4775. pragmatism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីប្រើប្រាស់ជាក់ស្ដែង / ក្បួនជោគជ័យ
1. Pragmatism, developed by Peirce, James, and Dewey, holds that the meaning and truth of ideas lie in their practical consequences.
2. If pragmatism is right, then a belief is true insofar as it works — that is, insofar as it helps us navigate experience successfully.
3. Critics of pragmatism argue that equating truth with usefulness collapses the important distinction between what is and what is beneficial.

---

### 4776. existentialism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីអត្ថិភាពនិយម
1. Existentialism emphasizes individual freedom and responsibility, insisting that existence precedes essence and that we define ourselves through choice.
2. If existentialism is correct, there is no pre-given human nature, and each person must create their own meaning in an indifferent universe.
3. Sartre, Camus, and Beauvoir are among the most important existentialist thinkers, each developing the tradition in a distinctive direction.

---

### 4777. nihilism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីអនិច្ចនិយម / ការប្រកាន់ខ្ជាប់ថាគ្មានអ្វីមានអត្ថន័យ
1. Nihilism, in its strongest form, denies that life has any objective meaning, value, or purpose whatsoever.
2. If nihilism is true, moral judgments are not genuine claims about the world but merely expressions of personal preference.
3. Nietzsche diagnosed nihilism as the great crisis of Western culture following the "death of God" and the collapse of traditional values.

---

### 4778. absurdism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីអសារៈនិយម
1. Absurdism, associated with Albert Camus, holds that the human search for meaning collides with a universe that offers none.
2. If absurdism is embraced rather than denied, one can live defiantly and joyfully in spite of the universe's indifference.
3. Camus argued that Sisyphus, condemned to roll his boulder forever, must be imagined as happy because he has accepted the absurd.

---

### 4779. stoicism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីស្ថឹរភាពចិត្ត / ស្ថាបនា
1. Stoicism teaches that we should focus only on what is within our control and accept with equanimity everything that is not.
2. If stoicism is practiced consistently, it can reduce anxiety by helping us distinguish between what we can and cannot change.
3. Marcus Aurelius, a Roman emperor and Stoic philosopher, recorded his meditations as a personal guide to living according to reason.

---

### 4780. epicureanism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីអព្ភ័យសម្ភារ / ការស្វែងរកអំណរស្ងប់ស្ងៀម
1. Epicureanism, contrary to popular misconception, advocates not excess but the simple pleasures of friendship, reflection, and tranquility.
2. If epicureanism is adopted as a philosophy of life, material wealth becomes far less important than peace of mind.
3. Epicurus argued that the fear of death is irrational, since when death is present we no longer exist to experience it.

---

### 4781. utilitarianism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីប្រយោជន៍និយម
1. Utilitarianism, developed by Bentham and refined by Mill, judges actions by their consequences for overall happiness or well-being.
2. If utilitarianism is applied strictly, it could justify harming one innocent person if doing so would benefit a much larger number.
3. Critics of utilitarianism argue that it ignores the separateness of persons by treating individual rights as negotiable against aggregate welfare.

---

### 4782. deontology /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីករណីករ / សីលធម៌ដោយតាមច្បាប់
1. Deontology holds that the morality of an action depends on whether it conforms to a rule or duty, not on its consequences.
2. If deontology is correct, lying is always wrong even if telling the truth in a particular situation would lead to terrible outcomes.
3. Kant's categorical imperative, the central principle of his deontological ethics, commands us to act only on maxims we could universalize.

---

### 4783. consequentialism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីផ្ទើលផ្ទំ / ការវិនិច្ឆ័យដោយផ្ទើលផ្ទំ
1. Consequentialism evaluates the rightness of actions solely by the value of the outcomes they produce in the world.
2. If consequentialism is true, a deceptive action that happens to produce great benefit would be morally praiseworthy, not blameworthy.
3. The challenge for any consequentialist theory is to specify what counts as a good outcome and how different outcomes should be compared.

---

### 4784. relativism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីទំនាក់ទំនងនិយម / ភាពខុសប្លែកតាមវប្បធម៌
1. Moral relativism holds that ethical judgments are true or false only relative to the cultural framework of the person making them.
2. If relativism is adopted completely, it becomes impossible to criticize the moral practices of another culture on any principled ground.
3. The challenge for relativism is to explain why, if all moral views are equally valid, we should prefer tolerance over intolerance.

---

### 4785. absolutism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីផ្ដាច់ការ / ភាពផ្ដាច់ការ
1. Moral absolutism maintains that certain actions are always right or wrong regardless of context, culture, or consequences.
2. If absolutism is true, then there are moral truths that hold universally, even when they conflict with the customs of particular societies.
3. Critics of moral absolutism argue that it fails to account for the complexity and contextual variation of real ethical situations.

---

### 4786. determinism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីកំណត់ចិត្ត / ភាពដែលគ្រប់ការណ៍ត្រូវបានកំណត់ពីមុន
1. Determinism holds that every event, including every human choice, is causally necessitated by prior events and the laws of nature.
2. If determinism is true, it is unclear how we can hold people morally responsible for actions they could not have avoided doing.
3. The debate between determinism and free will is one of the oldest and most persistent problems in both philosophy and science.

---

### 4787. free will /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សេរីភាពចិត្ត / ការជ្រើសរើសដោយខ្លួនឯង
1. Free will, if it exists, suggests that human beings are genuinely the authors of their own choices and not merely products of causation.
2. If free will is an illusion, as some neuroscientists claim, then our entire framework of praise, blame, and punishment needs rethinking.
3. Compatibilists argue that free will and determinism can coexist, since what matters is acting from one's own reasons without external compulsion.

---

### 4788. agency /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សមត្ថភាពធ្វើសកម្មភាព / ភ្នាក់ងារ
1. Human agency implies the capacity to initiate action based on one's own intentions rather than being driven entirely by external forces.
2. If agency requires the ability to have done otherwise, then any thoroughly deterministic universe would eliminate genuine human agency.
3. The concept of moral agency is central to ethics, since only agents who can reflect on their actions can be held responsible for them.

---

### 4789. consciousness /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ស្មារតី / ការដឹងខ្លួន
1. The hard problem of consciousness asks why there is subjective experience at all, given that the brain is a physical organ.
2. If consciousness is entirely reducible to brain activity, then subjective qualities like the redness of red would be fully explainable in physical terms.
3. Some philosophers argue that consciousness cannot be explained by current science and requires an entirely new conceptual framework.

---

### 4790. mind /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ចិត្ត / ស្មារតី / ប្រាជ្ញា
1. The philosophy of mind investigates the relationship between mental states, such as beliefs and desires, and the physical brain.
2. If the mind is entirely identical to the brain, as physicalists claim, then mental events are nothing over and above neural events.
3. Dualists argue that the mind and body are distinct substances, raising the difficult question of how they causally interact with each other.

---

### 4791. body /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** រាងកាយ / ខ្លួន
1. Descartes held that the body is an extended physical substance entirely distinct from the thinking, non-extended mind.
2. If the body determines consciousness in fundamental ways, then embodiment must be taken seriously by any adequate philosophy of mind.
3. Phenomenologists like Merleau-Ponty argued that the lived body, not the abstract mind, is our primary way of engaging with the world.

---

### 4792. soul /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ព្រលឹង / ជីវិតខាងវិញ្ញាណ
1. Plato argued that the soul is immortal, pre-existing the body and returning to a higher realm after the body's death.
2. If the soul is the source of our rational and moral capacities, then the cultivation of the soul is the highest human task.
3. Aristotle conceived of the soul not as a separate substance but as the form of the body — its organizing principle and essential structure.

---

### 4793. spirit /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** វិញ្ញាណ / ស្មារតី
1. Hegel used the term "spirit" (Geist) to describe the collective rational mind that unfolds through history toward greater self-awareness.
2. If the spirit is understood as the animating principle of a culture, then its decline signals the beginning of that civilization's collapse.
3. The concept of spirit in philosophy often bridges the gap between individual consciousness and the broader structures of thought and culture.

---

### 4794. existence /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អត្ថិភាព / ការមានជីវិត
1. Heidegger argued that the fundamental question of philosophy is why there is something rather than nothing — why existence itself?
2. If existence precedes essence, as Sartre claimed, then human beings have no fixed nature and must define themselves through their choices.
3. The question of what it means for something to exist has occupied philosophers from Parmenides in antiquity to the present day.

---

### 4795. being /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការគ្រប់គ្រងជាអ្វី / ភាពជាអ្វី
1. Heidegger's "Being and Time" attempts to recover the question of the meaning of Being, which he believed Western philosophy had forgotten.
2. If being is the most fundamental concept, any attempt to define it will already presuppose the very thing we are trying to explain.
3. The distinction between Being and beings — between existence itself and the particular things that exist — is central to ontology.

---

### 4796. nothingness /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពគ្មានអ្វី / អនត្ថភាព
1. Sartre argued that nothingness is not a mere absence but an active force that human consciousness introduces into the world.
2. If we contemplate nothingness seriously, we are confronted with the vertiginous possibility that everything could simply not have been.
3. The concept of nothingness challenges logic itself, since to say "nothing exists" seems to make nothing into a kind of something.

---

### 4797. infinity /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពអនន្ត / ឥតដែនកំណត់
1. Philosophical debates about infinity raise questions about whether the universe is finite or extends without limit in space and time.
2. If infinity exists as a mathematical concept, it is worth asking whether it corresponds to anything real in the physical world.
3. Cantor's revolutionary work on infinity showed that there are different sizes of infinite sets, a discovery that shocked mathematicians.

---

### 4798. finite /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** មានដែនកំណត់ / ចប់
1. The recognition that human life is finite gives urgency to the question of how that limited time should best be spent.
2. If human knowledge is finite, there will always be truths about the universe that no intelligence, however powerful, can ever reach.
3. Heidegger argued that acknowledging our own finitude — our being-toward-death — is essential to authentic human existence.

---

### 4799. abstract /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អរូបី / មិនជាក់លាក់
1. Abstract concepts like justice and beauty cannot be pointed to directly but must be grasped through reflection and argument.
2. If abstract entities like numbers exist independently of minds, as Platonists believe, then mathematics describes a non-physical reality.
3. The tension between abstract theory and concrete experience is one that philosophers in every tradition have had to navigate carefully.

### 4800. concrete /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ជាក់លាក់ / ជាប់ទំនាក់ជាក់ស្ដែង
1. A concrete example can sometimes illuminate an abstract philosophical principle more effectively than a lengthy theoretical exposition.
2. If philosophical ideas cannot be connected to concrete human experience, they risk becoming empty and irrelevant to real life.
3. Marx criticized Hegel for being too abstract and insisted that philosophy must engage with the concrete material conditions of human existence.

### 4801. universal /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សកល / ជាទូទៅ
1. The problem of universals asks whether general concepts like "redness" or "humanness" exist independently of the particular things they describe.
2. If there are universal moral truths, they must apply equally to all rational beings regardless of their culture or historical situation.
3. Kant believed that the categorical imperative provides a universal moral law that all rational agents can recognize through reason alone.

---

### 4802. particular /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ជាក់លាក់ / ជាពិសេស
1. The tension between the universal and the particular is central to ethics, since general rules must always be applied to specific cases.
2. If every moral situation is unique, it may be impossible to derive adequate guidance from universal principles that ignore the particular.
3. Aristotle emphasized the importance of practical wisdom in perceiving the morally relevant features of each particular situation.

---

### 4803. essence /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អង្គខ្លឹម / ធម្មជាតិខ្លឹមសារ
1. Plato held that the essence of a thing is its ideal Form, which is more real than any particular instance we encounter in experience.
2. If existentialism is correct that existence precedes essence, then there is no fixed human nature that determines what we must be.
3. The search for the essence of concepts like justice or beauty has driven philosophical inquiry since the dialogues of Socrates.

---

### 4804. appearance /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** រូបរាងខាងក្រៅ / ការបង្ហាញ
1. Plato distinguished between appearance, which is the world of the senses, and reality, which is the realm of the eternal Forms.
2. If reality differs systematically from appearance, we must ask how knowledge of the real can ever be grounded in what we perceive.
3. Kant argued that we can only know appearances — the world as it is structured by our own cognitive faculties — not things as they are in themselves.

---

### 4805. reality /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការពិត / ពិតប្រាកដ
1. What we take to be reality may be shaped as much by our conceptual frameworks as by the world as it exists independently of us.
2. If virtual reality becomes indistinguishable from ordinary experience, philosophers must reconsider what it means for something to be real.
3. The debate between realism and idealism is fundamentally a debate about the nature of reality and its relationship to the knowing mind.

---

### 4806. illusion /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ចក្ខុវិភ្រម / ការភ្លើ
1. Descartes used the possibility of systematic illusion to motivate the search for a foundation of knowledge that cannot be doubted.
2. If free will is an illusion, as some scientists argue, the entire legal and moral system built on individual responsibility is called into question.
3. Eastern philosophical traditions, particularly in Buddhism and Advaita Vedanta, have long treated the ordinary self as an illusion to be transcended.

---

### 4807. perception /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការយល់ឃើញ / ការដឹង
1. Philosophical accounts of perception must explain both how we acquire reliable knowledge of the external world and how perceptual error is possible.
2. If perception is always mediated by concepts and expectations, it becomes unclear whether we ever experience the world as it really is.
3. The phenomenology of perception, as developed by Merleau-Ponty, emphasizes the role of the body in shaping how the world appears to us.

### 4808. representation /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការតំណាង / ការបង្ហាញ
1. The question of how mental representations relate to the world they are supposed to represent is central to philosophy of language and mind.
2. If our beliefs are representations of reality, we must ask what makes a representation accurate rather than systematically misleading.
3. Post-structuralist thinkers challenged the assumption that language transparently represents reality rather than actively constructing it.

---

### 4809. concept /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** គំនិត / ទស្សនៈ
1. A concept is a mental category that allows us to classify, recognize, and reason about the objects and events we encounter in the world.
2. If concepts are wholly derived from experience, as empiricists claim, then innate ideas of the rationalists could not exist at all.
3. The relationship between concepts and language has been a central concern of analytic philosophy since Frege and Russell at the turn of the twentieth century.

---

### 4810. idea /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** គំនិត / ទស្សនៈ
1. Plato's theory of Ideas, or Forms, holds that abstract ideas are more real than the particular things that imperfectly instantiate them.
2. If an idea cannot be clearly formulated, it may be that the apparent difficulty points to a genuine incoherence in our thinking.
3. The history of philosophy can be read as the history of ideas — of concepts that transformed how human beings understand themselves and the world.

---

### 4811. thought /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការគិត / គំនិត
1. Descartes argued that thought is the one thing that cannot be doubted, since even doubting is itself a form of thinking.
2. If thought is entirely determined by the structure of language, then thinking in a different language might produce a genuinely different reality.
3. The nature of thought — whether it is essentially linguistic, imagistic, or something else entirely — remains deeply contested in philosophy.

---

### 4812. belief /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ជំនឿ / ការជឿ
1. A belief is a mental state that represents the world as being a certain way and that guides action when combined with desire.
2. If a belief is true but held for the wrong reasons, many epistemologists argue it does not qualify as genuine knowledge.
3. The distinction between belief and knowledge is one of the most fundamental issues in epistemology, going back to Plato's Meno.

---

### 4813. opinion /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** មតិ / ការយល់ឃើញ
1. Plato distinguished sharply between opinion, which can be mistaken, and knowledge, which concerns eternal and unchanging truths.
2. If all moral judgments are merely opinions rather than truths, then serious ethical debate would seem to be a pointless exercise.
3. Democratic societies must navigate the tension between respecting individual opinion and preventing harmful misinformation from spreading.

---

### 4814. doubt /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការសង្ស័យ / ការប្រកែក
1. Cartesian doubt, used as a methodological tool, involves suspending all beliefs that could conceivably be false in order to find certainty.
2. If doubt is the beginning of wisdom, then the person who never questions their beliefs is unlikely to arrive at genuine knowledge.
3. The relationship between doubt and faith has been a central preoccupation of religious philosophy from Augustine to Kierkegaard.

---

### 4815. certainty /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពច្បាស់លាស់ / ការប្រាកដ
1. Descartes sought a foundation of absolute certainty upon which all other knowledge could be securely built.
2. If moral certainty is impossible, we must still make decisions and act, which requires developing tolerance for moral uncertainty.
3. The quest for certainty in philosophy has often given way to a more modest search for well-grounded and revisable beliefs.

---

### 4816. probability /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ប្រូបាប / ចំណែកនៃការប្រហែលជា
1. Bayesian epistemology treats belief as a matter of degree, measured by probability, rather than as an all-or-nothing commitment.
2. If we accept that most of our beliefs have only a certain probability of being true, we should hold them with corresponding tentativeness.
3. The philosophical interpretation of probability — whether it refers to frequencies, propensities, or degrees of belief — remains deeply contested.

---

### 4817. possibility /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** លទ្ធភាព / ភាពអាចធ្វើ
1. Modal logic deals with possibility and necessity, exploring what is conceivable, what could be the case, and what must be the case.
2. If we take seriously the full range of possibilities for human life, we open ourselves to forms of flourishing we have not yet imagined.
3. The difference between logical possibility and physical possibility is crucial in many philosophical thought experiments.

---

### 4818. necessity /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពចាំបាច់ / ការចាំបាច់
1. Philosophers distinguish between logical necessity, where denial leads to contradiction, and natural necessity, which is imposed by the laws of nature.
2. If a moral principle holds with necessity, it applies to all rational beings in all possible circumstances without exception.
3. Kant argued that the necessity of mathematical and logical truths cannot be derived from experience but must be grounded in reason itself.

---

### 4819. contingency /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពអាស្រ័យ / ការប្រែប្រួលតាមកាលៈទេសៈ
1. The recognition of historical contingency suggests that the social arrangements we live under could have been otherwise and can be changed.
2. If everything that exists does so only contingently, there is no necessary being whose non-existence would be a logical contradiction.
3. Existentialists see human contingency — the fact that we did not choose to exist — as the starting point of authentic self-creation.

---

### 4820. causality /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ហេតុ-ផល / ការបង្ករ
1. Hume famously argued that causality is not observed in the world but is a habit of mind that we project onto a sequence of events.
2. If causality is a category of the mind rather than a feature of the world, then our causal explanations may not capture independent reality.
3. The philosophical analysis of causality has important implications for science, ethics, and the law, wherever responsibility must be assigned.

---

### 4821. teleology /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីគោលដៅ / ការបកស្រាយដោយគោលបំណង
1. Teleology explains phenomena in terms of purposes or goals, asking what something is for rather than merely what caused it.
2. If teleological explanations are legitimate in biology, organisms can be understood as directed toward the goal of survival and reproduction.
3. Aristotle's entire philosophy is shaped by teleological thinking, from his biology to his ethics and his understanding of politics.

---

### 4822. ontology /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីអត្ថិភាព / ការសិក្សាអំពីអ្វីដែលមានស្ថិតស្ថេរ
1. Ontology, the branch of philosophy concerned with what exists, asks whether abstract entities, possible worlds, or numbers are real.
2. If ontology is prior to epistemology, we must first ask what there is before we can ask how we know it.
3. The ontological argument for God's existence, first formulated by Anselm, attempts to derive existence from the concept of a greatest being.

---

### 4823. epistemology /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីចំណេះដឹង / ការសិក្សាអំពីចំណេះ
1. Epistemology investigates the nature, sources, and limits of human knowledge, asking what we can know and how we can know it.
2. If epistemology reveals that all our beliefs are uncertain, the appropriate response may be not skepticism but calibrated confidence.
3. The Gettier problem showed that traditional definitions of knowledge as justified true belief face serious and perhaps fatal counterexamples.

---

### 4824. metaphysics /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** តាមធម្មជាតិ / ទស្សនវិជ្ជាខ្ពស់
1. Metaphysics investigates the most fundamental questions about reality: what exists, what time is, and what it means to be a person.
2. If metaphysical questions cannot be answered by empirical science, we must ask whether they are genuine questions or merely pseudo-problems.
3. Kant attempted to set the limits of metaphysics by showing which questions reason can legitimately address and which it cannot.

---

### 4825. paradox /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការផ្ទុយ / គំនិតប្រឆាំង
1. The liar's paradox — "this statement is false" — shows that seemingly simple language can generate profound logical contradictions.
2. If a paradox arises from apparently valid reasoning and true premises, it suggests that one of those premises must be rejected.
3. Zeno's paradoxes of motion challenged philosophers and mathematicians for centuries before being resolved by the concept of infinite series.

---

### 4826. contradiction /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការផ្ទុយ / ភាពផ្ទុយគ្នា
1. A logical contradiction, where both a proposition and its negation are asserted simultaneously, cannot be true in classical logic.
2. If a theory leads to a contradiction, it must be either revised or abandoned, since contradictions destroy the possibility of coherent reasoning.
3. Hegel embraced contradiction as the engine of dialectical progress, arguing that every thesis generates its antithesis before a synthesis is reached.

---

### 4827. dilemma /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការជ្រើសរើសពិបាក / ស្ថានការណ៍ពិបាក
1. A genuine moral dilemma arises when two equally compelling moral obligations conflict and cannot both be satisfied at once.
2. If every ethical dilemma has a correct answer, the task of moral philosophy is to find the principles that reveal what that answer is.
3. The trolley problem, a famous philosophical dilemma, forces us to confront deep tensions between utilitarian and deontological reasoning.

---

### 4828. moral dilemma /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការជ្រើសរើសសីលធម៌ / ការតស៊ូខាងសីលធម៌
1. A moral dilemma, unlike an ordinary problem, cannot be resolved without violating some genuine moral obligation or value.
2. If moral dilemmas are genuine, then even a person of perfect virtue may sometimes act wrongly through no fault of their own.
3. Bernard Williams argued that moral dilemmas reveal that ethics cannot always provide clean, cost-free solutions to human conflicts.

---

### 4829. ethical question /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សំណួរសីលធម៌
1. Ethical questions about emerging technologies challenge philosophers to extend traditional moral frameworks into genuinely new domains.
2. If an ethical question has no definitive answer, it does not follow that all proposed answers are equally well-reasoned or justified.
3. The most pressing ethical questions of our time — about climate justice, AI, and global inequality — demand urgent philosophical attention.

---

### 4830. thought experiment /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការពិសោធន៍ក្នុងគំនិត
1. A thought experiment allows philosophers to test moral intuitions by imagining hypothetical scenarios that isolate relevant variables.
2. If a thought experiment produces a strong intuition, that intuition can serve as evidence against a theory that predicts the opposite.
3. Rawls's "original position" is a famous thought experiment designed to yield principles of justice that are fair to all parties.

---

### 4831. hypothetical /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ជាការសន្មត / ជំនួសដោយការសន្មត
1. A hypothetical case, by abstracting away from real-world complications, can reveal the logical structure of an ethical argument more clearly.
2. If we reason only from hypothetical examples, we risk losing sight of the complexity and particularity of actual moral situations.
3. Kant distinguished between hypothetical imperatives, which are conditional on our desires, and categorical imperatives, which are unconditional.

### 4832. assumption /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការសន្មត / ការទទួលយកដោយមិនជជែក
1. Every argument rests on certain assumptions that may themselves require justification if the argument is to be truly persuasive.
2. If we challenge the hidden assumptions of a theory, we often discover that its apparent strength depends on premises it has not defended.
3. The assumption that rational self-interest is the primary driver of human behavior underlies much of classical economic and social theory.

---

### 4833. premise /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** មូលហេតុ / ការទទួលស្គាល់ជាមូលដ្ឋាន
1. An argument is only as strong as its premises, so identifying and scrutinizing them is the first step in critical philosophical analysis.
2. If a premise is false, then even a perfectly valid argument built upon it may lead to a false or harmful conclusion.
3. In formal logic, premises are the propositions from which a conclusion is derived through the application of rules of inference.

---

### 4834. inference /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការសន្និដ្ឋាន / ការសង្ខេបហេតុផល
1. An inference is a step of reasoning in which we move from what we already know or believe to a new claim that follows from it.
2. If our inferences are consistently unreliable, the entire enterprise of reasoning from evidence to conclusions is undermined.
3. The distinction between deductive inference, which is truth-preserving, and inductive inference, which is merely probability-raising, is fundamental in logic.

---

### 4835. argument /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការជជែក / ហេតុផល
1. A philosophical argument consists of a set of premises offered in support of a conclusion, intended to provide reasons to believe it.
2. If an argument is valid but its premises are contested, the task is to evaluate those premises rather than the logical structure alone.
3. The ability to construct and critically evaluate arguments is the core skill that philosophy develops in those who study it seriously.

---

### 4836. fallacy /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** កំហុសសតវ័យ / ការអះអាងខុស
1. An ad hominem fallacy attacks the person making an argument rather than engaging with the substance of the argument itself.
2. If we can identify the fallacy in an argument, we can reject its conclusion without needing to show that the conclusion is false.
3. Philosophers have catalogued dozens of informal fallacies, each representing a characteristic way in which reasoning can go wrong.

### 4837. sophistry /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការប្រើហេតុផលបោកប្រាស់ / ចំណេះដឹងចង្អែ
1. Sophistry involves the use of clever but ultimately deceptive arguments designed to win debates rather than to arrive at truth.
2. If sophistry is indistinguishable from genuine philosophy to the untrained mind, education in critical thinking becomes essential.
3. Plato devoted much of his philosophical work to distinguishing genuine philosophical inquiry from the sophistry of the rhetoricians of his day.

### 4838. rhetoric /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ឃោសនា / សម្ដីជំនាញ
1. Rhetoric, when divorced from truth, becomes a tool of manipulation; when allied with it, it becomes a means of genuine persuasion.
2. If rhetoric is understood as the art of effective communication, then philosophers who write clearly are practicing it at its best.
3. Aristotle's treatise on rhetoric distinguished between logos, pathos, and ethos as the three modes of persuasion available to a speaker.

### 4839. dialectic /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការជជែកដើម្បីស្វែងរកការពិត / វិធីដោះស្រាយតាមការជជែក
1. The Socratic dialectic involves a method of questioning that exposes contradictions in an interlocutor's beliefs, leading toward greater clarity.
2. If dialectic is the proper method of philosophy, then truth is best reached through structured dialogue rather than solitary reflection.
3. Hegel developed dialectic into a comprehensive account of how ideas and history progress through conflict toward higher synthesis.

---

### 4840. thesis /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីបញ្ជាក់ / គំនិតចម្បង
1. A philosophical thesis is a position that its author proposes to defend through argument against possible objections.
2. If the thesis of a paper cannot be stated clearly in one or two sentences, the argument it supports is likely to lack focus.
3. In Hegelian dialectic, the thesis is the initial position that provokes its opposite, the antithesis, before both are resolved in a synthesis.

---

### 4841. antithesis /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការផ្ទុយ / ការប្រឆាំង
1. The antithesis, in Hegel's dialectic, is not merely the negation of the thesis but a position with its own internal coherence and truth.
2. If the antithesis merely contradicts without developing new insights, no genuine dialectical progress toward a higher synthesis can occur.
3. The tension between individualism and collectivism can be seen as a dialectical pair — thesis and antithesis — seeking resolution.

---

### 4842. synthesis /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការរួមបញ្ចូល / ការផ្សំ
1. A synthesis in philosophy integrates the insights of opposing positions while preserving what is most valuable from each.
2. If a genuine synthesis is achieved, it should not merely split the difference but raise the debate to a qualitatively higher level.
3. Kant's critical philosophy is often described as a synthesis of rationalism and empiricism, drawing on both traditions while transforming them.

### 4843. critique /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការរិះគន់ / ការវិភាគ
1. Kant's critiques were not attacks on reason but systematic investigations of its powers, limits, and legitimate domains of application.
2. If a critique of a theory is to be effective, it must engage with the strongest version of that theory rather than a weak caricature of it.
3. Feminist critiques of mainstream philosophy have revealed how apparently universal concepts often reflect the particular perspectives of privileged groups.

### 4844. deconstruction /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការបំបែករចនាសម្ព័ន្ធ / ការវែកញែក
1. Deconstruction, as practiced by Derrida, reveals how texts depend on binary oppositions that are internally unstable and reversible.
2. If deconstruction shows that no text has a fixed meaning, it raises the question of how communication and interpretation are possible at all.
3. Critics of deconstruction argue that it leads to a nihilistic relativism that makes it impossible to distinguish better from worse interpretations.

---

### 4845. hermeneutics /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីបកស្រាយ / ការអធិប្បាយអត្ថន័យ
1. Hermeneutics, the study of interpretation, asks how we can understand texts, practices, and expressions from other cultures or historical periods.
2. If all understanding involves interpretation, then the hermeneutic circle — where parts are understood through the whole and vice versa — is unavoidable.
3. Gadamer argued that understanding is never objective but always shaped by a "horizon" of assumptions that the interpreter brings to the text.

### 4846. phenomenology /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីបាតុភូត / ការសិក្សាបាតុភូតនៃចិត្ត
1. Phenomenology, founded by Husserl, investigates the structures of experience as they appear to consciousness, prior to theoretical reflection.
2. If phenomenology suspends assumptions about what exists, it can describe the world as it is directly lived without metaphysical presuppositions.
3. Heidegger radicalized phenomenology by using it not to study consciousness but to investigate the structure of human existence in the world.

---

### 4847. structuralism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីរចនាសម្ព័ន្ធ
1. Structuralism holds that meaning is not inherent in individual elements but arises from the relations between them within a system.
2. If structuralism is applied to language, then words do not have meaning in isolation but only in relation to other words within the system.
3. Lévi-Strauss applied structural analysis to mythology, arguing that all myths share a common underlying logical structure across cultures.

---

### 4848. post-structuralism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីក្រោយរចនាសម្ព័ន្ធ
1. Post-structuralism challenges the assumption that language has stable structures, arguing instead that meaning is always deferred and unstable.
2. If post-structuralism is correct, then the search for foundational principles in philosophy or social science is ultimately bound to fail.
3. Foucault, Derrida, and Deleuze are among the most influential post-structuralist thinkers, each transforming the tradition in different ways.

---

### 4849. modernism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទំនើបនិយម / ការទទួលយកទ្រឹស្ដីទំនើប
1. Modernism in philosophy was characterized by a faith in reason, progress, and the ability of science to provide universal knowledge.
2. If modernism's confidence in reason was shattered by the catastrophes of the twentieth century, postmodernism was the intellectual response.
3. The modernist project of founding knowledge on secure, indubitable principles was challenged by critics from many different philosophical traditions.

---

### 4850. postmodernism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ក្រោយទំនើបនិយម
1. Postmodernism rejects grand narratives and universal truths, emphasizing instead plurality, difference, and the local nature of knowledge.
2. If postmodernism is correct that all knowledge claims reflect power relations, then the question of whose knowledge counts becomes paramount.
3. Critics argue that postmodernism, by denying objective truth, undermines the critical standards needed to distinguish fact from propaganda.

---

### 4851. humanism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** មនុស្សនិយម / ការផ្ដោតលើ​ការអភិវឌ្ឍមនុស្ស
1. Humanism places human beings, their dignity, and their capacity for reason at the center of ethical and political thought.
2. If humanism is correct that human reason can solve human problems without divine assistance, then secular education becomes essential.
3. Renaissance humanism recovered the texts of classical antiquity and used them to articulate a vision of human flourishing centered on earthly life.

---

### 4852. transhumanism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីលើសមនុស្ស / ការអភិវឌ្ឍលើសពីមនុស្ស
1. Transhumanism holds that technology should be used to enhance human capacities beyond their current biological limits.
2. If transhumanism succeeds in dramatically extending human lifespan, it will raise profound questions about identity, fairness, and the meaning of life.
3. Critics of transhumanism argue that it ignores the value of human vulnerability and the meaning that comes from our natural limitations.

---

### 4853. utopia /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ស្ថានសួគ៌ / ទីកន្លែងល្អឥតខ្ចោះ
1. Thomas More coined the word "utopia" to describe an ideal society, while deliberately leaving open the question of whether it could ever exist.
2. If utopian thinking is dismissed as mere fantasy, we lose the ability to imagine genuinely better alternatives to current arrangements.
3. The history of attempts to build utopian communities offers sobering lessons about the gap between ideal visions and practical realities.

---

### 4854. dystopia /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ស្ថានត្រពាំង / ទីកន្លែងអាក្រក់
1. Dystopian literature, from Orwell to Atwood, uses extreme hypothetical futures to illuminate dangers already present in contemporary society.
2. If we take dystopian warnings seriously, we have an obligation to resist the social and political tendencies they identify before it is too late.
3. A dystopia is not simply a society that is bad but one that is presented as ideal by those in power, which is what makes it so dangerous.

---

### 4855. progress /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការរីកចម្រើន / វិវឌ្ឍន៍
1. The idea of historical progress — that humanity moves toward greater knowledge, freedom, and well-being — was central to Enlightenment philosophy.
2. If progress is not inevitable, then every generation bears responsibility for actively working to prevent regression and decline.
3. Critics of the idea of progress argue that it is linear, Eurocentric, and blind to the many forms of loss that accompany material advancement.

---

### 4856. decline /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការធ្លាក់ចុះ / ការអន់ចុះ
1. Spengler argued in "The Decline of the West" that civilizations, like organisms, pass through stages of growth and inevitable decay.
2. If moral decline is not inevitable but is a response to specific conditions, then identifying those conditions enables us to address them.
3. The perception of cultural or moral decline is often more revealing of the values of the perceiver than of any objective change in society.

---

### 4857. meaning /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អត្ថន័យ / ន័យ
1. The search for meaning in life is not primarily a cognitive task but an existential one, involving commitment and engagement.
2. If meaning is not discovered but created, then the question of how to live becomes radically open and personal for each individual.
3. Viktor Frankl argued that even in the most terrible circumstances, human beings retain the freedom to choose the attitude they take toward suffering.

---

### 4858. purpose /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** គោលបំណង / ចេតនា
1. A life lived with clear purpose is generally more resilient in the face of hardship than one guided merely by comfort or habit.
2. If human beings have no given purpose, as existentialists argue, then the project of creating purpose becomes our most fundamental task.
3. Teleological ethics asks what purpose human beings are meant to fulfill and judges actions by how well they serve that purpose.

---

### 4859. value /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** តម្លៃ / គុណតម្លៃ
1. Philosophers distinguish between intrinsic value, which things have in themselves, and instrumental value, which depends on usefulness.
2. If values are merely expressions of subjective preference, then disagreements about them cannot be resolved by argument or evidence.
3. The question of whether all values are commensurable — whether they can be placed on a single scale — is central to debates in ethics and economics.

---

### 4860. worth /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** តម្លៃ / ទ្រព្យ
1. Kant argued that human beings have dignity — an unconditional worth that cannot be traded off against any other value or consideration.
2. If the worth of a person were determined by their productivity or social contribution, the implications for how we treat the vulnerable would be deeply troubling.
3. The distinction between price and worth, which Kant drew sharply, suggests that some things cannot be assigned a monetary value.

---

### 4861. dignity /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សេចក្ដីថ្លៃថ្នូរ / សច្ចភាព
1. Human dignity, as enshrined in international human rights law, is the foundation from which all other rights are derived.
2. If dignity is inherent in all persons simply by virtue of their humanity, it cannot be forfeited even through terrible wrongdoing.
3. The concept of dignity is central to bioethics, where decisions about life, death, and medical treatment must respect the person's fundamental worth.

---

### 4862. autonomy /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ឯករាជ្យភាព / ការគ្រប់គ្រងខ្លួន
1. Autonomy, the capacity to govern one's own life according to self-chosen principles, is central to Kantian ethics and liberal political theory.
2. If we override someone's autonomous choice for their own benefit, we must be able to justify that paternalistic interference very carefully.
3. Respect for patient autonomy in medical ethics requires that informed consent be freely given without deception or undue pressure.

---

### 4863. liberty /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សេរីភាព / ភាពឯករាជ្យ
1. Isaiah Berlin distinguished between negative liberty, freedom from interference, and positive liberty, freedom to realize one's own potential.
2. If liberty is understood only as the absence of external constraint, then structural inequalities that limit real choices are overlooked.
3. The tension between individual liberty and collective welfare is a perennial challenge for democratic governance and political philosophy.

---

### 4864. equality /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សមភាព / ការស្មើភាព
1. Equality before the law is a foundational principle of justice, requiring that rules be applied consistently regardless of status or privilege.
2. If formal equality ignores substantive differences in power and opportunity, it may entrench rather than reduce existing inequalities.
3. Rawls argued that just inequalities are only those that benefit the least advantaged members of society, not merely the majority.

---

### 4865. solidarity /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពស្អិតរមួត / ការប្រមូលផ្ដុំគ្នា
1. Solidarity, the commitment to support others in their struggles, is both a moral value and a practical condition for collective action.
2. If solidarity is to be more than sentiment, it must translate into concrete actions that redistribute resources and risk more fairly.
3. The challenge of solidarity across difference requires that we recognize the perspectives of others without collapsing them into our own.

---

### 4866. community /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សហគមន៍ / ការរួបរួម
1. Communitarians argue that individual identity is constituted by community membership and cannot be abstracted from it as liberalism assumes.
2. If community life is essential to human flourishing, then social policies that weaken communal bonds have hidden costs that economists miss.
3. The tension between community loyalty and universal moral principles is one of the most enduring conflicts in ethical and political philosophy.

---

### 4867. individual /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** បុគ្គល / ជនម្នាក់ៗ
1. Liberal political philosophy places the individual — their rights, autonomy, and well-being — at the center of its moral and political vision.
2. If individuals are always embedded in social relationships, the image of the isolated self-sufficient individual may be a philosophical fiction.
3. The relationship between the individual and the state has been a central preoccupation of Western political philosophy from Plato to the present.

---

### 4868. society /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សង្គម / ជំនួបសង្គម
1. A just society, according to Rawls, is one whose basic structure is arranged so that it is fair to all citizens, including the least advantaged.
2. If society is more than the sum of its individual members, then sociological and philosophical analysis must attend to structural as well as individual factors.
3. The question of what kind of society promotes human flourishing is one that philosophy, politics, and social science must address together.

---

### 4869. power /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អំណាច / ថាមពល
1. Foucault analyzed power not as something possessed by individuals but as a network of relations that operates throughout society.
2. If power inevitably corrupts, then constitutional design must include mechanisms that prevent its concentration in any single hand.
3. The relationship between knowledge and power, as Foucault argued, suggests that what counts as truth is shaped by existing power structures.

---

### 4870. authority /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អំណាច / អាជ្ញាធរ
1. The legitimacy of authority depends not merely on the fact that it exists but on whether it serves the purposes that justify its existence.
2. If authority is to be respected rather than merely obeyed, it must be grounded in something more than the capacity to enforce compliance.
3. Weber distinguished three types of authority — traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal — each resting on a different basis of legitimacy.

### 4871. legitimacy /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពស្របច្បាប់ / ភាពត្រឹមត្រូវ
1. The legitimacy of a government depends on whether those it governs have good reasons to accept and comply with its authority.
2. If a political system lacks legitimacy, citizens may comply with its rules out of fear while withholding genuine moral endorsement.
3. Democratic theorists argue that legitimacy requires not only fair procedures but also substantive respect for the rights of all citizens.

---

### 4872. consent /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការយល់ព្រម / ការអនុញ្ញាត
1. Political consent, whether actual or hypothetical, is central to many accounts of legitimate political authority and the duty to obey the law.
2. If consent must be informed and voluntary to be valid, many historical and contemporary political arrangements fall short of this standard.
3. The concept of hypothetical consent — what we would agree to under ideal conditions — is used by theorists like Rawls to derive principles of justice.

### 4873. social contract /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** កិច្ចព្រមព្រៀងសង្គម / ការតក្លោកសង្គម
1. Social contract theory, developed by Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau, grounds political authority in an agreement, real or hypothetical, among citizens.
2. If the social contract requires the consent of those it binds, then those who were never consulted may have weaker obligations to obey.
3. The feminist critique of social contract theory points out that women were historically excluded from the original contracting parties.

### 4874. natural law /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ច្បាប់ធម្មជាតិ / ច្បាប់本然
1. Natural law theory holds that there are moral norms discoverable by reason that provide a standard against which positive law can be judged.
2. If natural law exists, then unjust laws that violate it may lack moral authority even if they are legally valid within a given system.
3. The natural law tradition, developed by Aquinas and later by Locke, played a crucial role in grounding the discourse of universal human rights.

---

### 4875. positive law /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ច្បាប់ស្ថាបនា / ច្បាប់ដែលបង្កើតឡើង
1. Positive law consists of the rules actually enacted by legitimate authorities, as distinct from moral norms derived from reason or nature.
2. If positive law is binding simply because it is enacted, then the content of the law — whether just or unjust — is legally irrelevant.
3. Legal positivists like Hart argued that the question of what the law is must be separated from the question of what the law ought to be.

---

### 4876. rights /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សិទ្ធិ / ជ័យជំនះ
1. Rights are moral or legal entitlements that constrain how individuals may be treated by others or by the state, regardless of aggregate benefit.
2. If rights are absolute constraints, then no consequentialist calculation can justify overriding them even for very large gains in overall welfare.
3. The debate between human rights universalists and cultural relativists concerns whether rights are genuinely universal or culturally specific.

---

### 4877. duties /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ករណីកិច្ច / ភារកិច្ច
1. Kant held that our duties arise from the categorical imperative and bind us absolutely, regardless of our desires or the consequences.
2. If duties can conflict — as when honesty and kindness point in opposite directions — we need principles for resolving such moral tensions.
3. The distinction between perfect duties, which admit no exceptions, and imperfect duties, which allow discretion in their fulfillment, is central to Kantian ethics.

---

### 4878. obligations /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** កាតព្វកិច្ច / ករណីកិច្ចទទួល
1. Moral obligations can arise from promises, relationships, or roles, each generating different kinds of claims on our behavior and attention.
2. If our obligations to future generations are real, then current generations have strong moral reasons to preserve the environment for those who will follow.
3. The question of whether we have obligations to distant strangers whom we will never meet is central to the philosophy of global justice.

---

### 4879. responsibility /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការទទួលខុសត្រូវ / ករណីកិច្ច
1. Moral responsibility presupposes that agents have sufficient freedom and knowledge to have done otherwise than they in fact did.
2. If determinism eliminates the ability to have done otherwise, then either moral responsibility is impossible or it must be redefined compatibilistically.
3. Collective responsibility, the idea that groups can be morally responsible for their members' actions, raises difficult questions about agency and accountability.

---

### 4880. accountability /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពទទួលខុសត្រូវ / ការទទួលខុស
1. Accountability requires not only that wrongdoers face consequences but that the processes by which judgments are made are fair and transparent.
2. If those in power are not held accountable, corruption tends to become systemic and eventually corrodes the institutions it inhabits.
3. Democratic accountability mechanisms, such as elections and judicial review, are designed to ensure that authority is exercised responsibly.

### 4881. sustainability /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពស្ថិតស្ថេរ / ការប្រើប្រាស់ដោយថ្នាំ
1. Sustainability requires that current generations meet their needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own.
2. If sustainability is taken seriously as a moral value, then economic growth that depletes irreplaceable natural resources cannot be justified.
3. The philosophy of sustainability challenges the temporal horizon of much political and economic thinking, which rarely extends beyond the short term.

### 4882. intergenerational /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ឆ្លងកាត់មនុស្សជំនាន់ / ពីជំនាន់មួយទៅជំនាន់មួយ
1. Intergenerational justice asks what obligations the current generation has to those who will live after the decisions being made today.
2. If intergenerational fairness requires us to leave resources intact, then many current environmental practices are profoundly unjust.
3. The challenge of intergenerational ethics lies in the fact that future people cannot participate in the decisions that will most affect their lives.

### 4883. legacy /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** មរតក / ការបន្ស្លែ
1. The legacy we leave for future generations is shaped by the choices we make today, including about the environment, institutions, and culture.
2. If we are concerned about the legacy of our civilization, we must think seriously about what we are preserving and what we are destroying.
3. Philosophers have asked whether we have obligations to build a positive legacy for posterity, even when doing so requires present sacrifice.

### 4884. future /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** អនាគត / ទៅថ្ងៃអនាគត
1. Our ethical obligations extend to the future, including to people who do not yet exist but whose lives will be profoundly shaped by our choices.
2. If the future is genuinely open and undetermined, then human agency has real power to shape it, for better or worse.
3. The philosophy of the future must grapple with deep uncertainty while still providing action-guiding principles for responsible decision-making.

### 4885. hope /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សង្ឃឹម / ការប្រាថ្នា
1. Hope, as a philosophical concept, involves not just a wish for a better future but a rational expectation that better outcomes are possible.
2. If hope is unreasonable given the evidence, it may encourage passivity by substituting wishful thinking for the hard work of change.
3. Ernst Bloch's "The Principle of Hope" argues that hope is not an illusion but the forward-looking dimension of all genuine human striving.

### 4886. despair /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ស្ទើរ / ការទុក្ខ

2. If despair arises from the belief that nothing can change, philosophical work that reveals possibilities can itself be a therapeutic intervention.
3. Camus argued that acknowledging despair honestly is the first step toward a life lived with greater authenticity and intensity.

### 4887. faith /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ជំនឿ / សទ្ធា
1. Kierkegaard argued that faith requires a "leap" beyond reason, committing to a belief that cannot be rationally demonstrated or justified.
2. If faith and reason are complementary rather than opposed, then religious belief need not conflict with intellectual rigor and scientific inquiry.
3. The epistemological status of faith — whether it can constitute genuine knowledge or is always merely belief — remains deeply contested in philosophy of religion.

### 4888. courage /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពក្លាហាន / ចិត្តក្លាហាន
1. Aristotle defined courage as the mean between cowardice and recklessness, requiring both the right response to fear and the right motivation.
2. If intellectual courage is required to follow an argument wherever it leads, philosophy demands a kind of bravery as well as logical rigor.

### 4889. fear /noun/

2. If fear of death motivates much of human cultural activity, as Becker argued, then confronting mortality honestly can be liberating.
3. The Stoics believed that fear arises from false judgments about what is genuinely harmful and can be overcome through rational self-examination.

### 4890. love /noun/

3. The philosophical analysis of love asks what makes it rational, whether it requires reciprocity, and how it relates to justice and respect.

### 4891. hate /noun/

1. Philosophers have asked whether hate can ever be rational and whether, as some argue, it is the appropriate response to genuine evil.
2. If hate dehumanizes both its object and the person who harbors it, then cultivating hate is self-destructive even when directed at wrongdoers.
3. The concept of hate in political philosophy raises difficult questions about the relationship between private emotion and public discourse.

### 4892. life /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ជីវិត / ការរស់នៅ

2. If the value of life cannot be measured in purely hedonic terms, then maximizing pleasure cannot be the whole story of what makes life meaningful.
3. Philosophical accounts of life's meaning must address both the subjective dimension — what makes life feel meaningful — and the objective dimension.

### 4893. death /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការស្លាប់ / ការបាត់បង់ជីវិត

2. If the awareness of death shapes how we live, as Heidegger argued, then confronting our mortality honestly can transform the quality of our existence.
3. Philosophical questions about death include whether it is always a harm, whether there is life after death, and how we should prepare for it.

### 4894. immortality /noun/

### 4895. transcendence /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការហួសផុត / ភាពហួសថ្នាក់
1. Transcendence refers to that which lies beyond ordinary experience and which, if real, would give life a dimension beyond the merely empirical.
2. If transcendence is understood secularly, it may refer to the capacity to go beyond one's current self through learning, love, or creative work.
3. Religious philosophers have debated whether genuine transcendence is accessible through mystical experience or only through faith and revelation.

### 4896. rational /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** មានហេតុផល / ស្មោះនិងហេតុផល
1. A rational agent is one who acts on the basis of reasons and revises beliefs in response to evidence rather than mere habit or emotion.
2. If human beings are not fully rational, as behavioral economics suggests, then models assuming perfect rationality may be systematically misleading.
3. The Enlightenment ideal of the rational subject — self-transparent, autonomous, and guided by reason — has been challenged from many directions.

### 4897. irrational /adjective/

**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ចិត្ដអាណិត / ការរួបរួមចិត្ត
1. Schopenhauer placed compassion at the foundation of his ethics, arguing that the recognition of our shared suffering dissolves the boundaries of the self.
2. If compassion is to be effective rather than merely well-intentioned, it must be informed by accurate knowledge of what others actually need.
3. Buddhist ethics identifies compassion (karuna) as one of the four "divine abodes," a quality of mind to be cultivated through spiritual practice.

---

### 4947. suffering /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការទុក្ខ / ការឈឺចាប់
1. The existence of suffering in the world raises the philosophical problem of evil and challenges the coherence of believing in an all-good God.
2. If the reduction of suffering is a primary moral obligation, then global inequality, which is a major cause of preventable suffering, demands urgent attention.
3. Buddhist philosophy identifies craving as the root cause of suffering and liberation from suffering as the ultimate goal of the spiritual path.

---

### 4948. happiness /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សេចក្ដីសុខ / ភាពសំណាង
1. Aristotle distinguished between hedonic happiness — the feeling of pleasure — and eudaimonia — the deeper flourishing of a life lived well.
2. If happiness is the ultimate goal of human life, we must ask whether it is a feeling to be pursued or a quality of a life to be achieved.
3. The paradox of happiness suggests that those who pursue it most directly are often least likely to achieve it, since it comes as a by-product of engagement.

---

### 4949. flourishing /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការរីកចម្រើន / ភាពជោគជ័យ
1. Aristotle's concept of eudaimonia, often translated as flourishing, refers to the fulfillment of human capacities through a life of virtuous activity.
2. If human flourishing requires more than pleasure, then any hedonic theory of the good life is at best incomplete and at worst misleading.
3. The philosophy of human flourishing asks which capacities are central to a distinctively human life and what conditions must be met to realize them.

---

### 4950. well-being /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ភាពល្អប្រសើរ / សុខដុម
1. Theories of well-being differ on whether it consists in pleasure, desire satisfaction, or the possession of objective goods like knowledge and friendship.
2. If well-being cannot be measured by a single metric, then attempts to maximize it through policy interventions face deep conceptual difficulties.
3. The question of whether animals have well-being, and what it consists in for them, is increasingly important in ethics and in debates about animal rights.

---

### 4951. virtue ethics /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** គតិសីលធម៌គុណធម៌ / ទ្រឹស្ដីសីលធម៌គុណធម៌
1. Virtue ethics focuses on the character of the moral agent rather than on rules or consequences, asking what kind of person one should be.
2. If virtue ethics is correct, then moral education consists not primarily in learning rules but in developing the habits and dispositions of a good person.
3. Alasdair MacIntyre argued for the revival of virtue ethics as an alternative to the dominant frameworks of utilitarian and Kantian moral philosophy.

---

### 4952. practical wisdom /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ប្រាជ្ញាជាក់ស្ដែង / ប្រាជ្ញារស់នៅ
1. Practical wisdom (phronesis), for Aristotle, is the master virtue that enables a person to perceive the morally relevant features of each situation.
2. If practical wisdom cannot be reduced to the application of general rules, then moral expertise is fundamentally different from technical expertise.
3. The practically wise person knows not only what the right general principle is but how to apply it fittingly to the complexities of actual life.

---

### 4953. moral psychology /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ចិត្តវិទ្យាសីលធម៌
1. Moral psychology investigates the mental processes — emotions, intuitions, reasoning — that underlie moral judgment and behavior.
2. If moral intuitions are unreliable guides shaped by evolutionary pressures rather than truth, then moral reasoning must be given priority over them.
3. Research in moral psychology has challenged the image of the rational deliberator that dominates much traditional moral philosophy.

---

### 4954. moral realism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការពិតនិយមខាងសីលធម៌
1. Moral realism holds that moral claims express genuine truths about the world that are independent of what anyone believes or desires.
2. If moral realism is correct, then moral disagreement can in principle be resolved by inquiry in the same way that factual disagreement can.
3. The challenge for moral realists is to explain what kind of entities moral facts are and how we come to know them through ordinary experience.

---

### 4955. moral anti-realism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការប្រកាន់ប្រឆាំងនឹងការពិតខាងសីលធម៌
1. Moral anti-realism denies that moral statements are objectively true or false, treating them instead as expressions of attitude or social conventions.
2. If moral anti-realism is correct, then even the most careful moral argument cannot establish that its conclusion is objectively true for everyone.
3. Expressivism, a form of moral anti-realism, holds that moral statements express emotional attitudes rather than asserting facts about the world.

---

### 4956. normativity /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ធម្មតានៃបទដ្ឋាន / ភាពបទដ្ឋាន
1. Normativity concerns the "oughtness" of moral and practical claims — the sense in which they demand or guide action rather than merely describing it.
2. If naturalism cannot account for normativity, then moral philosophy must acknowledge a dimension of reality that goes beyond the purely descriptive.
3. The source of normativity — whether it lies in reason, sentiment, social practice, or nature — is one of the deepest questions in metaethics.

---

### 4957. metaethics /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីរួមនៃសីលធម៌ / គ្រឹះសីលធម៌
1. Metaethics investigates the foundations of ethics, asking about the nature of moral facts, the meaning of moral language, and the basis of moral knowledge.
2. If metaethics concludes that moral realism is untenable, normative ethics must proceed without the assumption that its claims are objectively true.
3. The relationship between metaethics and normative ethics is contested — some hold that metaethical commitments directly constrain ethical conclusions.

---

### 4958. applied ethics /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** សីលធម៌ជាក់ស្ដែង / ការអនុវត្ដន៍សីលធម៌
1. Applied ethics examines specific moral issues — abortion, capital punishment, environmental policy — using the tools of philosophical analysis.
2. If applied ethics is to be practically useful, it must bridge the gap between abstract principles and the messy details of real moral situations.
3. Bioethics, environmental ethics, and business ethics are among the most active subfields of applied philosophy in contemporary scholarship.

---

### 4959. political philosophy /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទស្សនវិជ្ជានយោបាយ
1. Political philosophy investigates the justification of political authority, the nature of justice, and the proper limits of the state's power over individuals.
2. If political philosophy is to guide practice, its principles must be capable of justification to citizens with diverse values and competing interests.
3. The revival of political philosophy after Rawls's "A Theory of Justice" produced a richly diverse field debating justice, rights, and the common good.

---

### 4960. social philosophy /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទស្សនវិជ្ជាសង្គម
1. Social philosophy investigates the structure of social institutions, the nature of social norms, and the philosophical foundations of social criticism.
2. If social philosophy is to be genuinely critical, it must be willing to challenge the assumptions embedded in the dominant social arrangements.
3. The Frankfurt School developed a tradition of critical social philosophy that combined insights from Marx, Freud, and German idealism.

---

### 4961. philosophy of language /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទស្សនវិជ្ជាភាសា
1. Philosophy of language asks how words and sentences acquire meaning, how they refer to things in the world, and what it is to understand them.
2. If language shapes thought rather than merely expressing it, then philosophy of language is inseparable from philosophy of mind.
3. Frege's foundational work in logic and philosophy of language set the agenda for analytic philosophy throughout much of the twentieth century.

---

### 4962. philosophy of science /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទស្សនវិជ្ជាវិទ្យាសាស្ត្រ
1. Philosophy of science investigates the methods, assumptions, and implications of scientific inquiry, asking what makes it reliable and distinctive.
2. If scientific theories are underdetermined by evidence, then choosing among empirically equivalent theories requires values beyond mere predictive success.
3. The demarcation problem — distinguishing genuine science from pseudoscience — has been a central concern of philosophy of science since Popper.

---

### 4963. philosophy of religion /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទស្សនវិជ្ជាសាសនា
1. Philosophy of religion examines whether belief in God is rationally justified and how religious faith relates to science, morality, and human experience.
2. If the traditional arguments for God's existence all fail, it remains an open question whether religious belief can be justified on other grounds.
3. The problem of evil, the nature of religious experience, and the possibility of life after death are among the central topics in philosophy of religion.

---

### 4964. philosophy of mind /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទស្សនវិជ្ជាចិត្ត
1. Philosophy of mind examines the nature of mental states, the relationship between mind and brain, and the possibility of artificial intelligence.
2. If consciousness cannot be explained in purely physical terms, then materialism — the dominant view in philosophy of mind — faces serious difficulties.
3. The debate between functionalism and phenomenal consciousness has been one of the liveliest in philosophy of mind for several decades.

---

### 4965. analytical philosophy /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទស្សនវិជ្ជាវិភាគ
1. Analytic philosophy, characterized by precision of argument and logical rigor, has dominated English-language philosophy since the early twentieth century.
2. If analytic philosophy's emphasis on clarity is a virtue, then its critics must show that the questions it dismisses as meaningless are genuinely important.
3. The boundary between analytic and continental philosophy is often contested, and recent decades have seen increasing dialogue between the two traditions.

---

### 4966. continental philosophy /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទស្សនវិជ្ជាអឺរ៉ុប / ទស្សនវិជ្ជាបន្ត
1. Continental philosophy, associated with thinkers like Heidegger, Sartre, and Derrida, tends to engage with history, culture, and the human condition.
2. If continental philosophy is less rigorous than analytic philosophy, its defenders argue that it engages with questions the latter is too narrow to address.
3. The division between analytic and continental philosophy has produced unnecessary fragmentation in a discipline that would benefit from greater integration.

---

### 4967. Socratic method /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** វិធីសាស្ត្រស៊ូក្រាត / ការសួរដើម្បីស្វែងរកការពិត
1. The Socratic method uses systematic questioning to expose the inconsistencies in a person's beliefs and to stimulate deeper philosophical reflection.
2. If the Socratic method reveals that we know less than we think, this intellectual humility is the precondition for genuine philosophical inquiry.
3. Teachers who use the Socratic method must be willing to follow the argument wherever it leads, even if it challenges their own views.

---

### 4968. Cartesian /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទាក់ទងនឹងទស្សនៈ Descartes / Cartesian
1. The Cartesian picture of the mind as an inner theater, distinct from the external physical world, has been deeply influential and deeply contested.
2. If Cartesian dualism is incoherent, we need an alternative account of how the mental and physical relate that does not simply reduce one to the other.
3. Ryle famously described Descartes' mind-body dualism as positing a "ghost in the machine" — a picture he regarded as fundamentally misleading.

---

### 4969. Kantian /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទាក់ទងនឹងទស្សនៈ Kant / Kantian
1. The Kantian moral framework insists that persons must always be treated as ends in themselves and never merely as means to another's purposes.
2. If Kantian ethics is too rigid to accommodate the complexities of real moral situations, it may need to be supplemented by wisdom and judgment.
3. A Kantian response to the trolley problem would likely refuse to push the one person, since this would use them merely as a means to save the five.

---

### 4970. Hegelian /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទាក់ទងនឹងទស្សនៈ Hegel / Hegelian
1. The Hegelian dialectic views history not as a random sequence of events but as a rational process moving toward the realization of freedom.
2. If the Hegelian synthesis truly transcends both thesis and antithesis, it should preserve what was valid in each while overcoming their limitations.
3. Marx applied the Hegelian dialectic to economic history, transforming it from an idealist account of Spirit to a materialist account of class struggle.

---

### 4971. Marxist /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទាក់ទងនឹងទ្រឹស្ដីម៉ាក / Marxist
1. A Marxist analysis would argue that philosophical ideas are shaped by the material conditions of production rather than existing independently of them.
2. If Marxist theory correctly identifies capitalism as inherently exploitative, then reforming it from within may be insufficient to address its structural injustice.
3. Neo-Marxist thinkers have extended and revised Marx's analysis to account for forms of domination beyond class, including race and gender.

---

### 4972. Nietzschean /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទាក់ទងនឹងទស្សនៈ Nietzsche / Nietzschean
1. The Nietzschean critique of morality argues that conventional moral values are expressions of resentment and weakness rather than genuine goods.
2. If the Nietzschean "will to power" is not a biological drive but a philosophical ideal, it describes the affirmative force of creative self-overcoming.
3. A Nietzschean reading of contemporary culture would ask which values are life-affirming and which represent the victory of "slave morality."

---

### 4973. Aristotelian /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទាក់ទងនឹងទស្សនៈ Aristotle / Aristotelian
1. The Aristotelian tradition holds that ethics must be grounded in a realistic account of human nature and the conditions of genuine flourishing.
2. If an Aristotelian account of the virtues is correct, then moral education is fundamentally about forming character rather than imparting rules.
3. Contemporary virtue ethics has drawn heavily on Aristotelian resources while adapting them to the very different conditions of modern life.

---

### 4974. Platonic /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទាក់ទងនឹងទស្សនៈ Plato / Platonic
1. The Platonic tradition holds that mathematical objects and abstract Forms are more real than the particular things of ordinary experience.
2. If Platonic idealism is correct, then genuine knowledge is not of the changing world of appearance but of the eternal and unchanging Forms.
3. A Platonic account of beauty would hold that particular beautiful things are beautiful only by participation in the Form of Beauty itself.

---

### 4975. phenomenal /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ជាបាតុភូត / ទាក់ទងនឹងបាតុភូត
1. Phenomenal consciousness refers to the subjective, felt quality of experience — what it is like to see red, feel pain, or smell coffee.
2. If phenomenal consciousness cannot be explained in purely functional terms, then any purely computational account of mind will be incomplete.
3. The phenomenal world, in Kant's sense, is the world as structured by our cognitive faculties, as opposed to things as they are in themselves.

---

### 4976. noumenal /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** លើសពីការយល់/'noumenal'
1. The noumenal world, for Kant, is the realm of things as they are in themselves, inaccessible to human knowledge but presupposed by moral freedom.
2. If the noumenal self is the source of our freedom and moral agency, then human dignity is grounded in something that transcends the causal order.
3. Kant's distinction between the phenomenal and noumenal was designed to leave room for freedom and morality in a world governed by causal laws.

---

### 4977. transcendental /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ហួសពីបទពិសោធន៍ / Transcendental
1. Kant's transcendental philosophy investigates the conditions that must hold for experience to be possible, rather than the contents of experience itself.
2. If the transcendental conditions of experience are a priori — prior to experience — then they cannot be derived from or refuted by empirical inquiry.
3. The transcendental ego, for Husserl, is the pure subject of consciousness that underlies and unifies all experience without being an object within it.

---

### 4978. immanent /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ជាខាងក្នុង / ណែនាំ
1. An immanent critique evaluates a position by its own standards, showing that it fails to achieve the goals it sets for itself.
2. If God is understood as immanent rather than transcendent, the divine is present within the world rather than standing apart from and above it.
3. Deleuze developed the concept of immanence into a positive philosophy that rejects all forms of transcendence as mystifications of the real.

---

### 4979. dialectical /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទាក់ទងនឹងការជជែក / dialectical
1. A dialectical argument moves through contradiction and resolution, advancing understanding by incorporating and overcoming opposing positions.
2. If history is dialectical, as Hegel and Marx believed, then conflict and contradiction are not failures to be avoided but engines of progress.
3. The dialectical method, whether in Socrates' questioning or Hegel's system, treats opposition not as defeat but as a productive moment in inquiry.

---

### 4980. categorical /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ដាច់ខាត / ជាក្រុម
1. Kant's categorical imperative commands unconditionally — not "if you want X, then do Y," but simply "do Y," regardless of desire or consequence.
2. If moral obligations are categorical rather than hypothetical, they apply to all rational beings simply in virtue of their capacity for reason.
3. The distinction between categorical and hypothetical imperatives marks the boundary between genuine morality and mere prudential calculation for Kant.

---

### 4981. hypothetical imperative /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** បញ្ជាឧប្បត្តិ / ការបង្គាប់ตามលក្ខខណ្ឌ
1. A hypothetical imperative commands action only conditionally — if you want to achieve a certain goal, then you must take the necessary means.
2. If all our obligations were hypothetical imperatives, morality would be merely a system of prudential advice rather than a genuine set of binding duties.
3. Kant argued that the special authority of moral obligations comes from their being categorical, not hypothetical, and thus independent of desire.

---

### 4982. a priori /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** មុនបទពិសោធន៍ / a priori
1. A priori knowledge is knowledge that can be justified independently of sensory experience, such as the truths of mathematics and logic.
2. If moral principles are a priori, they cannot be refuted by empirical discoveries about how human beings actually think or behave.
3. Kant argued that both pure mathematics and the fundamental principles of natural science rest on synthetic a priori knowledge, which combines novelty with necessity.

---

### 4983. a posteriori /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ក្រោយបទពិសោធន៍ / a posteriori
1. A posteriori knowledge depends on sensory experience and can be revised in light of new empirical evidence, unlike a priori truths.
2. If all knowledge is a posteriori, as radical empiricists claim, then the necessity of mathematical truths must be explained in psychological or social terms.
3. The distinction between a priori and a posteriori knowledge is one of the most fundamental in epistemology and has been debated since Kant.

---

### 4984. analytic /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** វិភាគ / ទាក់ទងនឹងការវិភាគ
1. An analytic statement is one whose truth depends solely on the meanings of its terms, such as "all bachelors are unmarried men."
2. If all a priori truths are analytic, as Hume suggested, then Kant's claim that there is synthetic a priori knowledge must be rejected.
3. The distinction between analytic and synthetic statements was challenged by Quine, who argued it rests on a distinction that cannot be clearly drawn.

---

### 4985. synthetic /adjective/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ផ្សំ / ទំនាក់ទំនង
1. A synthetic statement adds information that goes beyond what is contained in the meanings of the terms involved, as in "the sky is blue."
2. If synthetic truths can be known a priori, as Kant believed, then there are necessary truths about the world that experience cannot refute.
3. Quine's attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction aimed to dissolve one of the foundational assumptions of twentieth-century philosophy of language.

---

### 4986. reduction /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការបន្ថយ / ការ減ចុះ
1. Philosophical reduction involves explaining one level of description in terms of another, typically lower-level, one — for example, the mental in terms of the physical.
2. If consciousness cannot be reduced to neural processes without remainder, then physicalism faces the hard problem that Chalmers has so forcefully articulated.
3. The question of whether biology can be reduced to physics and chemistry, or whether it requires autonomous concepts, is a central issue in philosophy of science.

---

### 4987. emergence /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការលេចឡើង / ភាពថ្មី
1. Emergence refers to properties that appear at higher levels of organization and that cannot be straightforwardly predicted from lower-level descriptions alone.
2. If consciousness is an emergent property of complex neural organization, it may be genuinely novel without being inexplicable in principle.
3. Strong emergence — where higher-level properties are irreducible in principle — would pose a fundamental challenge to physicalist accounts of mind.

---

### 4988. supervenience /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការអនុម័ត / ភាពពឹងផ្អែក
1. Mental properties are said to supervene on physical properties if any two possible beings that are physically identical must be mentally identical too.
2. If moral properties supervene on natural properties, then any two situations that are identical in all natural respects must be identical in moral respects.
3. Supervenience is widely accepted as a minimal constraint on physicalism, though it falls short of full reductive identity between the mental and physical.

---

### 4989. intentionality /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការចង់ / ភាពជំរុញ
1. Intentionality, in philosophy of mind, refers to the property of mental states whereby they are about or directed toward something beyond themselves.
2. If intentionality is the mark of the mental, then any account of mind must explain how purely physical processes can be about anything at all.
3. Husserl placed intentionality at the center of his phenomenology, arguing that consciousness is always consciousness of something.

---

### 4990. qualia /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** គុណភាពអារម្មណ៍ / ហ្ស្វ
1. Qualia are the subjective, felt qualities of experience — the redness of red, the painfulness of pain — that seem to resist purely functional description.
2. If qualia cannot be captured in any functional or physical account, then materialism faces an explanatory gap it may not be able to close.
3. Frank Jackson's "Mary's Room" thought experiment argues that knowing all physical facts about color vision does not tell one what it is like to see red.

---

### 4991. functionalism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីមុខងារ / functionalism
1. Functionalism holds that mental states are defined by their functional roles — their causal relations to inputs, outputs, and other mental states.
2. If functionalism is correct, then any system that plays the right causal roles would count as having mental states, whether biological or electronic.
3. The main challenge to functionalism is whether it can account for the phenomenal, felt quality of experience rather than just its functional profile.

---

### 4992. materialism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីសម្ភារ / ការប្រកាន់ខ្ជាប់ថាអ្វីៗគ្រប់យ៉ាងគឺជាសម្ភារ
1. Materialism holds that everything that exists is physical, and that mental phenomena are ultimately identical with or wholly dependent on physical processes.
2. If materialism is true, the challenge is to explain how purely physical processes give rise to the richness of subjective conscious experience.
3. Eliminative materialism, the most radical form, holds that folk psychological categories like belief and desire do not correspond to real neural kinds.

---

### 4993. dualism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីពីរ / ភាពពីរ
1. Substance dualism, associated with Descartes, holds that mind and body are distinct substances that are causally related but fundamentally different in nature.
2. If dualism is true, the hard problem of consciousness may be more tractable, but the problem of how mind and body interact becomes severe.
3. Property dualism holds that while there is only one substance — the physical — it has both physical and irreducibly mental properties.

---

### 4994. monism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីស្រទ្ធ / ភាពស្រទ្ធ
1. Monism holds that reality is ultimately one — whether that one substance is physical, mental, or neither, as different versions of the view propose.
2. If Spinoza's neutral monism is correct, then mind and body are two attributes of a single infinite substance, which he identified with God or Nature.
3. The appeal of monism lies in its metaphysical simplicity, though the challenge is to explain the apparent diversity and independence of phenomena within a single substance.

---

### 4995. panpsychism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីចិត្តគ្រប់ / ការជឿថាគ្រប់ចំពោះមានចិត្ត
1. Panpsychism holds that consciousness or proto-conscious properties are fundamental features of the universe, present even in simple physical entities.
2. If panpsychism can solve the hard problem of consciousness by making experience a basic feature of reality, it must also explain how simple experiences combine.
3. The "combination problem" for panpsychism asks how the micro-experiences of simple physical entities combine to form the unified consciousness of a human being.

---

### 4996. solipsism /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ទ្រឹស្ដីខ្លួនឯង / ការជឿថាខ្លួនឯងប៉ុណ្ណោះ
1. Solipsism, the view that only one's own mind is known to exist, is logically difficult to refute but almost universally rejected as a livable position.
2. If solipsism cannot be disproven, it illustrates the limits of Cartesian methodological doubt as a starting point for philosophical inquiry.
3. Wittgenstein argued that solipsism is not even a coherent position, since the concept of a "private language" on which it depends is incoherent.

---

### 4997. intersubjectivity /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការបំផ្លែងទ្វេ / ការចែករំលែករវាងប្រធានបទ
1. Intersubjectivity, the shared dimension of experience between subjects, is what makes genuine communication, science, and culture possible.
2. If knowledge is intersubjective rather than merely private, then philosophical inquiry is essentially a collaborative and social activity.
3. Husserl struggled to account for intersubjectivity within his framework, since starting from pure individual consciousness makes other minds difficult to reach.

---

### 4998. heresy /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ការបំបែកសាសនា / ការផ្ទុយនឹងគំនិតសំខាន់
1. In intellectual history, what is called heresy in one era is often recognized in a later era as genuine philosophical or scientific insight.
2. If orthodoxy stifles inquiry, then the courage to defend positions that challenge received wisdom is essential to intellectual progress.
3. The history of philosophy can partly be written as a history of heresies — bold ideas that violated the consensus of their time before becoming mainstream.

---

### 4999. dogma /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ដំណឹងផ្ដាច់ / ការចុះទទួល
1. A dogma is a belief held with absolute certainty and immune to revision, regardless of what evidence or argument might be brought against it.
2. If philosophy begins with questioning dogma, as Socrates believed, then the greatest obstacle to philosophical progress is the refusal to examine one's assumptions.
3. Quine famously attacked the "two dogmas of empiricism" — the analytic-synthetic distinction and reductionism — arguing that both must be abandoned.

---

### 5000. wisdom /noun/
**ន័យខ្មែរ:** ប្រាជ្ញា / ចំណេះដឹងជ្រៅជ្រះ
1. True wisdom, as Plato understood it, is not merely the possession of knowledge but the ability to use knowledge well in the service of justice and the good.
2. If wisdom cannot be transmitted by reading books alone, then it must be cultivated through reflection, experience, and genuine engagement with the world.
3. The Socratic tradition suggests that wisdom begins with the recognition of one's own limitations — a form of intellectual humility that opens the door to genuine inquiry.
